# Pittosporum 'James Stirling' Propagation

## Quick Facts

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Intermediate |
| **Best Method** | Semi-hardwood cuttings |
| **Alternative Methods** | Softwood cuttings, layering |
| **Time to Root** | 6-10 weeks |
| **Best Season** | Late summer to autumn |
| **Success Rate** | 70-85% typical |
| **Rooting Hormone** | Recommended |

## Overview

*Pittosporum tenuifolium* 'James Stirling' is a named cultivar of kohuhu, grown for very fine silvery grey-green leaves on near-black stems and a dense, fast, naturally upright habit. Nurseries list it at roughly 2-4m tall and 1-2m wide, and it is sold mainly for hedging and screening, in pots, and in coastal gardens.

Being a cultivar is the whole reason to propagate it from cuttings. Seed from a 'James Stirling' will germinate perfectly well and give you *Pittosporum tenuifolium* — but not this plant. The fine foliage, the dark stems and the tight columnar habit are the cultivar, and they only come across in a cutting. If you want a hedge that matches the plant you already have, every plant in it has to be a cutting from that plant.

That also makes it worth propagating in one go. A hedge grown from a single mother plant is uniform; a hedge grown from cuttings taken over three seasons from three different plants often is not.

## Best Uses for Propagated Plants

- Filling out or extending an existing hedge with plants that actually match
- Replacing gaps where a hedge plant has failed
- Screening, where a run of identical plants is the point
- Pots and formal positions where habit matters
- Coastal gardens, where the species does well

## Propagation Methods

### Semi-Hardwood Cuttings (Recommended)

**Overview:** The standard method for this cultivar and the only one that reproduces it faithfully at any scale.

**When to take material:**
- Best months: February to April (late summer to autumn)
- Wood condition: Current season's growth, firmed but not yet hard

**Step-by-step process:**

1. **Select material**
   - Current season's growth that snaps rather than bends
   - 8-12cm lengths
   - Side shoots root more readily than strong leaders
   - Avoid flowering wood

2. **Prepare cuttings**
   - Cut cleanly just below a node
   - Strip the lower third of leaves
   - Leave 4-6 leaves at the tip; halve any large ones

3. **Apply rooting hormone**
   - Medium-strength formula
   - Genuinely worth using here — Pittosporum is slower than the easy natives

4. **Insert cuttings**
   - Free-draining propagation mix, coarser than you would use for soft cuttings
   - Insert 3-4cm deep
   - Firm gently, water in once

5. **Provide environment**
   - Warm, bright, out of direct sun
   - Moderate to high humidity under cover
   - Bottom heat helps but is not essential

6. **Ongoing care** (6-10 weeks)
   - Keep just moist; this one rots more readily than it dries out
   - Ventilate regularly to keep botrytis off the stripped stems
   - Test with a gentle tug from about four weeks

7. **Pot up when rooted**
   - Standard potting mix
   - Pinch the tip once established to start it branching low

### Softwood Cuttings (Spring)

**When to use:** Earlier in the season, or when the plant is putting out a lot of soft growth.

**When to take material:**
- Best months: October to December (spring into early summer)

**Process:**
1. Take 8-10cm tips of soft new growth, early in the morning
2. Strip lower leaves, apply hormone
3. Insert in propagation mix under high humidity
4. Roots in 4-8 weeks
5. Noticeably more prone to wilting than semi-hardwood — keep them covered

### Layering (A Few Plants Only)

**When to use:** When you want two or three replacements and are not in a hurry.

**Process:**
1. Choose a low branch that will reach the ground
2. Wound the underside lightly, dust with hormone
3. Peg into soil, cover, leave for 6-12 months
4. Sever from the parent once well rooted

## Timing & Seasonality

### Southern Hemisphere Calendar

| Season | Suitability | Notes |
|--------|-------------|-------|
| **Spring** (Sep-Nov) | Good | Softwood cuttings once growth firms slightly |
| **Summer** (Dec-Feb) | Possible | Late summer is the start of the best window |
| **Autumn** (Mar-May) | Best | Semi-hardwood, prime time |
| **Winter** (Jun-Aug) | Avoid | Too cold to root before rot sets in |

### Optimal Timing

- **Semi-hardwood cuttings:** February to April
- **Softwood cuttings:** October to December
- **Key factor:** Wood that is firm enough to hold shape but still flexible

## Rooting Environment

| Factor | Requirement |
|--------|-------------|
| **Temperature** | Warm (18-24°C) |
| **Humidity** | Moderate to high |
| **Light** | Bright, indirect |
| **Medium** | Free-draining, low fertility |
| **Bottom heat** | Helpful, not essential |

### Environment Setup

A covered tray or a simple propagator in a bright spot out of direct sun is enough. Pittosporum cuttings are more likely to be lost to rot than to drying out, so err toward less water and more air movement than you would for a soft-stemmed cutting.

## Common Problems & Solutions

### Problem 1: Blackening at the Base

**Symptoms:** Stem goes black or mushy just below the medium line.

**Causes:** Too wet, too airless, or a torn rather than cut base.

**Solution:** Discard affected cuttings — this spreads. Increase ventilation, water less.

**Prevention:** Sharp clean cuts, coarser mix, ventilate daily.

### Problem 2: Nothing After Ten Weeks

**Symptoms:** Cuttings still green and turgid, no resistance to a tug.

**Causes:** Material too hard, too cold, or hormone skipped.

**Solution:** Leave them — Pittosporum is genuinely slow and often roots in the following spring.

**Prevention:** Take material at the semi-hardwood stage, not fully mature wood; use hormone; provide warmth.

### Problem 3: Rooted Plants Grow Straight Up and Thin

**Symptoms:** A single leader, bare at the base.

**Causes:** The cultivar's natural habit, unchecked.

**Solution:** Pinch the tip. Repeat through the first year.

**Prevention:** Pinch as soon as the cutting is established, and again each time it puts on 10cm.

## Pro Tips

1. **Take more than you need** — Success is good but not westringia-good. Twenty cuttings for a ten-plant hedge is sensible.

2. **One mother plant per hedge** — Cuttings from a single plant give a hedge that grows evenly. Mixing sources shows up three years later as an uneven top line.

3. **Do not feed the cuttings** — Low-fertility mix. Nutrients encourage the top to grow before the base has roots to support it.

4. **Seed is not a shortcut** — It will grow, and it will not be 'James Stirling'. See the overview.

5. **Trimmings are free material** — The best cutting material is what comes off the hedge when you shape it in late summer. Time the trim to the cutting window and take both at once.

## Mother Plant Care

### Maintaining Stock Plants

- **Position:** Full sun to part shade, well-drained soil.
- **Pruning:** Trim in late summer; the trimmings are the cutting material.
- **Feeding:** Balanced fertiliser in spring. Not phosphorus-sensitive.
- **Watering:** Regular while establishing, drought-tolerant after.

### Selecting Material

Choose material that displays:

- Current season's growth, firmed but flexible
- Fine, typical, well-coloured foliage
- Dense internodes rather than long drawn-out growth
- No leaf spot or scale
