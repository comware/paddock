# Root Cuttings

## Overview

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Advanced |
| **Success Rate** | 50-80% typical |
| **Time to Root** | 6-16 weeks typical |
| **Best Season** | Winter (dormant season) |
| **Equipment Needed** | Spade, sharp knife, containers, rooting medium |

## What Are Root Cuttings?

Root cuttings use sections of a plant's roots to produce new plants. Unlike stem cuttings where you cut above ground, root cuttings require digging up or accessing part of the root system and cutting sections that will regenerate into complete plants.

This method works because certain plants have the ability to produce adventitious shoots (new stems) directly from root tissue. When a root section is isolated and given proper conditions, it will first produce shoots and then develop its own root system to become independent.

Root cuttings are particularly valuable for plants that are difficult to propagate by other means, or for species that naturally spread through root suckers. It's a winter technique that works during the dormant season when plants are at rest.

## When to Use This Method

**Best for:**
- Raspberries and blackberries (brambles)
- Japanese anemones
- Oriental poppies
- Drumstick primulas (Primula denticulata)
- Sea holly (Eryngium)
- Mullein (Verbascum)
- Some trees (paulownia, robinia, ailanthus)

**Not recommended for:**
- Plants without thick, fleshy roots
- Fibrous-rooted plants
- Grafted plants (roots are rootstock, not desired variety)
- Plants that don't naturally sucker

## Step-by-Step Process

### Step 1: Timing

Take root cuttings during **deep winter dormancy** (June to August in Australia).

**Best timing:**
- Mid-winter when plants fully dormant
- Deciduous plants: after all leaves fallen
- Perennials: when top growth died back

**Why winter:**
- Roots have maximum stored energy
- Plant is at rest, less stress
- Cooler conditions reduce rot risk
- Shoots emerge in spring warmth

**Key points:**
- Later winter (July-August) often works well
- Avoid if ground is frozen solid
- Plant should be established (2+ years)

**Common mistakes:**
- Trying during active growth (depletes plant)
- Ground too wet to dig

### Step 2: Selecting and Harvesting Roots

Access roots by lifting part or all of the plant.

**For perennials:**
1. Lift entire plant if small enough
2. Shake or wash soil from roots
3. Select healthy, pencil-thick roots
4. Replant parent immediately after

**For larger plants/trees:**
1. Carefully dig to expose roots on one side
2. Select 2-3 suitable roots
3. Cut without disturbing whole plant
4. Backfill and firm soil

**What to look for:**
- Healthy, firm roots (not soft or diseased)
- Pencil thickness (5-10mm) for most perennials
- Thumb thickness (10-15mm) for trees
- Young-ish roots (current/last season's)

**Key points:**
- Don't take more than 1/3 of root system
- Thicker roots for vertical planting
- Thinner roots for horizontal planting

**Common mistakes:**
- Taking too many roots (weakens parent)
- Using old, woody roots

### Step 3: Preparing Root Cuttings

Cut roots into sections with clear orientation marks.

**Standard technique:**
1. Cut roots into 5-10cm sections
2. Make a straight cut at the top (was closest to plant crown)
3. Make an angled cut at the bottom (was furthest from crown)
4. This prevents planting upside down

**Why orientation matters:**
- Shoots grow from the end that was toward the crown
- Roots grow from the end that was toward the tip
- Upside down = fails

**Root thickness determines method:**
- Thick roots (>8mm): Plant vertically
- Thin roots (<8mm): Lay horizontally

**Key points:**
- Work quickly to prevent drying
- Keep cuttings damp between cutting and planting
- Consistent cutting length aids handling

**Common mistakes:**
- Forgetting which end is which (critical!)
- Letting roots dry out

### Step 4: Preparing Medium and Containers

Set up containers with free-draining mix.

**Medium recipe:**
- 50% coarse sand or grit
- 50% potting mix or loam
- Or pure coarse sand

**Container options:**
- Deep pots (15cm+) for vertical method
- Seed trays for horizontal method
- Individual cells for small quantities

**Preparation:**
1. Fill containers with moist medium
2. Ensure drainage holes clear
3. Allow medium to drain before planting

**Key points:**
- Drainage is critical (roots rot easily)
- Don't pack medium too tightly
- Pre-moisten but not soggy

**Common mistakes:**
- Medium too fine (holds water)
- Waterlogging

### Step 5: Planting - Vertical Method (Thick Roots)

For roots thicker than 8mm.

**Process:**
1. Make hole in medium with dibber
2. Insert cutting with angled end down
3. Top (straight cut) should be flush with or just below surface
4. Space 5-7cm apart if multiple
5. Top-dress with grit

**Depth:**
- Top of cutting just below surface (1-2cm)
- Don't leave protruding above surface

**Key points:**
- Straight cut up, angled cut down
- Surface level prevents drying
- Grit keeps crown clean

**Common mistakes:**
- Planting upside down
- Top exposed to air (dries out)

### Step 6: Planting - Horizontal Method (Thin Roots)

For roots thinner than 8mm.

**Process:**
1. Lay root sections flat on medium surface
2. Space 3-5cm apart
3. Cover with 1-2cm of grit or medium
4. Press gently to ensure contact

**When to use:**
- Drumstick primulas
- Japanese anemones
- Sea hollies
- Thinner roots of most species

**Key points:**
- Orientation less critical when horizontal
- Even coverage important
- Shallow burial only

**Common mistakes:**
- Burying too deep
- Overcrowding

### Step 7: Environmental Conditions

Provide cool, protected conditions.

**Placement:**
- Cold frame (ideal)
- Unheated greenhouse
- Sheltered outdoor position
- Frost protection if extreme

**Conditions:**
- Cool: 5-10°C fine
- Good light (not essential until shoots appear)
- Keep moist but not wet
- Protect from hard frost and waterlogging

**Key points:**
- Don't bring inside to warmth
- Natural cold period is beneficial
- Monitor for drying or waterlogging

**Common mistakes:**
- Too warm too soon (weak shoots)
- Letting medium dry out completely

### Step 8: Winter Care

Minimal intervention needed during dormancy.

**Winter tasks:**
- Check moisture weekly
- Ensure drainage working
- Remove any rotted material
- Protect from rodents

**What to expect:**
- Nothing visible for weeks/months
- Callus forming at cut surfaces
- Shoots developing underground

**Key points:**
- Patience - nothing happens visibly for a while
- Don't disturb or "check" by digging
- Keep medium barely moist

**Common mistakes:**
- Digging to see what's happening
- Overwatering
- Giving up

### Step 9: Spring Development

Shoots emerge as temperatures warm.

**What to expect:**
- Shoots appear from top of vertical cuttings
- Shoots emerge along horizontal cuttings
- Late winter to mid-spring emergence typical

**Care during emergence:**
- Keep moist (shoots are delicate)
- Protect from late frost
- Begin light feeding once leaves develop
- Ensure adequate light

**Key points:**
- Shoots appear before substantial root development
- Be gentle - new shoots are fragile
- Don't move to full sun immediately

**Common mistakes:**
- Exposing young shoots to hard frost
- Overfeeding

### Step 10: Growing On and Potting Up

Establish young plants before transplanting.

**When ready to pot up:**
- Several leaves developed
- Visible new roots from cutting
- Typically late spring to early summer

**Potting up:**
1. Lift carefully
2. Pot individually
3. Use standard potting mix
4. Keep shaded for a week
5. Water well

**Growing on:**
- Keep in pots through first summer
- Regular watering and feeding
- Plant out autumn or following spring

**Key points:**
- Don't rush - let plants establish
- Handle roots gently
- Some plants ready same year, others need longer

**Common mistakes:**
- Planting out too small
- Neglecting water in summer

## Common Problems & Solutions

### Problem 1: Cuttings Rot Before Shooting

**Symptoms:** Soft, brown roots; no shoots appear; smell of decay.

**Causes:**
- Medium too wet
- Poor drainage
- Damaged or diseased root material
- Planted during wet period

**Solutions:**
- Can't save rotted cuttings
- Improve drainage for remaining
- Take fresh cuttings if season allows

**Prevention:**
- Excellent drainage essential
- Don't overwater
- Use healthy root material
- Avoid waterlogged conditions

### Problem 2: No Shoots After Months

**Symptoms:** Cuttings still firm but no growth, even in spring.

**Causes:**
- Planted upside down
- Root too thin/lacking energy
- Too cold conditions
- Wrong species (can't regenerate)

**Solutions:**
- Check cutting is still alive (firm, not mushy)
- Add gentle warmth if very cold
- Be patient - some are slow
- May need to try again

**Prevention:**
- Clear orientation marking
- Use adequately thick roots
- Protect from extreme cold
- Choose known root-cutting species

### Problem 3: Weak, Spindly Shoots

**Symptoms:** Shoots emerge but are pale, thin, and weak.

**Causes:**
- Insufficient light
- Too warm too early
- Overwatering
- Cutting material low in reserves

**Solutions:**
- Move to better light
- Reduce water slightly
- Gentle feeding once leaves open
- May strengthen as roots develop

**Prevention:**
- Adequate light once shoots appear
- Cool conditions until spring
- Healthy, thick root material

## Pro Tips

1. **Take cuttings from young roots** - Current season's or one-year-old roots have more regenerative capacity than old, woody roots. They're usually paler and more flexible.

2. **Thicker is better** - Pencil thickness or thicker contains more stored energy for shoot and root production. Very thin roots may not have enough reserves.

3. **The angled cut is your friend** - Making an angled cut at the bottom and straight at top makes it impossible to plant upside down. This simple step prevents the most common failure.

4. **Some plants regenerate from any fragment** - Species like Japanese anemone and comfrey are so vigorous that even small root fragments left in soil will grow. Be careful with weedy species.

5. **Root cuttings solve pest problems** - For plants affected by root pests like eelworms, take root cuttings from the upper, unaffected parts of the root system. The pests are left behind.
