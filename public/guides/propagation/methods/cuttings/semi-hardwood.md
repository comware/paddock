# Semi-Hardwood Cuttings

## Overview

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Intermediate |
| **Success Rate** | 60-85% typical |
| **Time to Root** | 4-10 weeks typical |
| **Best Season** | Late summer to autumn |
| **Equipment Needed** | Secateurs, rooting medium, humidity dome, heat mat, rooting hormone |

## What Are Semi-Hardwood Cuttings?

Semi-hardwood cuttings (also called semi-ripe cuttings) are taken from the current season's growth after it has begun to mature and firm up, but before it becomes fully woody. This stage typically occurs in late summer to autumn when spring growth has hardened off but isn't yet dormant.

The stems should feel firm and slightly woody at the base while remaining flexible enough to bend without snapping cleanly. Leaves are fully developed and mature. This intermediate stage offers a balance between the fast-rooting potential of softwood and the resilience of hardwood.

Semi-hardwood cuttings are particularly valuable for evergreen shrubs, Australian natives, and many plants that don't root reliably from softer or harder wood. They're more forgiving than softwood cuttings because they're less prone to wilting, yet root faster than hardwood cuttings.

## When to Use This Method

**Best for:**
- Evergreen shrubs (camellias, gardenias, azaleas)
- Australian natives (grevilleas, westringias, leptospermums)
- Citrus and olive trees
- Conifers and hedging plants
- Many Mediterranean herbs when mature

**Not recommended for:**
- Herbaceous plants (use softwood instead)
- Deciduous plants in dormancy (use hardwood)
- Plants that root easily from softwood
- Very slow-rooting species that need hardwood

## Step-by-Step Process

### Step 1: Timing Your Cuttings

Take semi-hardwood cuttings in **late summer to mid-autumn** (February to April in Australia).

**Signs material is ready:**
- Current season's growth has firmed
- Stems bend but feel woody at base
- Leaves are fully mature
- Growth has slowed from spring flush

**Best time of day:** Early morning when stems are turgid.

**Key points:**
- Too early = still softwood (wilts easily)
- Too late = becoming hardwood (roots slowly)
- Most shrubs ready by late February/March

**Common mistakes:**
- Confusing old woody growth with semi-hardwood
- Taking material too late in autumn

### Step 2: Selecting and Cutting Material

Choose healthy current season's shoots that have matured.

**Selection criteria:**
- Non-flowering shoots preferred
- Healthy, typical growth
- Free from pests and disease
- Current season's growth, not old wood

**Taking the cutting:**
1. Select shoots 10-15cm long
2. Cut just below a node with sharp secateurs
3. Take cuttings with a "heel" if possible (small strip of older wood attached)
4. Place in damp bag immediately

**Key points:**
- Heel cuttings often root better (more cambium exposed)
- Morning cuttings contain more moisture
- Take more than you need

**Common mistakes:**
- Using old growth instead of current season
- Letting cuttings dry out

### Step 3: Preparing Cuttings

Process cuttings in a cool, shaded area.

**Preparation steps:**
1. Trim to 10-15cm if needed
2. Remove lower leaves (keep top 3-4 leaves)
3. Reduce large leaves by half
4. Trim heel if ragged (but keep it)
5. Wound the base (see below)

**Wounding technique:**
- Use sharp knife to remove thin strip of bark (2-3cm long) on one side of stem base
- Exposes more cambium for root initiation
- Especially helpful for difficult species

**Key points:**
- Wounding significantly improves rooting on woody material
- Keep wounds shallow (don't damage core)
- Some gardeners wound both sides

**Common mistakes:**
- Forgetting to wound (reduces success)
- Wounding too deeply

### Step 4: Applying Rooting Hormone

Semi-hardwood requires medium to strong hormone concentration.

**Application:**
1. Use medium strength hormone (IBA 3-8g/kg)
2. Dip base and wounded area 3-4cm deep
3. Ensure hormone contacts wound
4. Tap off excess

**Hormone selection:**
- Purple/No.1: Too weak for most semi-hardwood
- Red/No.2: Ideal for most species
- Yellow/No.3: For difficult species

**Key points:**
- Hormone more important for semi-hardwood than softwood
- Gel provides better contact on woody stems
- Fresh hormone works better

**Common mistakes:**
- Using wrong strength
- Missing the wounded area

### Step 5: Preparing Medium and Containers

Semi-hardwood needs excellent drainage.

**Recommended mixes:**

| Plant Type | Mix Ratio | Notes |
|------------|-----------|-------|
| Australian natives | 70% coarse sand, 30% perlite | Excellent drainage essential |
| General shrubs | 50% perlite, 50% coco coir | Good all-round mix |
| Conifers | 50% perlite, 50% fine bark | Slightly acidic |
| Camellias/azaleas | 50% perlite, 50% peat | Acidic, well-draining |

**Container options:**
- Deep tubes (50mm forestry tubes) for natives
- 10cm pots for larger cuttings
- Cell trays for smaller batches

**Key points:**
- Drainage is critical for semi-hardwood
- Deep containers encourage deep rooting
- Pre-moisten medium before planting

**Common mistakes:**
- Using standard potting mix (holds too much water)
- Shallow containers limiting root development

### Step 6: Planting Cuttings

Insert prepared cuttings into medium.

**Process:**
1. Make hole with dibber (don't push cutting in)
2. Insert cutting 4-5cm deep
3. Firm medium around stem gently
4. Water in lightly to settle

**Spacing:**
- Leaves shouldn't touch each other
- Allow airflow between cuttings
- One cutting per tube/cell

**Key points:**
- Pre-made holes prevent hormone loss
- Deeper planting than softwood
- Firm but not compacted

**Common mistakes:**
- Planting too shallow
- Overcrowding

### Step 7: Environmental Setup

Semi-hardwood needs less humidity than softwood but still requires protection.

**Humidity:**
- 70-85% (lower than softwood's 85-95%)
- Dome with vents partly open
- Reduce gradually as rooting progresses

**Temperature:**
- Air: 15-20°C (cooler than softwood)
- Bottom heat: 18-22°C
- Autumn cuttings benefit greatly from heat mat

**Light:**
- Bright indirect light
- Some morning sun acceptable
- Avoid hot afternoon sun

**Key points:**
- Semi-hardwood tolerates lower humidity than softwood
- Bottom heat is very important for autumn cuttings
- Too much humidity can cause fungal problems

**Common mistakes:**
- Excessive humidity causing rot
- No bottom heat in cool conditions

### Step 8: Ongoing Care

Semi-hardwood cuttings need less intensive care but longer attention.

**Routine care:**
- Check moisture weekly (not daily)
- Vent dome regularly
- Remove any dead or diseased material
- Maintain bottom heat through winter

**Watering:**
- Less frequent than softwood
- Medium should dry slightly between watering
- Bottom water when needed

**Timeline:**
- 4-6 weeks: Callus formation
- 6-10 weeks: Roots developing
- 10-16 weeks: Ready for potting up

**Key points:**
- Patience required - takes longer than softwood
- Reduce humidity gradually over time
- Maintain heat through rooting period

**Common mistakes:**
- Overwatering
- Removing heat too early
- Giving up before roots develop

### Step 9: Checking for Roots

Semi-hardwood takes longer to root - don't rush.

**When to check:**
- After 6-8 weeks minimum
- When new growth appears
- When cutting feels stable

**How to check:**
- Look for roots through drainage holes
- Very gentle tug test
- New growth is a good sign

**Key points:**
- Some species take 3-4 months
- Australian natives can be slow
- Callus doesn't always mean roots follow

**Common mistakes:**
- Checking too early
- Pulling too hard

### Step 10: Potting Up and Hardening

Transition rooted cuttings to normal conditions.

**Timing:**
- When roots fill container
- Strong new growth visible
- Usually late winter/spring for autumn cuttings

**Potting up:**
1. Pot into free-draining potting mix
2. Use appropriate pot size (not too large)
3. Water with seaweed solution
4. Harden off gradually

**Hardening off:**
- Reduce humidity over 1-2 weeks
- Increase light gradually
- Introduce outdoor conditions slowly

**Key points:**
- Autumn cuttings often potted up in spring
- Don't overpot - roots rot in too much soil
- Some species benefit from staying in tubes over winter

**Common mistakes:**
- Potting too early
- Putting straight into full sun

## Environmental Requirements

| Factor | Optimal Range | Acceptable Range |
|--------|---------------|------------------|
| **Temperature** | 18-22°C (medium) | 15-25°C |
| **Humidity** | 70-85% | 60-90% |
| **Light** | Bright indirect | Moderate to bright |
| **Air circulation** | Moderate with regular venting | Avoid stagnant air |

### Creating the Right Environment

**Option 1: Heated propagator with thermostat**
Best option for semi-hardwood. Set to 20°C, vent frequently, position in good light.

**Option 2: Cold frame with heat mat**
Heat mat in cold frame provides bottom heat while frame protects from weather. Good for larger quantities.

**Option 3: Shadehouse with misting**
For Mediterranean climates and hardier species. Intermittent misting, good drainage essential.

| Root initiation | 4-8 weeks | First roots from callus |

### Factors That Affect Speed

**Faster rooting:**
- Bottom heat maintained at 20-22°C
- Correct hormone strength
- Proper wounding technique
- Easy-rooting species
- Good quality cutting material

**Slower rooting:**
- Cool conditions (no bottom heat)
- Autumn/winter ambient temperatures
- Difficult species
- Poor quality parent material
- Late-season cuttings

## Common Problems & Solutions

### Problem 1: Cuttings Sitting Without Rooting

**Symptoms:** Months pass, cuttings look alive but no roots, no new growth.

**Causes:**
- Too cold (no bottom heat)
- Wrong cutting type (old wood, not current season)
- Poor hormone application
- Species is simply slow

**Solutions:**
- Add or increase bottom heat
- Be patient - some take 4-6 months
- Try rooting hormone if not used
- Accept some species need hardwood technique

**Prevention:**
- Always use bottom heat for autumn cuttings
- Ensure correct cutting type
- Apply hormone thoroughly

### Problem 2: Fungal Problems

**Symptoms:** Grey mould on leaves, black spots, rotting despite not overwatering.

**Causes:**
- Too much humidity
- Poor ventilation
- Contaminated medium or tools
- Dead material not removed

**Solutions:**
- Increase ventilation dramatically
- Remove affected material
- Apply fungicide spray
- May need to restart batch

**Prevention:**
- Vent daily
- Don't overcrowd
- Use sterile medium
- Remove dead material promptly

### Problem 3: Leaf Drop

**Symptoms:** Lower leaves yellowing and falling, may progress upward.

**Causes:**
- Natural shedding (some species do this)
- Overwatering
- Too dark location
- Stress response

**Solutions:**
- If cutting is otherwise healthy, may be normal
- Reduce watering
- Improve light
- Monitor for other problems

**Prevention:**
- Correct watering regime
- Adequate light
- Healthy cutting material

## Pro Tips

1. **Heel cuttings improve success dramatically** - When removing cutting, pull downward to take a strip of older bark (heel). This exposes more cambium and often improves rooting by 20-30%.

2. **Wound difficult species on both sides** - For challenging plants, wound both sides of the stem base. More exposed cambium means more potential root initiation points.

3. **Use forestry tubes for Australian natives** - Deep tubes (50mm diameter) encourage downward root growth, which natives need. Better than shallow trays.

4. **Don't abandon over winter** - Autumn cuttings often look unchanged for months. If they're not rotting, they may be developing callus or roots slowly. Check properly in spring.

5. **Match medium to species** - Natives need coarse, sandy mix. Camellias need acidic mix. Getting this wrong is a common cause of failure.

## Variations & Adaptations

### Mallet Cuttings

A semi-hardwood cutting that includes a section of the previous year's growth at the base, forming a T-shape (mallet). Used for:
- Berberis
- Mahonia
- Some conifers

The older wood at the base provides stability and additional cambium for rooting.

### Nodal vs Internodal

**Nodal:** Cut just below a node (standard approach)
**Internodal:** Cut between nodes

Most species root best from nodal cuttings, but some (clematis, for example) prefer internodal cuts.
