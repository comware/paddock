# Hardwood Cuttings

## Overview

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Intermediate |
| **Success Rate** | 60-90% typical |
| **Time to Root** | 3-6 months typical |
| **Best Season** | Winter (dormant season) |
| **Equipment Needed** | Secateurs, spade, well-drained soil or containers |

## What Are Hardwood Cuttings?

Hardwood cuttings are taken from fully dormant, mature woody stems during the winter months when deciduous plants have dropped their leaves. The wood is firm, brown, and completely hardened - the previous season's growth that has now become permanent structure.

This is one of the simplest and most hands-off propagation methods. Cuttings are typically planted directly outdoors or in deep containers and left to root over winter and spring. There's no need for humidity domes or constant attention - the natural cool, moist conditions of winter provide what the cuttings need.

Hardwood cuttings are particularly effective for deciduous trees and shrubs, fruiting plants, and many ornamentals. While they take longer than softwood cuttings, the success rate is often higher because the cuttings are more resilient and the process is less fussy.

## When to Use This Method

**Best for:**
- Deciduous fruit (figs, mulberries, pomegranates, quinces)
- Grape vines
- Roses (especially species and old garden roses)
- Willows, poplars, and dogwoods
- Currants and gooseberries
- Deciduous hedging (privet, forsythia)

**Not recommended for:**
- Evergreen plants (use semi-hardwood)
- Most conifers (need different techniques)
- Herbaceous plants
- Tropical and subtropical species

## Step-by-Step Process

### Step 1: Timing Your Cuttings

Take hardwood cuttings during **full winter dormancy** (June to August in Australia).

**Best timing:**
- After leaf fall for deciduous plants
- During coldest months
- Before buds swell in late winter

**Signs the plant is ready:**
- Leaves completely fallen
- No active growth
- Wood is hard and brown
- Buds are dormant (not swelling)

**Key points:**
- Earlier in dormancy (June-July) often better
- Avoid periods of hard frost for taking cuttings
- Some species root better from mid-winter wood

**Common mistakes:**
- Taking cuttings before leaves have fully fallen
- Waiting until buds are swelling in spring

### Step 2: Selecting Material

Choose healthy, vigorous stems from the current or previous season's growth.

**Selection criteria:**
- Pencil thickness (6-10mm diameter) is ideal
- Straight, healthy growth
- From healthy, disease-free plants
- Avoid very thin or very thick stems
- Current or one-year-old wood preferred

**What to look for:**
- Well-developed buds along stem
- No signs of disease or damage
- Vigorous parent plant

**Key points:**
- Thicker cuttings have more reserves but root slower
- Very thin cuttings may dessicate
- Water shoots (vigorous upright growth) often root well

**Common mistakes:**
- Using old, multi-year wood
- Choosing weak or damaged stems

### Step 3: Cutting the Material

Take long cuttings that will be trimmed to size.

**Process:**
1. Cut stems 30-45cm long from parent plant
2. Bundle and label by variety immediately
3. Trim to final size when preparing (see Step 4)

**Why long pieces:**
- Easier to handle
- Can trim to ideal length later
- Prevents confusion about top/bottom

**Storage if not planting immediately:**
- Bundle and bury in sand or soil
- Or wrap in damp newspaper in plastic bag
- Store in cool location (shed, garage)
- Plant within 2-3 weeks

**Key points:**
- Early morning is fine (no urgency unlike softwood)
- Can take multiple stems and process later
- Keep varieties separate and labeled

**Common mistakes:**
- Mixing up top and bottom of cuttings
- Letting cuttings dry out completely

### Step 4: Preparing Cuttings for Planting

Trim cuttings to final size and prepare for rooting.

**Standard cutting preparation:**
1. Trim to 20-30cm long
2. Make top cut just above a bud (angled cut, sloping away from bud)
3. Make bottom cut just below a bud (straight cut)
4. Remove any side shoots

**Why different cuts top and bottom:**
- Angled top sheds water, prevents rot
- Identifies top from bottom
- Straight bottom maximises contact with soil

**For some species, wound the base:**
- Remove thin strip of bark (2-3cm) on one or both sides
- Especially helpful for roses, difficult rooters

**Key points:**
- Most cuttings have 4-6 buds
- Only bottom 2-3 buds will be underground
- Top buds will produce shoots

**Common mistakes:**
- Cutting above a bud at bottom (no root initiation point)
- Planting upside down

### Step 5: Applying Rooting Hormone (Recommended)

Use strongest hormone formulation for hardwood.

**Application:**
1. Dip or roll base in hormone powder
2. Cover bottom 3-4cm including wounds
3. Tap off excess

**Hormone strength:**
- Use strong formula (yellow/No.3 or 8g/kg IBA)
- Hardwood needs stronger hormone than softwood

**Key points:**
- More important for difficult species
- Easy rooters (willows, poplars) don't need hormone
- Wound + hormone combination most effective

**Common mistakes:**
- Using weak hormone meant for softwood
- Skipping hormone on difficult species

### Step 6: Planting - Ground Method

The traditional approach: plant directly in garden soil.

**Site preparation:**
1. Choose well-drained spot (not waterlogged)
2. Dig trench 15-20cm deep
3. Add sand to bottom if soil is heavy
4. Work in compost if soil is poor

**Planting process:**
1. Insert cuttings vertically, 15cm apart
2. Two-thirds of cutting below ground
3. One-third (2-3 buds) above ground
4. Firm soil around cuttings
5. Water in well

**Trench method advantages:**
- Simple and low-tech
- Natural temperature and moisture
- No ongoing attention needed
- Good for large quantities

**Key points:**
- North-facing location (shade) often best
- Protect from rabbits/hares with mesh
- Label clearly - they look the same in winter!

**Common mistakes:**
- Planting in waterlogged soil
- Not deep enough (dries out)
- Forgetting which variety is which

### Step 7: Planting - Container Method

Alternative for smaller spaces or more control.

**Container setup:**
1. Use deep pots (20cm+) or tall tubes
2. Fill with 50/50 coarse sand and potting mix
3. Ensure drainage holes clear

**Planting process:**
1. Insert cutting vertically, halfway down pot
2. One-third above surface
3. Firm gently
4. Water in well

**Container placement:**
- Cold frame ideal (protection + cold exposure)
- Against south wall (shade in summer)
- Unheated greenhouse
- Can even be outdoors if protected from extremes

**Key points:**
- Containers need monitoring for moisture
- Protect from drying winds
- Don't bring into warm greenhouse

**Common mistakes:**
- Using shallow containers
- Bringing into warm conditions too early

### Step 8: Winter and Spring Care

Hardwood cuttings need minimal intervention.

**Winter months:**
- Essentially leave alone
- Check drainage after heavy rain
- Protect from severe frost if extreme
- Watch for animal damage

**Spring months:**
- Buds begin swelling (normal)
- Shoots appear (normal)
- DON'T assume roots have formed
- Continue to protect from extremes

**Watering:**
- Ground plantings rarely need water in winter
- Containers may need occasional water if drying
- Spring: water if soil dries out

**Key points:**
- Shoots appear before roots - don't disturb
- Patience through spring is essential
- Growth above ground doesn't mean ready to transplant

**Common mistakes:**
- Thinking shoots mean it's rooted
- Moving too early in spring
- Overwatering

### Step 9: Checking for Roots

Wait until late spring or early summer to assess.

**When to check:**
- After new shoots are several centimetres long
- When leaves are fully developed
- Typically November-December (late spring)

**How to check:**
- Container cuttings: Look for roots at drainage holes
- Ground cuttings: Gentle tug test
- Dig one to inspect if uncertain

**Signs of success:**
- Vigorous new growth
- Resistance when gently pulled
- Roots visible (containers)

**Key points:**
- Some species take full year before rooting well
- Failed cuttings usually obvious (dead, no shoots)
- Successful ones can stay in place until autumn

**Common mistakes:**
- Checking too early
- Disturbing before roots established

### Step 10: Transplanting

Move rooted cuttings to permanent positions.

**Best timing:**
- Late autumn (next dormant season)
- Or following winter
- Avoid transplanting in summer

**Process:**
1. Lift carefully to preserve roots
2. Plant at same depth or slightly deeper
3. Stake if tall
4. Water in well
5. Mulch around base

**First year care:**
- Water during dry periods
- Protect from strong winds
- Don't fertilise heavily first year

**Key points:**
- Autumn planting allows winter root establishment
- Handle roots gently - still developing
- Some growers leave until second winter

**Common mistakes:**
- Transplanting in summer heat
- Breaking newly formed roots
- Over-fertilising

## Environmental Requirements

| Factor | Optimal Range | Acceptable Range |
|--------|---------------|------------------|
| **Temperature** | 0-10°C (cold winter) | -5 to 15°C |
| **Moisture** | Moist but well-drained | Not waterlogged |
| **Light** | Any (dormant) → Indirect (shoots) | Shade to part sun |
| **Air circulation** | Good | Avoid stagnant wet conditions |

### Creating the Right Environment

**Option 1: Open ground (traditional)**
Trench in well-drained soil in sheltered location. Simplest method with excellent results for most species.

**Option 2: Cold frame**
Provides protection from extremes while maintaining cold exposure. Ideal for containers or more tender species.

**Option 3: Deep containers outdoors**
Flexible option for limited space. Needs monitoring for moisture. Position against wall for protection.

### Factors That Affect Speed

**Faster rooting:**
- Easy species (willow, fig, grape)
- Warm spring weather
- Well-drained soil
- Healthy parent material

**Slower rooting:**
- Difficult species (roses, some ornamentals)
- Cold, wet spring
- Heavy soil
- Weak or old cutting material

## Common Problems & Solutions

### Problem 1: Shoots Appear But Cutting Dies in Spring

**Symptoms:** Healthy-looking shoots in September, then wilting and death in October-November.

**Causes:**
- No roots formed - cutting exhausted reserves
- Shoots grew using stored energy, then starved
- Often due to poor cutting material or wrong timing

**Solutions:**
- Unfortunately, nothing to do once dying
- Take notes for next year
- Try earlier timing or different material

**Prevention:**
- Use strong, healthy cutting material
- Apply rooting hormone
- Ensure good drainage
- Be patient - some just fail

### Problem 2: Rot or Fungal Problems

**Symptoms:** Cutting soft and mushy, may have visible mould, doesn't produce shoots.

**Causes:**
- Waterlogged soil
- Poor drainage
- Damaged or diseased cutting material
- Contaminated tools

**Solutions:**
- Remove affected cuttings
- Improve drainage for remaining
- Check other cuttings for problems

**Prevention:**
- Well-drained site essential
- Add grit to heavy soils
- Use clean, sharp tools
- Choose healthy material

### Problem 3: Animal Damage

**Symptoms:** Cuttings nibbled, bark stripped, buds eaten.

**Causes:**
- Rabbits, hares
- Possums (Australia)
- Deer

**Solutions:**
- Wire mesh guards around cuttings
- Remove damaged portions if still viable
- Relocate to protected area

**Prevention:**
- Mesh protection from the start
- Cold frame with lid
- Physical barriers

## Pro Tips

1. **Mark the top clearly** - Paint the top cut with white paint or grafting wax. This prevents planting upside down (a surprisingly common mistake) and seals the cut against moisture loss and infection.

2. **Bundle and bury for callusing** - Some growers bundle cuttings, bury them upside down in sand for 4-6 weeks (callusing), then plant right way up. The warm soil at surface accelerates callus formation at what will become the base.

3. **Willows make their own rooting hormone** - Chop willow stems and soak in water for 24-48 hours. Use this "willow water" as a natural rooting hormone for other hardwood cuttings.

4. **Longer cuttings for difficult species** - For roses and other tricky plants, use 30cm cuttings rather than 20cm. More stored energy, higher success rate.

5. **Hot callusing for commercial operations** - Storing cuttings at 20°C for a few weeks before cold planting accelerates callus formation. Then plant into cold conditions to trigger root growth.

## Variations & Adaptations

### Simple Cuttings

Standard approach described above - single stem section with multiple buds.

### Mallet Cuttings

Include section of older (two-year) wood at base, forming a T-shape. Older wood provides more reserves and a wider base for rooting. Good for:
- Berberis
- Ribes (currants)
- Some conifers

### Vine Eyes

Single bud with short sections of stem above and below. Used for grapes and kiwifruit when material is limited. Each bud becomes a new plant.
