# Air Layering

## Overview

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Intermediate |
| **Success Rate** | 70-90% typical |
| **Time to Root** | 2-6 months typical |
| **Best Season** | Spring to early summer |
| **Equipment Needed** | Sharp knife, sphagnum moss, plastic wrap, ties, rooting hormone |

## What Is Air Layering?

Air layering (also called marcotting) is a propagation technique where roots are induced to form on a stem while it's still attached to the parent plant, but above ground level. A section of bark is removed, treated with rooting hormone, wrapped in moist sphagnum moss, and enclosed in plastic. Roots develop within the moss, and once established, the new plant is severed and potted.

This method is particularly valuable for plants with rigid stems that can't be bent to the ground for simple layering, or for producing larger plants more quickly than would be possible from cuttings. It's commonly used for houseplants, tropical plants, and ornamental trees.

Like simple layering, air layering is low-risk because the developing plant remains connected to its parent. If the layer fails, the parent plant is unharmed and you can try again.

## When to Use This Method

**Best for:**
- Large houseplants (rubber plant, fiddle leaf fig, dracaena)
- Tropical trees (citrus, mango, lychee)
- Magnolias and camellias
- Plants with upright, rigid stems
- Producing larger plants than cuttings allow
- Species difficult to root from cuttings

**Not recommended for:**
- Plants with very thin stems
- Plants easily propagated by cuttings
- Mass propagation (time-intensive per plant)
- Very young, soft growth

## Step-by-Step Process

### Step 1: Timing

**Best time:** Spring to early summer (September-December in Australia)

**Why this timing:**
- Plant in active growth
- Warmth encourages rooting
- Full growing season to establish
- Maximum sap flow for wound response

**Also possible:**
- Late summer (still warm enough)
- Indoor plants: any time with warmth

**Avoid:**
- Winter (too slow)
- Times of plant stress

**Key points:**
- Warm temperatures speed rooting dramatically
- Indoor plants with heating can layer year-round
- Outdoor plants need the warmer months

**Common mistakes:**
- Attempting in winter (very slow)
- Layering stressed or unhealthy plants

### Step 2: Selecting the Stem

Choose an appropriate location on the plant.

**Stem selection:**
- Current or one-year-old growth
- Pencil thickness to thumb thickness
- Healthy, typical growth
- 30-60cm from the growing tip

**Location on plant:**
- Below where you want the new plant's base
- Consider the finished plant size and shape
- Usually mid-way on indoor plants
- On a main branch for trees

**Key points:**
- Avoid very old, woody stems (slower rooting)
- The section above your cut becomes the new plant
- Think about the resulting plant's appearance

**Common mistakes:**
- Choosing too-thin stems
- Selecting old, heavily barked wood
- Poor position for finished plant shape

### Step 3: Preparing the Moss

Prepare sphagnum moss for wrapping.

**Process:**
1. Soak moss in water for 30-60 minutes
2. Squeeze out excess water firmly
3. Moss should be moist but not dripping
4. Fluff up to increase volume

**Quantity needed:**
- Enough to form ball/cylinder 8-10cm diameter
- More for larger stems
- Roughly two large handfuls per layer

**Key points:**
- Moss too wet = rot risk
- Moss too dry = rooting failure
- Pre-soaking ensures even moisture

**Common mistakes:**
- Moss not squeezed out enough
- Using dry moss (won't sustain roots)

### Step 4: Wounding the Stem

Create the wound where roots will develop.

**Ring bark method (most common):**
1. Make two parallel cuts around stem, 2-3cm apart
2. Cut through bark to the wood, not into wood
3. Remove ring of bark completely
4. Scrape any remaining green cambium layer
5. Allow to dry briefly (10-20 minutes)

**Tongue method (alternative):**
1. Make upward slanting cut into stem
2. Cut about one-third through
3. Insert toothpick to keep wound open
4. Good for thinner stems

**Key points:**
- Ring must be complete - no bridge of bark
- Scrape cambium thoroughly (green layer under bark)
- Wider ring = more rooting area
- Don't cut into the woody core

**Common mistakes:**
- Ring too narrow
- Leaving cambium layer (stem heals without rooting)
- Cutting too deep (weakens stem)

### Step 5: Applying Rooting Hormone

Treat the wounded area to encourage rooting.

**Application:**
1. Apply hormone to both cut edges of ring
2. Apply generously to exposed wood
3. Powder or paste/gel both work
4. Use strong strength for woody plants

**Key points:**
- Generous application improves success
- Cover all exposed surfaces
- Strong hormone for woody stems

**Common mistakes:**
- Skipping hormone (reduces success)
- Insufficient application

### Step 6: Applying the Moss

Wrap the wound with moist moss.

**Process:**
1. Take handful of prepared moss
2. Form around wound, covering completely
3. Build up to 8-10cm diameter ball
4. Ensure wound is completely covered
5. Moss should be firm but not compressed

**Key points:**
- Wound must be completely encased
- Firm contact with wound edges
- Ball big enough to sustain roots
- Even distribution around stem

**Common mistakes:**
- Moss ball too small
- Gaps in coverage
- Moss too compressed (poor air)

### Step 7: Wrapping with Plastic

Enclose the moss to retain moisture.

**Process:**
1. Cut plastic wrap 30-40cm square
2. Wrap tightly around moss ball
3. Overlap edges generously
4. Tie firmly at top and bottom
5. Ensure no gaps for moisture loss

**Tie technique:**
- Top tie: very tight (prevents water entry)
- Bottom tie: firm but allows some drainage
- Use string, twist ties, or grafting tape

**Optional: aluminium foil**
- Wrap over plastic
- Excludes light (may reduce algae)
- Helps retain heat
- Some propagators prefer this

**Key points:**
- Seal must be tight at top (rain entry causes rot)
- Bottom can be slightly loose (drainage)
- Clear plastic allows monitoring
- Foil adds heat retention

**Common mistakes:**
- Loose wrapping (dries out)
- No seal at top (water entry)
- Using black plastic (can't monitor)

### Step 8: Monitoring and Maintenance

Check progress and maintain conditions.

**What to check:**
- Moss staying moist (should see some condensation)
- No rot or mould
- Signs of roots (white tips visible through plastic)
- Plant health above the layer

**Maintaining moisture:**
- If moss drying, inject water with syringe
- Or carefully open bottom, add water, reseal
- Should rarely be necessary if well-sealed

**Timeline expectations:**
- Fast species: 4-8 weeks
- Medium: 8-16 weeks
- Slow: 16-26+ weeks

**Key points:**
- Check weekly initially
- Don't open unnecessarily (moisture loss)
- Patience - roots take time

**Common mistakes:**
- Opening to check too often
- Ignoring dried-out moss
- Giving up too early

### Step 9: Confirming Root Development

Know when roots are sufficient.

**Signs of rooting:**
- White or light brown roots visible through plastic
- Roots filling moss ball
- Strong root network established
- Usually multiple roots visible

**When ready:**
- Roots clearly visible throughout moss
- Root tips at edge of plastic
- Typically many roots, not just one or two

**Don't rush:**
- More roots = better success
- Insufficient roots = transplant shock
- Wait until roots are abundant

**Key points:**
- Clearly visible root network needed
- Patience produces stronger plants
- Better to wait than rush

**Common mistakes:**
- Severing with minimal roots
- Assuming one or two roots is enough

### Step 10: Severing and Potting

Cut the new plant free and pot up.

**Severing:**
1. Cut below the moss ball with sharp secateurs
2. Support the new plant as you cut
3. Remove plastic wrapping carefully
4. Leave moss attached to roots (don't disturb)

**Potting:**
1. Use pot just larger than root ball
2. Standard potting mix suitable
3. Position at same depth as rooting
4. Water in well
5. Keep in shade for 1-2 weeks

**Aftercare:**
- Reduce leaf area if plant is large (optional)
- Keep humidity high initially
- Shade from direct sun
- Water regularly
- Gradually introduce to normal conditions

**Key points:**
- Don't remove moss (roots are growing through it)
- Minimise root disturbance
- Support top-heavy plants with stakes

**Common mistakes:**
- Removing moss (damages roots)
- Potting in full sun immediately
- Overwatering compensating for stress

## Common Problems & Solutions

### Problem 1: Moss Dries Out

**Symptoms:** Moss looks pale, no condensation visible, roots brown or not developing.

**Causes:**
- Poor seal on plastic
- Hot conditions
- Plastic degrading in sun

**Solutions:**
- Rewet moss (inject water or open bottom)
- Reseal more thoroughly
- Add foil layer for protection

**Prevention:**
- Tight seal especially at top
- Use quality plastic
- Foil outer layer protects

### Problem 2: Rot or Mould Develops

**Symptoms:** Black or mushy wound, foul smell, mould on moss.

**Causes:**
- Moss too wet at setup
- Water entering from top
- Poor air exchange
- Diseased parent material

**Solutions:**
- Usually too late to save
- If caught early, unwrap, dry, rewrap
- Start fresh layer in new location

**Prevention:**
- Squeeze moss thoroughly
- Seal top tightly
- Don't pack moss too densely

### Problem 3: Callus But No Roots

**Symptoms:** Wound heals over with callus tissue but no roots emerge.

**Causes:**
- Cambium not completely removed
- Hormone not applied
- Temperature too cold
- Species naturally difficult

**Solutions:**
- May eventually root with patience
- Consider re-wounding if accessible
- Increase warmth

**Prevention:**
- Complete cambium removal
- Apply hormone thoroughly
- Ensure warm conditions

## Pro Tips

1. **Use two layers at once** - If you have space on the plant, set up two air layers at different heights. If one fails, you have a backup. Both may succeed.

2. **Time your severing** - Sever in autumn for outdoor plants, allowing winter establishment. For houseplants, sever when convenient but keep warm and humid after.

3. **The new plant is top-heavy** - The new plant will have a stem and leaves but a small root system. Stake it and keep humidity high until roots catch up.

4. **Air layering pods exist** - Commercial air layering pods (plastic clamshells) are reusable and make the process easier. Worth buying if you do this regularly.

5. **Use on leggy houseplants** - Perfect for rejuvenating rubber plants and dracaenas that have become too tall. Layer, sever, and you have a fresh compact plant. The stump often resprouts too.
