# Simple Layering

## Overview

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Beginner |
| **Success Rate** | 80-95% typical |
| **Time to Root** | 3-12 months typical |
| **Best Season** | Spring or autumn |
| **Equipment Needed** | Garden fork, wire pegs/stones, knife, soil |

## What Is Simple Layering?

Simple layering (also called ground layering) is a propagation method where a low-growing branch is bent to the ground, wounded, and buried while still attached to the parent plant. The buried section develops roots while being nourished by the parent, and once rooted, is severed to become an independent plant.

This technique mimics what many plants do naturally - branches that touch the ground often root spontaneously. By deliberately positioning and wounding a branch, we encourage and speed up this natural process.

Simple layering is one of the safest propagation methods because the developing plant remains connected to its parent throughout the rooting period. There's no risk of wilting or failure from lack of water. It's particularly valuable for plants that are difficult to root from cuttings.

## When to Use This Method

**Best for:**
- Climbers and ramblers (wisteria, jasmine, climbing roses)
- Low-branching shrubs (rhododendrons, magnolias, camellias)
- Plants difficult to root from cuttings
- Borderline hardy plants where cuttings struggle
- Creating a few high-value plants (quality over quantity)

**Not recommended for:**
- Upright trees without low branches
- Plants that don't produce flexible stems
- Mass propagation (too slow, space-intensive)
- Plants with very brittle stems

## Step-by-Step Process

### Step 1: Timing

Layer in **spring** (September-November) or **autumn** (March-May).

**Spring layering:**
- Young, flexible growth
- Full growing season ahead
- May root by autumn
- Best for deciduous plants

**Autumn layering:**
- Current year's ripened growth
- Roots develop slowly over winter
- Ready following spring or summer
- Good for evergreens

**Best conditions:**
- Soil moist and workable
- Not frozen or waterlogged
- Parent plant in active growth (or just entering dormancy)

**Key points:**
- Autumn layers often more reliable than spring
- Some plants layer any time (willows, etc.)
- Consider when you'll sever (following autumn ideal)

**Common mistakes:**
- Layering in dry summer (moisture stress)
- Attempting with frozen ground

### Step 2: Selecting a Suitable Stem

Choose a healthy, flexible branch that reaches the ground.

**Selection criteria:**
- Current or one-year-old growth (flexible, not too woody)
- Low-growing or can be bent to ground without snapping
- Healthy, typical growth
- 30-60cm from tip will be buried
- Tip will remain above ground

**Ideal characteristics:**
- Pencil-thick to finger-thick
- Bends without breaking
- No pest or disease issues
- Multiple shoots available (try more than one)

**Key points:**
- Younger wood roots faster
- Must be flexible enough to bend safely
- Don't force brittle stems

**Common mistakes:**
- Using old, woody branches (slow or won't root)
- Snapping stem by bending too far

### Step 3: Preparing the Site

Make the ground ready to receive the layer.

**Process:**
1. Identify where stem will touch ground
2. Loosen soil with fork (15cm deep, 30cm diameter)
3. Work in compost or peat for moisture retention
4. Make shallow trench or depression for stem

**Site requirements:**
- Within reach of selected branch
- Free-draining but moisture-retentive
- Some shade preferred (less drying)
- Accessible for monitoring

**Key points:**
- Good soil contact essential
- Loose soil makes root penetration easier
- Adding organic matter helps

**Common mistakes:**
- Not loosening compacted soil
- Choosing a site that dries out

### Step 4: Wounding the Stem

Create a wound where roots will develop.

**Wounding technique:**
1. Measure 30-40cm from stem tip
2. Make a slanting cut into stem, angled toward tip
3. Cut about halfway through the stem
4. Insert matchstick or toothpick to keep wound open

**Alternative methods:**
- Tongue cut: Partial cut, bend open to form tongue
- Scraping: Remove bark in 3-5cm strip
- Notch: Small V-shaped notch

**Key points:**
- Wound should be on underside (will face down)
- Don't cut more than halfway (stem must survive)
- Wounding is essential - don't skip this step

**Common mistakes:**
- Cutting too deep (severs stem)
- Forgetting to wound (much slower rooting)

### Step 5: Applying Rooting Hormone

Treat the wound to encourage rooting.

**Application:**
1. Apply rooting hormone powder or gel to wound
2. Work into cut surfaces
3. Include tongue/flap if present

**Hormone type:**
- Medium strength suitable for most
- Strong for difficult species
- Optional for easy rooters (willows, etc.)

**Key points:**
- Ensures good contact with wound
- Fresh hormone works better
- Not essential but improves results

**Common mistakes:**
- Skipping hormone on difficult species
- Using expired hormone

### Step 6: Burying the Layer

Position and cover the wounded section.

**Process:**
1. Bend stem gently into prepared trench
2. Position wound 5-10cm below soil surface
3. Pin firmly with wire pegs or weight with stones
4. Backfill with soil mixture
5. Leave tip exposed and upright

**Tip positioning:**
- 15-20cm of stem tip above ground
- Stake if needed to keep upright
- This becomes the new plant's top growth

**Key points:**
- Firm soil contact with wound essential
- Don't damage stem when pegging
- Tip must remain above ground and healthy

**Common mistakes:**
- Burying too shallow (dries out)
- Burying the tip (can't photosynthesise)
- Inadequate pegging (springs out)

### Step 7: Aftercare

Maintain conditions while roots develop.

**Ongoing care:**
- Keep soil consistently moist
- Mulch to retain moisture
- Check pegs haven't dislodged
- Remove weeds from around layer

**Watering:**
- Regular in dry periods
- Don't let area dry completely
- Well-mulched layers need less water

**Duration:**
- Fast rooters (willows, honeysuckle): 3-6 months
- Medium (roses, hydrangeas): 6-12 months
- Slow (magnolias, rhododendrons): 12-24 months

**Key points:**
- Patience is essential
- Roots form slowly underground
- Keep area undisturbed

**Common mistakes:**
- Letting layer dry out
- Disturbing to "check" too often
- Giving up too early

### Step 8: Checking for Rooting

Assess whether roots have developed.

**When to check:**
- After minimum expected time for species
- When new growth is vigorous
- Before planning to sever

**How to check:**
1. Gently scrape away some soil
2. Look for white roots at wound site
3. If rooted, roots will be visible
4. Replace soil carefully if checking

**Signs of success:**
- Strong new top growth
- Resistance when very gently tugged
- Visible roots at wound
- Layer survives partial severing

**Key points:**
- Don't check too frequently
- Some roots can establish without being visible
- New growth is a good indicator

**Common mistakes:**
- Damaging developing roots when checking
- Checking too often
- Assuming no roots because not visible

### Step 9: Severing the Layer

Cut the new plant free from its parent.

**When ready:**
- Root system well established
- Preferably during dormancy or cool weather
- Typically autumn after spring layering

**Severing process:**
1. Gradually sever over 2-4 weeks (reduces shock)
2. Week 1: Cut halfway through connecting stem
3. Week 2: Cut another quarter
4. Week 3-4: Complete the cut
5. Or: cut completely if roots are very strong

**Key points:**
- Gradual severing reduces transplant shock
- Autumn severing allows winter establishment
- Don't rush if uncertain about roots

**Common mistakes:**
- Severing before adequate roots
- Not allowing gradual transition
- Severing during hot, dry weather

### Step 10: Transplanting

Move the new plant to its permanent position.

**Process:**
1. After complete severing, wait 2-4 weeks
2. Lift carefully with good root ball
3. Transplant to prepared position
4. Water well and mulch
5. Stake if top-heavy

**Timing:**
- Autumn ideal (establishes over winter)
- Spring acceptable
- Avoid summer heat

**Care after transplanting:**
- Water regularly for first season
- Protect from extremes
- Don't fertilise heavily immediately

**Key points:**
- Handle roots gently
- Maintain soil on roots if possible
- Position at same depth as when layered

**Common mistakes:**
- Rushing to transplant
- Disturbing root ball
- Planting too deep

## Common Problems & Solutions

### Problem 1: Stem Breaks While Bending

**Symptoms:** Stem snaps or cracks when trying to bend to ground.

**Causes:**
- Wood too old and rigid
- Bent too sharply
- Cold weather makes wood brittle

**Solutions:**
- If partially cracked, may still work - bury anyway
- If completely broken, discard and try another
- Try air layering instead for difficult stems

**Prevention:**
- Use younger, more flexible growth
- Bend gradually over time if needed
- Warm days are better for bending

### Problem 2: Layer Dries Out and Dies

**Symptoms:** Buried portion dies, no rooting occurs.

**Causes:**
- Soil too dry
- Shallow burial
- Hot, exposed site
- Poor soil contact

**Solutions:**
- Usually too late to save
- Start new layer with better conditions

**Prevention:**
- Mulch heavily
- Water regularly in dry periods
- Bury at adequate depth
- Choose shadier site

### Problem 3: No Roots After Expected Time

**Symptoms:** Layer still alive but no roots visible after many months.

**Causes:**
- No wound made (or healed over)
- Wrong time of year
- Species is naturally slow
- Environmental conditions poor

**Solutions:**
- Re-wound if accessible
- Give more time (some take 2+ years)
- Ensure conditions are right
- Accept some species are difficult

**Prevention:**
- Always wound the stem
- Keep wound open (matchstick)
- Apply rooting hormone
- Research species-specific timing

## Pro Tips

1. **Layer into a sunken pot** - Sink a pot into the ground beneath the layer. Roots develop in the pot, making eventual transplanting much easier. Just lift the pot when ready.

2. **Try multiple layers at once** - Most shrubs have several suitable branches. Layer 2-3 at the same time to increase your chances of success and produce multiple plants.

3. **Stake the tip upright** - An upright growing tip makes a better-shaped plant. Stake it vertically from the start, and it will develop natural apical dominance.

4. **Gradual severance is worth the effort** - Cutting the connection in stages over a month really does reduce transplant shock. The layer learns to support itself gradually.

5. **Check in autumn, sever in spring** - Check for rooting in autumn, but wait until early spring to sever and transplant. This allows a full growing season for establishment.

## Variations

### Serpentine Layering

For long, flexible stems (like vines):
1. Bury multiple points along a single stem
2. Alternate buried and exposed sections
3. Each buried node roots
4. Results in multiple plants from one stem

Good for: Grapes, wisteria, climbing hydrangea

### Mound Layering (Stool Layering)

For multi-stemmed shrubs:
1. Cut parent plant back hard
2. Mound soil over emerging shoots
3. Each shoot roots into mound
4. Separate rooted shoots in autumn

Good for: Gooseberries, quinces, some rootstocks
