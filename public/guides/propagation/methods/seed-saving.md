# Seed Saving

## Overview

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Intermediate |
| **Success Rate** | 80-95% germination typical |
| **Time Investment** | Season-long |
| **Best Season** | Summer to autumn (collection) |
| **Equipment Needed** | Containers, labels, dry storage, envelopes |

## What Is Seed Saving?

Seed saving is the practice of collecting, processing, and storing seeds from plants for future sowing. Unlike vegetative propagation which creates clones, seed-grown plants are the offspring of their parents and may show variation.

For self-pollinating and stable varieties, seed saving is an economical and rewarding way to maintain your own seed stock. For cross-pollinating plants, saving seed requires more planning to ensure true-to-type offspring.

Seed saving connects gardeners to centuries of agricultural tradition. Every heirloom variety exists because generations of growers saved and shared seed. By learning this skill, you participate in that lineage.

## When to Use This Method

**Best for:**
- Open-pollinated (OP) and heirloom varieties
- Self-pollinating plants (tomatoes, beans, lettuce, peppers)
- Saving money on annual seed purchases
- Preserving rare or local varieties
- Adapting plants to your local conditions

**Not recommended for:**
- F1 hybrid varieties (offspring won't be true to type)
- Cross-pollinating plants without isolation (will be variable)
- Plants you want genetically identical to parent (use cuttings)
- Legally protected varieties (plant breeder's rights)

## Understanding Pollination Types

Before saving seed, understand how your plant reproduces.

### Self-Pollinating Plants

**Characteristics:**
- Flowers pollinate themselves before opening
- Limited natural cross-pollination
- Offspring very similar to parents
- Easiest for seed saving

**Examples:** Tomatoes, beans, peas, lettuce, peppers (hot peppers cross more easily)

**Isolation needed:** Minimal - a few metres typically sufficient

### Cross-Pollinating Plants

**Characteristics:**
- Require pollen from another flower
- May cross with other varieties of same species
- Offspring can be highly variable
- Require isolation for true-to-type seed

**Examples:** Brassicas (cabbage, broccoli), cucurbits (squash, cucumber), corn, carrots, beets

**Isolation needed:** Significant - 400m to several kilometres depending on species

### What Happens Without Isolation

**Year 1:** Grow multiple varieties, save seed
**Year 2:** Plant saved seed - offspring may show:
- Unexpected colours, shapes, sizes
- Mixed characteristics from both parents
- Sometimes interesting, often undesirable
- F1 hybrid breakdown (if parents were hybrids)

## Step-by-Step Process

### Step 1: Selecting Plants for Seed Saving

Choose the best individuals for seed production.

**Selection criteria:**
- True to variety type
- Healthiest, most vigorous plants
- Best fruit/flower characteristics
- Good disease resistance
- Early maturity (desirable for adaptation)

**Mark seed plants:**
- Flag or label plants selected for seed
- Don't harvest these for eating
- Allow to fully mature
- Protect from animals if needed

**Key points:**
- Select multiple plants (genetic diversity)
- Avoid saving from the earliest OR latest maturing
- Choose "typical" specimens, not outliers

**Common mistakes:**
- Saving seed from the weakest plants (harvest rest first)
- Using only one plant (genetic bottleneck)
- Saving from atypical specimens

### Step 2: Allowing Seeds to Mature

Let seeds develop fully before harvesting.

**Dry seeds (beans, lettuce, flowers):**
- Leave on plant until pods/heads are dry
- Seeds should be hard and separated
- Harvest before shattering/dispersal
- Often rattle in dry pods

**Wet seeds (tomatoes, cucumbers, squash):**
- Fruit should be fully ripe to overripe
- Often past edible stage
- Leave on plant as long as possible
- Seeds should be fully developed inside

**Key points:**
- Patience - immature seeds won't germinate well
- Timing - before seeds disperse naturally
- Weather - harvest before wet weather if drying on plant

**Common mistakes:**
- Harvesting too early (immature, low germination)
- Letting dry seeds shatter (lost to ground)

### Step 3: Harvesting Seeds

Collect mature seeds from plants.

**Dry seed method:**
1. Cut entire seed heads or pods
2. Place in paper bags or trays
3. Label immediately
4. Bring to dry location for processing

**Wet seed method:**
1. Cut fruit from plant
2. Scoop out seeds with surrounding pulp
3. Place in container for processing
4. Process within a few days

**Key points:**
- Harvest on dry days when possible
- Label everything immediately
- Don't mix varieties during harvest

**Common mistakes:**
- Harvesting wet (causes mould)
- Losing track of varieties

### Step 4: Processing Dry Seeds

Clean and prepare dry seeds for storage.

**Threshing (releasing seeds from pods/heads):**
1. Crush pods/heads gently
2. Rub between hands
3. Use old pillowcase (thresh inside it)
4. Seeds separate from chaff

**Winnowing (separating seeds from chaff):**
1. Pour seeds from one container to another
2. Let gentle breeze blow chaff away
3. Or blow gently yourself
4. Repeat until clean

**Sieving:**
- Different mesh sizes separate debris
- Seeds fall through appropriate size
- Larger debris stays on top

**Key points:**
- Completely dry before storage
- Clean seeds store better
- Some chaff is okay

**Common mistakes:**
- Storing with too much debris
- Not drying completely first

### Step 5: Processing Wet Seeds

Extract seeds from wet fruit.

**Fermentation method (tomatoes, some cucurbits):**
1. Scoop seeds and pulp into container
2. Add equal amount of water
3. Cover loosely, place in warm spot
4. Stir daily for 2-4 days
5. Viable seeds sink, pulp and dead seeds float
6. Pour off floating material
7. Rinse good seeds thoroughly
8. Dry on plate or screen

**Why ferment:**
- Removes germination inhibitors
- Kills some seed-borne diseases
- Good seeds separate from bad
- Traditional method for tomatoes

**Simple rinse method (cucumbers, squash):**
1. Scoop seeds from fruit
2. Rinse in strainer
3. Remove pulp manually
4. Spread to dry

**Key points:**
- Fermentation produces odour (work outside)
- Don't ferment too long (seeds may germinate)
- Dry completely before storage

**Common mistakes:**
- Over-fermentation (seeds start sprouting)
- Insufficient rinsing (pulp remnants)
- Drying too slowly (mould develops)

### Step 6: Drying Seeds

Ensure seeds are completely dry before storage.

**Drying method:**
1. Spread seeds in single layer
2. Use plate, screen, or paper towel
3. Place in warm, dry, airy location
4. Stir occasionally for even drying
5. Dry for 1-2 weeks minimum

**Testing dryness:**
- Seeds should be hard, not pliable
- Small seeds snap or shatter when bent
- Large seeds can be tested with fingernail (shouldn't dent)
- No moisture felt when handling

**Speeding drying:**
- Fan circulation helps
- Low dehumidified room
- NOT in oven (kills seeds)
- Silica gel in container (careful - can over-dry)

**Key points:**
- Drying is critical - mould destroys wet seeds
- Air circulation is important
- Patience - rush causes failures

**Common mistakes:**
- Storing before fully dry (mould)
- Using heat to dry (kills seeds)
- Layers too thick (dries unevenly)

### Step 7: Cleaning Final Seeds

Prepare clean seeds for storage.

**Final cleaning:**
- Remove remaining chaff or debris
- Discard obviously damaged seeds
- Remove discoloured or shrivelled seeds
- Sort for uniformity if desired

**Quality indicators:**
- Full, plump seeds
- Uniform colour
- Appropriate size for variety
- No signs of pest damage

**Key points:**
- Clean seeds store better
- Damaged seeds may not germinate
- Uniform size suggests uniform maturity

### Step 8: Storing Seeds

Package seeds for long-term viability.

**Packaging:**
1. Place seeds in paper envelope
2. Label with: variety name, date collected, any notes
3. Store envelope in airtight container
4. Add silica gel packet to container
5. Store in cool, dark location

**Storage conditions:**
- Cool: Refrigerator ideal (5°C)
- Dry: Low humidity essential
- Dark: Light degrades viability
- Stable: Avoid temperature fluctuations

**Container options:**
- Glass jars with tight lids
- Plastic containers with good seals
- Metal tins (ensure dry)
- Always with desiccant

**Key points:**
- Cool + dry = long life
- Label everything (you WILL forget)
- Desiccant absorbs residual moisture

**Common mistakes:**
- Warm, humid storage (rapid viability loss)
- Forgetting to label
- Plastic bags alone (not airtight enough)

### Step 9: Testing Germination

Check viability before planting season.

**Simple germination test:**
1. Place 10 seeds on damp paper towel
2. Fold towel over seeds
3. Place in plastic bag (partly open)
4. Keep warm (20-25°C)
5. Check daily for germination
6. Count germinated seeds after species' typical time

**Interpreting results:**
- 8-10 germinate = Excellent (80-100%)
- 6-7 germinate = Good (60-70%)
- 4-5 germinate = Acceptable (sow more thickly)
- Below 4 = Poor (may need fresh seed)

**When to test:**
- Before planting season
- Old seed you're unsure about
- Annually for stored seed

**Key points:**
- Tests small sample, may not be perfect
- Old seed may need more time to germinate
- Poor results = plant more densely or get fresh seed

### Step 10: Record Keeping

Document your seed saving for future reference.

**Records to keep:**
- Variety name and source
- Date collected
- Number of seeds (approximate)
- Parent plant notes
- Storage location
- Germination test results
- Planting and harvest notes

**Why record:**
- Track what works
- Remember which varieties to continue
- Note any variation or problems
- Build institutional knowledge

**Key points:**
- Simple system you'll actually use
- Digital or paper both work
- Update regularly

## Seed Viability by Species

Different seeds last different lengths in storage.

| Species | Years Viable | Notes |
|---------|--------------|-------|
| Onion, leek | 1-2 | Short-lived, test annually |
| Corn, parsnip | 1-2 | Use quickly |
| Bean, pea | 3-4 | Medium storage life |
| Carrot, pepper | 3-4 | Store cool and dry |
| Tomato, eggplant | 4-5 | Good keepers |
| Brassicas | 4-5 | Cool storage extends life |
| Cucumber, melon | 5-6 | Long-lived |
| Lettuce | 5-6 | Germinates best when fresh |

*Cool, dry storage can extend all of these significantly.*

## Common Problems & Solutions

### Problem 1: Low Germination Rate

**Symptoms:** Few seeds sprout from saved batch.

**Causes:**
- Seeds harvested too early
- Improper drying (too moist)
- Poor storage conditions
- Old seed
- Seed-borne disease

**Solutions:**
- Test before planting (adjust sowing rate)
- Purchase fresh seed as backup
- Review harvesting and storage process

**Prevention:**
- Allow full maturity before harvest
- Dry thoroughly
- Store cool and dry
- Test before each season

### Problem 2: Seeds Mouldy in Storage

**Symptoms:** White or coloured mould on stored seeds.

**Causes:**
- Seeds not fully dry when stored
- Humid storage conditions
- Container not airtight
- No desiccant used

**Solutions:**
- Usually too late to save
- May try drying and testing
- Often best to discard

**Prevention:**
- Complete drying essential
- Use desiccant
- Airtight containers
- Cool, dry storage

### Problem 3: Offspring Don't Match Parent

**Symptoms:** Plants from saved seed look different from parent plant.

**Causes:**
- Cross-pollination occurred
- Parent was F1 hybrid
- Normal variation in cross-pollinators
- Different variety got mixed in

**Solutions:**
- Grow out and select best individuals
- Accept the variation (may be interesting)
- Source fresh seed of original variety

**Prevention:**
- Isolate varieties appropriately
- Don't save from F1 hybrids
- Careful labeling and separation
- Know your plant's pollination type

## Species-Specific Notes

### Tomatoes

- Self-pollinating (mostly true to type)
- Use fermentation method
- Ferment 2-4 days
- Rinse thoroughly and dry
- Seeds viable 4-5+ years

### Beans and Peas

- Self-pollinating
- Leave pods on plant until dry
- Thresh when completely dry
- Easy for beginners
- Viable 3-4 years

### Lettuce

- Self-pollinating
- Allow to bolt and flower
- Harvest fluffy seed heads
- Crush and winnow
- Viable 5-6 years

### Peppers

- Mostly self-pollinating
- Hot peppers cross more easily
- Harvest from very ripe fruit
- Rinse and dry
- Viable 2-4 years

### Squash and Cucurbits

- Cross-pollinating within species
- Need isolation OR hand pollination
- Harvest from overripe fruit
- Rinse and dry
- Viable 5-6 years

## Pro Tips

1. **Select for your conditions** - The best seed saving adapts varieties to your specific garden. Select from plants that performed well in YOUR conditions, and over generations you develop locally adapted strains.

2. **Save from at least 5 plants** - Saving from a single plant narrows genetic diversity. Using 5-10 plants maintains healthy variation and avoids inbreeding depression.

3. **Know your isolation distances** - Tomatoes need just a few metres between varieties. Corn needs 1km+ for pure seed. Research your specific crop's requirements.

4. **The fermentation smell is normal** - Tomato seed fermentation smells bad. This is the process working. Do it outside if possible, and don't panic.

5. **Seed libraries and swaps extend your collection** - Join local seed saver networks to exchange varieties and gain access to rare seeds. Sharing seeds builds community and preserves diversity.
