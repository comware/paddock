|-----------|-------|

## What Is Whip and Tongue Grafting?

Whip and tongue grafting (also called splice grafting or whip grafting) is a technique for joining two plant pieces of similar diameter. A matching diagonal cut is made on both the rootstock and scion, with an additional "tongue" cut that allows the pieces to interlock. This creates maximum cambium contact and a very strong union.

This is the most common grafting method for fruit trees, particularly apples, pears, plums, and cherries. It's also used for ornamental trees, roses, and many other woody plants. The technique requires practice to master but produces excellent results.

Grafting allows growers to combine the best qualities of two plants: a hardy, vigorous, or dwarfing rootstock with a scion (top part) that produces desired fruit, flowers, or foliage.

## When to Use This Method

**Best for:**
- Fruit trees (apple, pear, plum, cherry)
- Rose production
- Ornamental trees
- Nut trees
- Grape vines
- Any woody plant where scion and rootstock are similar diameter

**Not recommended for:**
- Very different stem diameters (use cleft grafting instead)
- Herbaceous plants
- Active growth periods
- Beginners without practice (start with simpler grafts)

## Understanding the Components

### Rootstock

The lower portion providing the root system.

**Characteristics:**
- Determines tree size (dwarf, semi-dwarf, standard)
- Provides disease resistance
- Affects hardiness and adaptation
- Usually unnamed variety bred for roots

**Common fruit tree rootstocks (Australia):**
- Apple: MM106 (semi-dwarf), M26 (dwarf), M9 (very dwarf)
- Pear: Quince A, Quince C
- Stone fruit: Nemaguard, Mariana, Myrobalan
- Cherry: Colt, Gisela

**Sourcing:**
- Specialist nurseries
- Grafting suppliers
- Sometimes grown from seed (seedling rootstock)

### Scionwood

The upper portion providing the desired variety.

**Collection:**
- Cut during dormancy (June-August in Australia)
- From healthy, true-to-type trees
- Select pencil-thick, one-year-old wood
- Take from mid-section of shoots (not tips)

**Storage:**
- Bundle and label immediately
- Wrap in damp paper, place in plastic bag
- Store in refrigerator (2-4°C)
- Use within 2-3 months

## Step-by-Step Process

### Step 1: Timing

Graft in **late winter** (July-August in Australia) while still dormant.

**Ideal timing:**
- Just before bud swell
- Rootstock still dormant
- Stored scion still dormant
- Cold enough to prevent drying

**Why late winter:**
- Cambium just becoming active
- Strong callus formation
- Scion remains dormant until union established
- Natural growth resumes after healing

**Key points:**
- Too early = slow healing
- Too late = scion breaks bud before union
- Timing varies by climate and species

**Common mistakes:**
- Grafting when rootstock buds are swelling
- Using scion that has already broken dormancy

### Step 2: Preparing Rootstock

Ready the base plant for grafting.

**If field planting (already planted rootstock):**
1. Cut rootstock at desired height (10-15cm above ground typical)
2. Make smooth horizontal cut first
3. Select straight section for grafting
4. Graft immediately

**If bench grafting (grafting before planting):**
1. Trim roots if very long
2. Cut top at suitable height
3. Work at comfortable bench height
4. Can do many in succession

**Key points:**
- Diameter should match scion closely
- Straight, clean section of stem
- Work quickly to prevent drying

**Common mistakes:**
- Rootstock diameter too different from scion
- Letting cut surface dry before grafting

### Step 3: Making the Rootstock Cut

Create the sloping cut for attachment.

**The whip cut:**
1. Hold rootstock firmly
2. Place knife on stem at 30° angle
3. Draw knife through in single smooth stroke
4. Cut should be 3-5cm long (about 4x stem diameter)
5. Surface should be flat and smooth

**Cut quality:**
- Single pass (no sawing)
- Flat surface (not concave or convex)
- Clean (no crushing)
- Correct angle (about 30°)

**Key points:**
- Sharp knife essential
- Practice on scrap wood first
- The cut length determines union strength

**Common mistakes:**
- Multiple cuts (rough surface)
- Cut too short (weak union)
- Knife not sharp (crushed tissue)

### Step 4: Making the Tongue Cut on Rootstock

Add the interlocking tongue.

**The tongue cut:**
1. Start about 1/3 down from top of the sloping cut
2. Cut downward at slight angle, parallel to grain
3. Cut should be about 1/3 of the stem's diameter deep
4. Create a "tongue" of wood that lifts slightly

**Positioning:**
- Beginning: 1/3 from top of slope
- Direction: Into the cut, not across it
- Depth: 1/3 of stem diameter

**Key points:**
- Don't cut tongue off (it must hinge)
- Smooth single cut
- Practice essential

**Common mistakes:**
- Tongue too deep (breaks off)
- Tongue in wrong position
- Multiple cuts (weak tongue)

### Step 5: Preparing the Scion

Cut scionwood to match rootstock.

**Trimming scion:**
1. Select section with 3-4 good buds
2. Make horizontal cut just above top bud
3. Bottom will have the graft cuts

**Making matching cuts:**
1. Make whip cut matching rootstock length and angle
2. Make tongue cut matching position and depth
3. Handle only at ends (don't touch cut surfaces)

**Key points:**
- Scion diameter should match rootstock
- Cuts must match precisely
- Keep cut surfaces clean

**Common mistakes:**
- Scion much different diameter than rootstock
- Touching cut surfaces (oils and contamination)
- Non-matching angles

### Step 6: Joining the Pieces

Fit scion and rootstock together.

**Assembly:**
1. Slide tongues together
2. Push until cuts match along full length
3. Tongues should interlock
4. Cambium layers must align on at least one side

**Cambium alignment:**
- Cambium is the green layer just under bark
- Maximum contact = strongest union
- If diameters differ slightly, align on one side
- Some cambium contact on both sides is ideal

**Key points:**
- Snug fit without forcing
- Full contact along cut length
- Cambium alignment critical

**Common mistakes:**
- Leaving gaps in union
- Pushing too hard (splitting tongues)
- Poor cambium alignment

### Step 7: Wrapping the Union

Secure and protect the graft.

**Wrapping technique:**
1. Start just below the union
2. Wrap upward with overlapping turns
3. Cover entire union and exposed cuts
4. Wrap tightly but don't strangle
5. Tie off or tuck end

**Wrapping materials:**
- Grafting tape (stretchy, best option)
- Wide rubber bands
- Parafilm (self-sealing)
- Masking tape (less ideal)

**Key points:**
- Complete coverage of union
- Firm tension (not too tight)
- No exposed cut surfaces

**Common mistakes:**
- Gaps in wrapping
- Too loose (doesn't hold)
- Too tight (strangles growth)

### Step 8: Sealing Exposed Surfaces

Protect from drying.

**What to seal:**
- Top of scion (horizontal cut above buds)
- Any exposed rootstock bark
- Edges of wrapping if not complete

**Sealants:**
- Grafting wax (traditional)
- Pruning seal compounds
- Doc Farwell's or similar products
- Even candle wax works

**Application:**
- Completely cover exposed cuts
- Thin coating sufficient
- Don't coat buds (they need to emerge)

**Key points:**
- Sealing prevents critical water loss
- Don't skip this step
- Avoid getting on bud tips

**Common mistakes:**
- Forgetting to seal top cut
- Coating over the buds

### Step 9: Aftercare and Environment

Provide conditions for healing.

**For bench grafts (before planting):**
1. Bundle grafted plants
2. Store in cool (5-10°C), humid conditions
3. Heel in to moist sawdust or store in plastic
4. Check periodically for drying or mould
5. Plant out when callus has formed (2-4 weeks)

**For field grafts:**
1. Protect from sun and wind (shade if exposed)
2. Keep rootstock watered
3. Watch for pests damaging graft
4. Stake if needed to prevent breakage

**Healing indicators:**
- Callus tissue forming at union (white growth)
- Scion buds swelling
- No drying or browning

**Key points:**
- Humidity prevents drying during healing
- Cool delays bud break until union forms
- Callus formation indicates success

**Common mistakes:**
- Letting grafts dry out
- Warm conditions forcing early bud break
- Not protecting from sun/wind

### Step 10: First Season Care

Manage the young grafted tree.

**As growth begins:**
- Buds on scion break and grow
- Rootstock may send up suckers - remove promptly
- Support new growth if needed
- Maintain water and nutrition

**Removing wrapping:**
- Check union after 6-8 weeks
- If callus is complete, remove or loosen tape
- If not healed, leave longer
- Don't let tape girdle growing stem

**First season goals:**
- Establish strong union
- Encourage scion growth
- Prevent rootstock dominance
- Shape future tree

**Key points:**
- Always remove rootstock suckers
- Don't let tape strangle the graft
- This is the critical establishment year

**Common mistakes:**
- Ignoring rootstock suckers
- Leaving tape too long
- Expecting fruit this year

## Common Problems & Solutions

### Problem 1: Graft Fails to Take

**Symptoms:** Scion dies, buds don't break, union doesn't callus.

**Causes:**
- Poor cambium alignment
- Graft dried out
- Incompatible stock/scion
- Scion dead at grafting time
- Wrong timing

**Solutions:**
- Usually too late - must regraft
- Learn from failure for next attempt

**Prevention:**
- Practice making matching cuts
- Keep grafts humid during healing
- Verify compatibility
- Use properly stored scion
- Graft at correct time

### Problem 2: Delayed Bud Break

**Symptoms:** Scion remains dormant long after other trees leaf out.

**Causes:**
- Slow healing at union
- Cold storage effects
- Weak scion
- Partial connection

**Solutions:**
- Patience if scion still green and viable
- Ensure watering adequate
- May catch up as season progresses

**Prevention:**
- Strong scions with good buds
- Adequate storage moisture
- Good cuts and cambium contact

### Problem 3: Scion Grew but Then Died

**Symptoms:** Scion starts well but fails mid-season.

**Causes:**
- Incomplete union (weak spot failed)
- Girdling by tape or pests
- Water stress
- Disease entering incomplete union

**Solutions:**
- Usually fatal - regraft next season
- Remove tape if girdling suspected
- Improve watering

**Prevention:**
- Complete cambium contact
- Remove tape on time
- Maintain water
- Seal all cuts

## Pro Tips

1. **The knife is everything** - A razor-sharp, single-bevel grafting knife makes this possible. Invest in quality and keep it sharp. Strop before each grafting session.

2. **Practice on dormant prunings** - Before grafting day, practice cuts on random dormant wood. Make matching pairs until they fit perfectly. Speed comes with practice.

3. **Work fast** - From first cut to wrapping should take 1-2 minutes maximum. Exposed cambium begins drying immediately. Have everything ready before making cuts.

4. **The tongue is for strength, cambium for healing** - The interlocking tongue holds pieces together while they heal, but it's the cambium contact that creates the actual union.

5. **Callusing chambers accelerate union** - Commercial growers use warm (15-20°C), humid callusing rooms after grafting. DIY version: plastic tub with damp moss in warm spot (not hot).

## Variations

### Simple Whip Graft

Same sloping cut, but without the tongue. Easier but weaker union. Good for beginners learning the cut angles before adding complexity.

### Saddle Graft

V-shaped cuts instead of sloping. Scion straddles rootstock. Strong union for softer wood or larger size differences.

### Omega Graft

Machine-made matching omega-shaped cuts. Very fast for production but requires specialised tool.
