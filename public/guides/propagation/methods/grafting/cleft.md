# Cleft Grafting

## Overview

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Advanced |
| **Success Rate** | 60-85% typical |
| **Time to Unite** | 3-6 weeks for callus |
| **Best Season** | Late winter (dormant) |
| **Equipment Needed** | Grafting knife, mallet, chisel or wedge, sealant, tape |

## What Is Cleft Grafting?

Cleft grafting is a technique where a split is made down the centre of a rootstock or branch, and one or two scion pieces are inserted into the cleft. This method is particularly useful when the rootstock is much larger in diameter than the scion - a situation where whip and tongue grafting isn't possible.

This is one of the oldest grafting techniques, used for centuries to top-work fruit trees (changing the variety of an established tree) or to graft onto thick rootstock. It's relatively simple compared to whip grafting because the cuts are straightforward, though getting good cambium contact requires careful positioning.

Cleft grafting is commonly used for renovating old fruit trees, converting a tree to a better variety, or when only thick rootstock is available.

## When to Use This Method

**Best for:**
- Top-working established trees (changing varieties)
- Large rootstock (2-10cm diameter)
- Multiple scions onto one rootstock
- Renovating old fruit trees
- When rootstock is much thicker than scion

**Not recommended for:**
- Matched diameters (use whip and tongue)
- Young, thin rootstock
- Very large limbs (over 10cm - consider bark grafting)
- Growing season (except some tropical)

## Step-by-Step Process

### Step 1: Timing

Graft in **late winter** (July-August in Australia) before growth begins.

**Ideal timing:**
- Rootstock still dormant
- Buds beginning to swell
- Before leaves emerge
- Stored scion dormant

**Why late winter:**
- Maximum cambium activity about to begin
- Scion remains dormant during healing
- Natural growth pushes once united
- Strong callusing response

**Key points:**
- Earlier is better for large clefts (more healing time)
- Don't wait until rootstock is actively growing
- Have scion ready and chilled

**Common mistakes:**
- Too late (rootstock leaves open, scion dries out)
- Scion no longer dormant

### Step 2: Preparing the Rootstock

Cut and prepare the stock for splitting.

**For top-working (established tree):**
1. Select limb to graft (2-10cm diameter)
2. Cut limb at right angle to growth
3. Make smooth, clean cut
4. Position should be accessible and allow good branch angle

**For bench grafting (rootstock before planting):**
1. Cut top of rootstock cleanly
2. Appropriate height above roots
3. Smooth horizontal cut

**Key points:**
- Clean cuts heal better
- Avoid splitting or ragged bark
- Position matters for tree structure

**Common mistakes:**
- Ragged cuts from dull tools
- Limb too large for good healing

### Step 3: Making the Cleft

Split the rootstock down the centre.

**Process:**
1. Position knife or chisel on centre of cut surface
2. Strike mallet firmly to split wood 5-8cm deep
3. Split should go through centre
4. Insert wedge or screwdriver to hold cleft open

**Cleft characteristics:**
- Depth: 5-8cm (proportional to diameter)
- Through exact centre
- Clean split (not twisted)
- Held open for scion insertion

**Key points:**
- Split on "quiet" side (no major branches below)
- Avoid splitting off bark (can happen with dull tools)
- Keep cleft wedged open while inserting scions

**Common mistakes:**
- Cleft too shallow (scions sit poorly)
- Split not centred (uneven cambium contact)
- Bark peeling off edge

### Step 4: Preparing Scions

Cut scions to fit the cleft.

**Scion preparation:**
1. Select scion pencil-thick (or to match cleft taper)
2. Make long wedge at base (5-8cm, matching cleft depth)
3. Wedge is asymmetrical: one side slightly thicker
4. Keep 3-4 buds above the wedge
5. Cut just above top bud

**The wedge shape:**
- Length: Matches cleft depth (5-8cm)
- Outer edge: Slightly thicker (this side outward)
- Inner edge: Thinner (this side toward centre)
- Base: Clean, chisel-shaped point

**Key points:**
- Thicker outer edge aligns cambium properly
- Both cut surfaces must be smooth
- Handle only by bark-covered parts

**Common mistakes:**
- Symmetrical wedge (cambium won't align)
- Wedge too short (poor contact)
- Touching cut surfaces

### Step 5: Inserting Scions

Position scions in the cleft for cambium contact.

**For two scions (standard method):**
1. Insert one scion at each edge of cleft
2. Position with thicker edge outward
3. Align cambium of scion with cambium of rootstock
4. Cambium is just under bark - not at bark surface

**Cambium alignment:**
- Scion cambium must contact rootstock cambium
- Usually means scion slightly inward from bark edge
- Thicker rootstock bark compensated by scion positioning

**For single scion:**
- Position at one edge of cleft
- Fill other side with sealant
- Smaller rootstock may only fit one

**Key points:**
- Cambium alignment is everything
- Slight inset from bark edge is correct
- Both sides of cleft can receive scions

**Common mistakes:**
- Scion bark flush with rootstock bark (cambiums don't meet)
- Not understanding where cambium actually is

### Step 6: Removing the Wedge

Let the cleft close around scions.

**Process:**
1. Once scions are positioned, remove holding wedge
2. Cleft wood springs closed
3. Scions held tightly in place
4. Verify scions haven't shifted

**Check points:**
- Scions still properly positioned?
- Cambium alignment maintained?
- Scions firmly held?

**Key points:**
- Tight grip from rootstock is the primary holder
- Don't wrap if grip is adequate
- Scions should not pull out easily

**Common mistakes:**
- Scions shift as wedge removed
- Not checking alignment after

### Step 7: Sealing the Graft

Completely cover all exposed surfaces.

**What to seal:**
- Top of rootstock (the entire cut surface)
- All around scions where they enter cleft
- Top cut of each scion
- Any cracks in bark
- The cleft split itself

**Sealing materials:**
- Grafting wax (traditional, effective)
- Wound sealant/pruning seal
- Grafting mastic
- Doc Farwell's compound

**Application:**
- Thick coating on all exposed surfaces
- Press into cracks and gaps
- Don't cover buds on scion
- Cover 100% of exposed wood

**Key points:**
- Sealing is MORE critical for cleft grafts than whip grafts
- Exposure causes drying and failure
- Check and reseal if cracks develop

**Common mistakes:**
- Incomplete coverage
- Sealing over buds
- Using sealant that cracks

### Step 8: Additional Support (Optional)

Secure scions if needed.

**When to wrap/support:**
- Scions not held firmly by cleft
- Large cleft on horizontal limb
- Windy locations
- Very thin scions

**Methods:**
- Stretch grafting tape around scions and stock
- Wide rubber bands
- Twine tied carefully
- Wire cages for bird protection

**Key points:**
- Often not necessary if cleft grips well
- Don't dislodge scions when wrapping
- Remove support as scion grows

**Common mistakes:**
- Dislodging scions while wrapping
- Leaving support too long (girdling)

### Step 9: Aftercare

Manage the graft through the first season.

**Monitoring:**
- Check sealant for cracks (reseal if needed)
- Watch for scion bud break (success sign)
- Remove rootstock suckers promptly
- Protect from birds and wind

**Sucker management:**
- Remove all rootstock suckers
- They divert energy from scion
- Keep rootstock foliage-free

**Watering:**
- Maintain normal watering
- Don't let tree dry out
- Good hydration aids healing

**Key points:**
- Suckers are the biggest threat to graft success
- Persistent sucker removal needed all season
- Sealant may need refreshing

**Common mistakes:**
- Letting suckers grow (graft fails)
- Neglecting to reseal cracked wax

### Step 10: First Season and Beyond

Manage new growth for tree development.

**As scions grow:**
- If both scions take, may remove weaker later
- Or train both for bushier tree
- Stake new growth if needed
- Pinch to encourage branching if desired

**If only one scion takes:**
- Perfectly acceptable result
- Reseal the failed scion's position
- Focus on successful scion

**Long-term:**
- Remove tape/ties before girdling
- Prune to develop good structure
- Union strengthens over years
- Eventually becomes invisible

**Key points:**
- Two scions is insurance, not requirement
- Healthy single scion makes fine tree
- Ongoing sucker removal may be needed

**Common mistakes:**
- Removing successful scion to keep failed one
- Forgetting to remove ties/tape

## Common Problems & Solutions

### Problem 1: Neither Scion Takes

**Symptoms:** Both scions fail, no bud break, scions dry out.

**Causes:**
- Poor cambium alignment
- Sealant cracked, grafts dried
- Dead or weak scionwood
- Incompatible rootstock/scion

**Solutions:**
- Cut back to healthy wood
- Regraft next season
- Try bark graft later in season

**Prevention:**
- Careful cambium positioning
- Complete, crack-free sealing
- Verified compatible combination
- Healthy scion, properly stored

### Problem 2: Sealant Cracks and Falls Off

**Symptoms:** Wax or sealant cracks, peels, or falls off exposing graft.

**Causes:**
- Hot sun on sealant
- Poor quality sealant
- Applied too thin
- Applied to wet surface

**Solutions:**
- Reseal immediately
- Apply thicker layer
- Cover with foil if sunny location

**Prevention:**
- Apply generously
- Use quality sealant
- Apply to dry surface
- Shade large grafts if needed

### Problem 3: One Scion Takes, One Fails

**Symptoms:** Only one of two scions survives.

**This is normal!**
- Very common outcome
- Single successful scion is fine
- Produces good tree

**Action:**
- Seal over failed scion position
- Focus care on successful graft
- Train as single-leader tree

## Best Applications

### Top-Working Established Trees

**Purpose:** Change variety of existing tree

**Process:**
1. Cut major limbs at 5-10cm diameter
2. Cleft graft each limb
3. New variety grows from grafts
4. Remove any remaining old growth
5. Tree structure maintained, variety changed

**Benefits:**
- Fast results (big root system feeds new growth)
- No need to start new tree
- Change to better variety

### Rescue Grafting

**Purpose:** Save damaged tree or add pollinator

**Applications:**
- Trunk damaged but roots alive
- Add pollinating variety to established tree
- Replace failed graft from previous year

### Multi-Variety Trees

**Purpose:** Several varieties on one tree

**Method:**
- Cleft graft different varieties on different limbs
- "Fruit salad" trees
- Pollination and variety in one tree

## Pro Tips

1. **Position the scion correctly** - The scion's thicker wedge side goes outward. This tilts the scion slightly inward, which is exactly right for cambium alignment. If in doubt, it's better to have the scion slightly inward from the bark edge.

2. **Seal like your graft depends on it** - Because it does. Cleft grafts expose more surface area than whip grafts. Check after one week, two weeks, and reseal any cracks. Hot sunny days cause expansion that cracks sealant.

3. **Two scions are insurance** - Put scions in both sides of the cleft. If both take, choose the best-positioned one to keep. If only one takes, you still succeed. Never rely on a single scion for an important graft.

4. **Match your scion diameter to the cleft** - Scion should be slightly thicker than the cleft opening when closed. Too thin = poor grip and contact. Too thick = won't fit. Often means selecting scions to match rootstock.

5. **The bigger the cut, the longer the healing** - Large clefts take longer to heal over. Expect 2-3 seasons before a large cleft is completely grown over. Keep monitoring and protecting.

## Variations

### Side Cleft

Insert scion into side of rootstock rather than top:
- Doesn't kill rootstock immediately
- Nurse limb above graft keeps sap flowing
- Remove nurse limb after graft established

### Wedge Graft

Similar to cleft but V-notch removed from rootstock:
- Better fit for some applications
- More controlled cleft shape
- Takes more time to prepare
