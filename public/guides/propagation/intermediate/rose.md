# Rose Propagation

## Quick Facts

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Intermediate |
| **Best Method** | Hardwood cuttings |
| **Alternative Methods** | Softwood cuttings, layering, budding |
| **Time to Root** | 3-6 months (hardwood), 4-6 weeks (softwood) |
| **Best Season** | Winter (hardwood), Summer (softwood) |
| **Success Rate** | 60-80% typical |
| **Rooting Hormone** | Required for best results |

## Overview

Roses have been propagated from cuttings for centuries, making it possible for gardeners to share favourite varieties without the cost of nursery plants. While commercial roses are often budded onto rootstock for vigour, own-root roses grown from cuttings are perfectly viable and often longer-lived.

The most reliable method for home gardeners is hardwood cuttings taken during winter dormancy. These require minimal attention and root slowly over winter and spring. Softwood cuttings in summer are faster but need more careful attention to humidity and water.

Success rates vary by rose type. Old garden roses, shrub roses, and species roses generally root more easily than modern hybrid teas and floribundas. However, with good technique, most roses can be propagated successfully.

## Best Uses for Propagated Plants

- Preserving heritage and old garden roses
- Sharing special varieties with friends
- Creating rose hedges economically
- Replacing lost or ailing plants
- Building collections of own-root roses

## Propagation Methods

### Hardwood Cuttings (Recommended)

**Overview:** Winter cuttings from dormant wood are the most forgiving method for rose propagation.

**When to take material:**
- Best months: June to August (winter)
- Time of day: Any time during dormancy
- Plant condition: Fully dormant, leaves fallen

**Step-by-step process:**

1. **Select material**
   - Choose pencil-thick stems from current year's growth
   - Select straight, healthy wood without disease
   - Each cutting 20-25cm long
   - Avoid very thin or very thick stems

2. **Prepare cutting**
   - Make sloping cut just above a bud at top
   - Make straight cut just below a bud at bottom
   - This helps identify which end is up
   - Remove any remaining leaves or soft tip growth

3. **Apply rooting hormone**
   - Use strong (hardwood strength) rooting hormone
   - Dip bottom 2-3cm of cutting
   - Tap off excess
   - Hormone is essential for roses

4. **Plant cutting**
   - Choose sheltered spot outdoors or use pots in cold frame
   - Insert 2/3 of cutting into ground
   - Space 15cm apart if doing multiples
   - Firm soil well around cuttings

5. **Provide environment**
   - Sheltered from harsh sun and wind
   - Well-drained soil or potting mix
   - No cover needed - winter chill is beneficial
   - Water if soil dries out

6. **Leave undisturbed**
   - Cuttings root slowly over winter and spring
   - Buds will swell and open in spring
   - Don't disturb to check - just wait
   - Callus and roots form underground

7. **Check for success**
   - By late spring, strong growth indicates success
   - Weak or no growth suggests failure
   - Gentle tug shows root resistance

8. **Grow on**
   - Leave in place for full growing season
   - Transplant following autumn or winter
   - Pot up if more convenient

### Softwood Cuttings (Alternative)

**When to use:** Summer propagation for faster results, though needs more attention.

**Process:**
1. Take 15cm cuttings from soft, new growth (November-December)
2. Remove all but top 2 leaves
3. Remove thorns from lower half
4. Dip in rooting hormone
5. Insert in moist propagation mix
6. Cover with plastic for humidity
7. Keep in bright shade, warm conditions
8. Roots develop in 4-6 weeks
9. Pot up once rooted

### Layering (Alternative)

**When to use:** For guaranteed success with minimal intervention.

**Process:**
1. Select a low, flexible cane
2. Bend to ground, wound where it touches soil
3. Bury wounded section 10cm deep
4. Pin in place with wire hoop
5. Leave 6-12 months
6. Sever from parent when well-rooted

## Timing & Seasonality

### Southern Hemisphere Calendar

| Season | Suitability | Notes |
|--------|-------------|-------|
| **Spring** (Sep-Nov) | Growth period | Monitor hardwood cuttings for growth |
| **Summer** (Dec-Feb) | Good | Softwood cuttings possible |
| **Autumn** (Mar-May) | Possible | Late semi-hardwood cuttings |
| **Winter** (Jun-Aug) | Best | Prime hardwood cutting season |

### Optimal Timing

- **Best window for hardwood:** June to August
- **Best window for softwood:** November to December
- **Avoid:** Spring (sap rising too vigorously), late summer (wood too hard)

## Rooting Environment

| Factor | Requirement |
|--------|-------------|
| **Temperature** | Ambient outdoor (hardwood), 18-22°C (softwood) |
| **Humidity** | Ambient (hardwood), high (softwood) |
| **Light** | Shade to part sun |
| **Medium** | Well-drained soil or propagation mix |
| **Container** | Direct ground or deep pots |

### Environment Setup

**Hardwood outdoors:**
Choose a sheltered spot with good drainage. North or east-facing is ideal (protected from harsh afternoon sun). Insert cuttings deeply and firm well.

**Softwood under cover:**
Use propagator or pots covered with clear plastic. Maintain high humidity. Vent daily. Keep in bright shade - direct sun will cook cuttings.

## Common Problems & Solutions

### Problem 1: Cuttings Die Over Winter

**Symptoms:** Cuttings turn black, stems shrivel, no spring growth.

**Causes:** Waterlogged soil, severe frost without protection, disease in cutting material.

**Solution:** Improve drainage next time, protect from extreme frost, select healthy wood.

**Prevention:** Well-drained spot, shallow mulch protection, disease-free source material.

### Problem 2: Cuttings Grow But Have No Roots

**Symptoms:** Leaves appear in spring but cutting pulls out easily with no roots.

**Causes:** Energy from stored carbohydrates produced leaves before roots developed.

**Solution:** Replace in soil, shade heavily, water well. May still root if not too late.

**Prevention:** Use hormone, don't disturb too early, allow full season for rooting.

### Problem 3: Rose Cuttings Get Disease

**Symptoms:** Black spot, rust, or mildew on cutting material.

**Causes:** Disease carried from parent plant or contracted in propagation.

**Solution:** Remove affected leaves, improve air circulation, fungicide if severe.

**Prevention:** Take cuttings from disease-free plants, good hygiene, adequate spacing.

## Pro Tips

1. **The "shoe test"** - Push cuttings into the ground beside a shed or garage on the south side. The building provides shelter and the foundation provides drainage. Check in spring.

2. **Heritage roses root best** - Old garden roses, species roses, and shrub roses root much more readily than modern hybrid teas. Start with easier types to build confidence.

3. **Take more than you need** - Rose cuttings have variable success rates. Take 3-4 cuttings for every plant you want, and you'll have spares to share.

4. **The potato method is a myth** - Despite internet claims, inserting cuttings into potatoes does not improve rooting. Use proper rooting hormone instead.

5. **Own-root vs grafted** - Cuttings produce "own-root" roses that may sucker true to variety. They're often longer-lived than grafted roses and won't produce rootstock suckers.

## Mother Plant Care

### Maintaining Stock Plants

For good cutting material:

- **Pruning regime:** Annual pruning produces strong new growth ideal for cuttings.
- **Feeding:** Regular rose fertiliser promotes healthy, disease-resistant growth.
- **Health monitoring:** Only propagate from disease-free plants. Treat black spot, rust, and mildew.
- **Watering:** Consistent water, especially during active growth.

### Selecting Mother Plants

Choose mother plants that display:

- Strong, healthy growth
- Disease resistance
- Desired flower form and fragrance
- Good garden performance
