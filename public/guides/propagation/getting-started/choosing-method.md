# Choosing a Propagation Method

Different plants respond best to different propagation methods. This guide helps you select the right approach.

---

## Quick Decision Tree

```
Is this a clumping plant that grows from multiple crowns?
  YES → Use Division

Is this a trailing or climbing plant?
  YES → Consider Layering (stays attached to parent)

Is this a grafted variety (fruit trees, roses)?
  YES → Maintain by Grafting (same method as original)

For everything else → Cuttings (most versatile)
```

---

## Method Overview

| Method | Difficulty | Speed | Best For |
|--------|-----------|-------|----------|
| **Softwood Cuttings** | Beginner | Fast (2-4 weeks) | Herbs, perennials, houseplants |
| **Semi-Hardwood Cuttings** | Intermediate | Medium (4-8 weeks) | Shrubs, some trees |
| **Hardwood Cuttings** | Intermediate | Slow (3-6 months) | Deciduous trees, roses, grape vines |
| **Leaf Cuttings** | Beginner | Medium (4-8 weeks) | Succulents, begonias, some houseplants |
| **Division** | Beginner | Instant | Perennials, grasses, clumping plants |
| **Simple Layering** | Beginner | Medium (3-6 months) | Climbers, low-branching shrubs |
| **Air Layering** | Intermediate | Medium (2-4 months) | Trees, plants with rigid stems |
| **Grafting** | Advanced | Fast (2-4 weeks) | Fruit trees, roses, specialist varieties |

## Cuttings

The most versatile and commonly used propagation method.

### Softwood Cuttings

**What they are:** Taken from new, green, flexible growth early in the growing season.

**Best timing:** Spring to early summer (September-December in Australia)

**Ideal candidates:**
- Herbs (basil, mint, oregano, rosemary)
- Perennials (salvia, lavender, geraniums)
- Houseplants (pothos, philodendron)
- Fuchsias, hydrangeas, deciduous azaleas

**Characteristics:**
- Soft, green stems that bend easily
- Active new growth
- Root quickly (2-4 weeks typically)
- Need high humidity (wilt easily)

**Pros:** Fast rooting, high success rate on suitable plants
**Cons:** Need constant humidity, wilting risk, short window for collection

---

### Semi-Hardwood Cuttings

**What they are:** Taken from current season's growth that has started to mature and firm up.

**Best timing:** Late summer to autumn (February-April in Australia)

**Ideal candidates:**
- Camellias, gardenias, azaleas
- Citrus, olive
- Evergreen shrubs
- Australian natives (grevilleas, banksias)

**Characteristics:**
- Stems flex but don't snap
- Leaves fully developed
- Some woodiness at base
- Root in 4-8 weeks typically

**Pros:** Longer collection window, less wilting than softwood
**Cons:** Slower than softwood, need more attention to humidity

---

### Hardwood Cuttings

**What they are:** Taken from fully dormant, woody growth during winter.

**Best timing:** Winter (June-August in Australia)

**Ideal candidates:**
- Roses
- Grape vines
- Figs, mulberries
- Deciduous fruit trees (some)
- Dogwood, willow, poplar

**Characteristics:**
- Woody, brown stems
- No leaves present
- Pencil-thickness stems
- Root slowly (3-6 months)

**Pros:** Very easy, low maintenance, can be done outdoors
**Cons:** Slow, need to wait for spring growth to confirm success

---

### Leaf Cuttings

**What they are:** Using leaves (whole or partial) to produce new plants.

**Best for:**
- Succulents (echeveria, sedum, crassula)
- Begonias (rex types)
- African violets
- Peperomias
- Snake plants (sansevieria)

**Types:**
- **Whole leaf with petiole:** African violet, peperomia
- **Whole leaf:** Most succulents
- **Leaf sections:** Begonia rex, snake plant
- **Leaf-bud cutting:** Includes a node (rubber plant)

**Pros:** Get many plants from one leaf, fascinating to watch
**Cons:** Slow, not all species work, some produce different foliage

---

### Root Cuttings

**What they are:** Sections of root that grow into new plants.

**Best for:**
- Raspberry, blackberry
- Japanese anemones
- Oriental poppies
- Horseradish
- Some trees (paulownia, robinia)

**When to take:** Winter dormancy (June-August)

**Pros:** Works for plants difficult to propagate otherwise
**Cons:** Need to disturb parent plant, limited species suitable

---

## Division

The quickest way to multiply clumping plants.

**What it is:** Physically separating a clump into multiple plants.

**Best timing:**
- Cool-season grasses: Autumn (March-May)
- Warm-season plants: Spring (September-November)
- Most perennials: Either season

**Ideal candidates:**
- Ornamental grasses
- Hostas, daylilies
- Iris, agapanthus
- Lemongrass, chives
- Most clumping perennials

**Process:**
1. Dig up entire clump (or portion)
2. Separate into sections with roots attached
3. Replant immediately at same depth
4. Water well and keep moist

**Pros:** Instant new plants, high success rate, rejuvenates parent
**Cons:** Limited to clumping plants, reduces parent size

---

## Layering

Rooting stems while still attached to the parent plant.

### Simple Layering

**What it is:** Bending a low branch to the ground and covering with soil until it roots.

**Best for:**
- Climbing roses
- Wisteria, jasmine
- Rhododendrons, azaleas
- Berries (especially tip layering for brambles)
- Magnolias

**Timing:** Spring or autumn when soil is moist

**Process:**
1. Select flexible low branch
2. Wound underside lightly (scrape bark)
3. Pin to ground in shallow trench
4. Cover with soil, leaving tip exposed
5. Keep moist until rooted (3-12 months)
6. Sever and transplant

**Pros:** No special equipment, high success, large new plants
**Cons:** Slow, need low branches, takes garden space

---

### Air Layering

**What it is:** Rooting a stem while attached to the plant, higher up where branches can't reach the ground.

**Best for:**
- Magnolias, camellias
- Rubber plants (ficus elastica)
- Dracaenas
- Citrus
- Large indoor plants

**Process:**
1. Select pencil-thick stem
2. Remove ring of bark (2-3cm wide)
3. Apply rooting hormone to exposed area
4. Wrap with moist sphagnum moss
5. Cover with plastic, seal ends
6. Wait for roots to fill moss (2-4 months)
7. Cut below roots and pot up

**Pros:** Get large plants quickly, works on difficult species
**Cons:** More complex setup, limits to where you can work

---

### Tip Layering

**What it is:** Burying the tip of an arching stem to produce a new plant.

**Best for:**
- Blackberries, raspberries
- Loganberries, boysenberries
- Some climbing roses

**Natural behaviour:** Many brambles do this automatically.

---

## Grafting

Joining parts of two plants together to combine their qualities.

### When Grafting is Necessary

- **Fruit trees:** Combine vigorous rootstock with desired variety
- **Roses:** Many named varieties are grafted
- **Difficult-to-root species:** Graft onto easy-rooting relatives
- **Dwarfing:** Use dwarfing rootstock to control size
- **Disease resistance:** Resistant rootstock, desired tops

### Common Types

**Whip and tongue:** Most common for fruit trees
**Cleft graft:** Simple, for thicker rootstock
**Bud graft:** Single bud inserted into rootstock (roses, citrus)
**Approach graft:** Two growing plants joined

**When to graft:**
- Whip/cleft: Late winter when dormant (July-August)
- Bud grafting: Late summer/early autumn (February-March)

**Pros:** Only way to reproduce some varieties exactly
**Cons:** Requires more skill, specific rootstock needed

---

## Seed Saving

Growing new plants from collected seeds.

**When appropriate:**
- Open-pollinated varieties (not hybrids)
- Species plants
- When genetic variation is acceptable/desired
- Building seed bank

**Not recommended for:**
- F1 hybrids (won't come true)
- Grafted varieties
- Plants that need clonal reproduction

**See method guide:** [Seed Saving](../methods/seed-saving.md)

## Choosing by Plant Type

### Herbs

| Herb | Best Method | Timing |
|------|-------------|--------|
| Basil | Softwood cuttings | Spring-summer |
| Rosemary | Semi-hardwood cuttings | Autumn |
| Mint | Division or cuttings | Spring |
| Thyme | Layering or cuttings | Spring-autumn |
| Lavender | Semi-hardwood cuttings | Autumn |
| Chives | Division | Spring or autumn |

### Fruit

| Fruit | Best Method | Notes |
|-------|-------------|-------|
| Citrus | Air layering or grafting | Cuttings possible but slow |
| Figs | Hardwood cuttings | Very easy |
| Grapes | Hardwood cuttings | Winter dormant wood |
| Berries | Division, tip layering | Species dependent |
| Stone fruit | Grafting | Need compatible rootstock |
| Pome fruit | Grafting | Need compatible rootstock |

### Houseplants

| Plant | Best Method | Notes |
|-------|-------------|-------|
| Pothos, philodendron | Stem cuttings | Easy, water or soil |
| Snake plant | Division or leaf cuttings | Leaf cuttings lose variegation |
| Succulents | Leaf or stem cuttings | Let callus first |
| Monstera | Stem cuttings (with node) | Need aerial root or node |
| Spider plant | Division or plantlets | Runners produce babies |
| Rubber plant | Air layering | For large plants |

### Australian Natives

| Plant | Best Method | Notes |
|-------|-------------|-------|
| Grevillea | Semi-hardwood cuttings | Autumn, good drainage essential |
| Banksia | Semi-hardwood cuttings | Challenging, low success rate |
| Westringia | Semi-hardwood cuttings | Relatively easy |
| Leptospermum | Semi-hardwood cuttings | Spring-autumn |
| Crowea | Semi-hardwood cuttings | Easy for beginners |
| Wattle (Acacia) | Seed | Most don't strike from cuttings |

## When You're Unsure

**Try cuttings first.** Most plants can be propagated from cuttings, even if it's not the fastest method.

**Start with easy species.** Build confidence before tackling difficult plants.

**Take more cuttings than you need.** Expect some failures, especially when learning.

**Observe the parent plant.** Plants often show you how they propagate naturally (runners, offsets, self-layering).

**Consult species-specific guides.** Each plant has optimal conditions for success.
