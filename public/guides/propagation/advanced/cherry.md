# Cherry Propagation

## Quick Facts

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Advanced |
| **Best Method** | Grafting (whip and tongue, chip budding) |
| **Alternative Methods** | Hardwood cuttings (limited success) |
| **Time to Root** | N/A (grafting), unreliable (cuttings) |
| **Best Season** | Late winter (grafting), late summer (budding) |
| **Success Rate** | 70-90% (grafting), 10-30% (cuttings) |
| **Rooting Hormone** | Essential for cuttings |

## Overview

Cherries are propagated by grafting onto selected rootstocks, following the same logic as apples: seeds don't come true, and rootstocks provide disease resistance and size control. However, cherries present additional challenges that move them firmly into advanced territory.

Cherry rootstocks have specific compatibility requirements. The cherry family (Prunus) includes sweet cherries, sour cherries, and ornamental flowering cherries, all with slightly different graft compatibilities. Getting the right rootstock for your variety and conditions is essential.

Timing is also more critical with cherries than apples. Cherries are prone to bacterial canker and gumming, especially when wounded during active growth. The dormant grafting window is narrower, and summer budding must be timed carefully.

The good news: once you understand these requirements, cherry grafting follows the same fundamental techniques as other stone fruit. The skills transfer well.

## Best Uses for Propagated Plants

- Creating trees on size-controlling rootstocks
- Preserving rare or heritage cherry varieties
- Converting ornamental cherries to fruiting
- Multi-variety trees for pollination
- Restoring old trees through top-working

## Propagation Methods

### Whip and Tongue Grafting (Recommended)

**Overview:** The standard method for bench grafting cherries in winter. Identical technique to apples.

**When to graft:**
- Best months: July to early August (late winter)
- Scion collection: June to July (fully dormant)
- Plant condition: Completely dormant, before any bud swell

**Step-by-step process:**

1. **Collect scion wood** (June-July)
   - One-year-old wood, pencil thickness
   - Healthy, well-budded shoots
   - Cut 20-30cm lengths
   - Store in plastic bag in refrigerator
   - Must remain fully dormant

2. **Prepare rootstock**
   - Bare-root rootstock, still dormant
   - Commonly Colt, Gisela series, or Mahaleb
   - Cut to 15-25cm above ground
   - Clean, smooth stem section

3. **Make matching cuts**
   - Long sloping cut (3-4cm) on rootstock
   - Identical cut on scion base
   - Tongue cuts 1/3 down from tip
   - Same technique as apple grafting

4. **Join and wrap**
   - Interlock tongues
   - Align cambium (critical)
   - Wrap firmly with grafting tape
   - Seal all exposed cuts with wax

5. **Plant or store**
   - Plant immediately or heel into moist medium
   - Protect from frost and desiccation
   - Keep cool until natural bud break

6. **Aftercare**
   - Remove wrapping after 3-4 months
   - Remove rootstock suckers promptly
   - Stake as needed

### Chip Budding (Alternative)

**When to use:** Late summer/autumn when bark is firm, or when bench grafting not possible.

**Best months:** February to April (late summer to autumn)

**Process:**
1. Select current season's budwood with mature buds
2. Remove chip from rootstock (matching notch)
3. Cut identical chip with bud from scion
4. Fit precisely, tape firmly
5. Bud remains dormant over winter
6. Remove rootstock above bud in late winter
7. Bud grows as new tree in spring

### T-Budding (Alternative)

**When to use:** Summer when bark slipping, less common with cherries.

**Best months:** December to February (midsummer)

**Process:**
1. Standard T-budding technique
2. Insert shield bud under bark flaps
3. Wrap, leaving bud exposed
4. Allow to overwinter dormant
5. Force growth following spring

### Hardwood Cuttings (Experimental)

**When to use:** Not recommended for sweet cherries. Some sour cherry varieties root from hardwood cuttings.

**Process:**
1. Collect dormant hardwood in winter
2. Treat with strong rooting hormone (IBA 3000-5000ppm)
3. Plant deeply in well-drained medium
4. Bottom heat helpful
5. Success rates 10-30% at best
6. Creates own-root trees without rootstock benefits

## Timing & Seasonality

### Southern Hemisphere Calendar

| Season | Suitability | Notes |
|--------|-------------|-------|
| **Spring** (Sep-Nov) | Avoid | Active growth, canker risk |
| **Summer** (Dec-Feb) | Budding only | T-budding when bark slipping |
| **Autumn** (Mar-May) | Chip budding | Bark firm, buds dormant |
| **Winter** (Jun-Aug) | Best for grafting | Dormant wood, lowest disease risk |

### Optimal Timing

- **Scion collection:** June-July (fully dormant, no bud swell)
- **Whip grafting:** July to early August
- **T-budding:** December-January (bark slipping)
- **Chip budding:** February-April
- **Avoid:** Any grafting during active spring growth

## Rooting Environment

| Factor | Requirement |
|--------|-------------|
| **Temperature** | Cool for storage, mild for healing |
| **Humidity** | Moderate around union |
| **Light** | Not critical during healing |
| **Medium** | Well-drained soil or healing bed |
| **Storage** | Refrigerator for scion (2-4°C) |

### Environment Setup

**Scion storage:**
Sealed plastic bag in refrigerator. Check for mould. Discard any that break dormancy.

**Graft healing:**
Plant in nursery row or heel into damp sand/sawdust. Protect from frost and desiccation. Good drainage essential - cherries hate wet feet.

## Common Problems & Solutions

### Problem 1: Bacterial Canker at Graft

**Symptoms:** Gummy amber exudate at graft site, bark discoloured, graft fails.

**Causes:** Grafting during wet weather, contaminated tools, susceptible rootstock.

**Solution:** Cut below infection if possible. Often fatal to graft.

**Prevention:** Graft in dry weather, sterilise tools, use resistant rootstocks, avoid wounding during active growth.

### Problem 2: Graft Incompatibility

**Symptoms:** Good initial growth, then decline over 1-3 years. Swelling at graft union, breakage.

**Causes:** Incompatible rootstock/scion combination (some ornamental × fruiting combinations problematic).

**Solution:** Cannot be fixed. Regraft onto compatible rootstock.

**Prevention:** Research compatibility before grafting. Stick to known compatible combinations.

### Problem 3: Gumming/Gummosis

**Symptoms:** Clear to amber gum exuding from wounds and graft sites.

**Causes:** Stress response to injury, bacterial or fungal infection, environmental stress.

**Solution:** Improve growing conditions, address underlying cause. Light gumming often not fatal.

**Prevention:** Graft when fully dormant, minimise wounds, maintain tree health, ensure good drainage.

## Pro Tips

1. **Dormancy is everything** - More than any other fruit tree, cherries must be grafted fully dormant. Even slight bud swell increases failure rates. Collect scion early and keep refrigerated.

2. **Rootstock matters more** - Colt is vigorous but widely compatible. Gisela series offers size control but needs good soil. Mahaleb suits dry conditions. Match rootstock to your site.

3. **Ornamental cherry compatibility** - Most ornamental flowering cherries (Prunus serrulata) can accept sweet cherry scions, but check specific combinations. Some make beautiful dual-purpose trees.

4. **The 'bleeding' cherry myth** - Cherries don't actually bleed sap like birches or maples. The gumming is a stress response, not sap loss. Still, avoid unnecessary wounds.

5. **Top-working old trees** - Large established cherry trees can be top-worked to new varieties. Framework grafting spreads risk across multiple graft points. Dramatic size reduction in one season isn't advisable.

## Mother Plant Care

### Maintaining Scion Sources

Healthy mother trees provide the best grafting material:

- **Disease management:** Bacterial canker is the primary concern. Copper sprays in autumn help.
- **Pruning timing:** Prune only in summer when wounds heal fastest and infection risk lowest.
- **Scion selection:** One-year wood from healthy, productive branches. Avoid water sprouts.
- **Variety confirmation:** Many cherries look similar. Confirm identity before propagating.

### Selecting Scion Wood

Choose material that displays:

- One-year-old wood, pencil thickness
- Plump, healthy buds
- No signs of canker or gumming
- From productive, true-to-type trees
