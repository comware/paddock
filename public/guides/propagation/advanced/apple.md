## Quick Facts

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Advanced |
| **Best Method** | Grafting (whip and tongue) |
| **Alternative Methods** | Budding, hardwood cuttings (rare) |
| **Time to Root** | N/A (grafting), 6-12 months (cuttings) |
| **Best Season** | Late winter (grafting), summer (budding) |
| **Success Rate** | 80-95% (grafting), 10-30% (cuttings) |
| **Rooting Hormone** | Not needed for grafting |

## Overview

Apples are almost exclusively propagated by grafting, and this has been the case for centuries. The reason is simple: apple seeds don't grow true to type. A seed from a Granny Smith will produce an apple tree, but the fruit will be completely different - usually inferior - to its parent. Every named apple variety is a clone, maintained through vegetative propagation.

Grafting joins a scion (cutting from desired variety) to a rootstock (selected for size control, disease resistance, and soil adaptation). This combination is what makes modern apple growing possible - imagine if every orchard tree grew to 10 metres tall!

The techniques here focus on winter grafting (bench grafting) using whip and tongue joins, the most common method for apple propagation. Summer budding is an alternative when scion wood isn't available in winter.

## Best Uses for Propagated Plants

- Creating trees on size-controlling rootstocks
- Preserving heritage or rare varieties
- Multi-variety trees for small gardens
- Replacing lost varieties from scion wood
- Understanding fruit tree management

## Propagation Methods

### Whip and Tongue Grafting (Recommended)

**Overview:** Join scion and rootstock of similar diameter with interlocking cuts. Very strong union.

**When to graft:**
- Best months: July to August (late winter, before bud break)
- Scion collection: June to July (fully dormant)
- Plant condition: Dormant scion, rootstock just beginning to wake

**Step-by-step process:**

1. **Collect scion wood** (earlier in winter)
   - Select pencil-thick one-year-old wood
   - Straight, healthy, well-budded shoots
   - Cut 20-30cm lengths
   - Bundle, label, store in plastic bag in refrigerator
   - Keep moist but not wet

2. **Prepare rootstock**
   - Bare-root rootstock, dormant or just waking
   - Cut to desired height (usually 15-25cm above ground)
   - Clean stem section for grafting

3. **Make rootstock cut**
   - Long sloping cut (3-4cm) on one side
   - Smooth, single knife stroke
   - Cut should be flat, not curved

4. **Make tongue cut in rootstock**
   - Starting 1/3 down from tip of slope
   - Cut downward into wood (1-1.5cm)
   - Creates a "tongue" that will interlock

5. **Prepare scion**
   - Match diameter to rootstock as closely as possible
   - Make identical sloping cut on scion base
   - Make identical tongue cut
   - Keep 3-4 buds on scion

6. **Join scion to rootstock**
   - Slide tongues together, interlocking
   - Cambium layers must align on at least one side
   - If diameters differ, align one side perfectly

7. **Wrap the graft**
   - Wrap firmly with grafting tape or parafilm
   - Cover all cut surfaces
   - Scion buds can be left exposed or covered (tape stretches)

8. **Seal exposed cuts**
   - Apply grafting wax or sealant to top of scion
   - Prevents desiccation

9. **Plant or heel in**
   - Plant grafted trees with union above soil level
   - Or heel into damp sawdust until planting time
   - Keep cool but frost-free

10. **Aftercare**
    - Remove wrapping once union healed (3-4 months)
    - Remove any rootstock shoots
    - Stake as needed

### T-Budding (Alternative - Summer)

**When to use:** When scion wood unavailable in winter, or for field budding onto established rootstocks.

**Process:**
1. July to August, when bark slipping
2. Collect budwood from current season's growth
3. T-bud as described in citrus guide
4. Allow bud to overwinter dormant
5. Cut rootstock above bud in late winter
6. Bud grows as new tree in spring

### Chip Budding (Alternative - Any Season)

**When to use:** When bark won't slip for T-budding, or for winter budding.

**Process:**
1. Remove chip from rootstock (bud-sized notch)
2. Match with identical chip containing bud from scion
3. Tape firmly
4. Works when bark isn't slipping

## Timing & Seasonality

### Southern Hemisphere Calendar

| Season | Suitability | Notes |
|--------|-------------|-------|
| **Spring** (Sep-Nov) | Avoid grafting | Active growth too advanced |
| **Summer** (Dec-Feb) | Budding possible | Bark slipping for T-buds |
| **Autumn** (Mar-May) | Chip budding | Collect scion wood late |
| **Winter** (Jun-Aug) | Best for grafting | Dormant wood, bare-root rootstocks |

### Optimal Timing

- **Scion collection:** June-July (fully dormant, before any bud swell)
- **Bench grafting:** July-August (just before rootstocks begin growing)
- **T-budding:** December-February (bark slipping freely)
- **Chip budding:** Year-round (doesn't require bark slip)

## Rooting Environment

| Factor | Requirement |
|--------|-------------|
| **Temperature** | Cool for storage, mild for healing |
| **Humidity** | High around graft union |
| **Light** | Not critical during healing |
| **Medium** | Moist sawdust or soil for healing |
| **Storage** | Refrigerator for scion (2-4°C) |

### Environment Setup

**Scion storage:**
Plastic bag in refrigerator vegetable crisper. Check monthly for mould. Slightly moist, not wet.

**Graft healing:**
Plant out or heel into moist sawdust. Protect from frost and drying wind. Keep union moist but not waterlogged.

## Common Problems & Solutions

### Problem 1: Graft Doesn't Take

**Symptoms:** Scion dries out, buds don't swell in spring, bark separates from wood.

**Causes:** Poor cambium contact, scion dried out, grafted too late in season, incompatible rootstock (rare with apples).

**Solution:** Remove failed scion, try again next season if rootstock alive.

**Prevention:** Match diameters, align cambium, wrap tightly, seal exposed cuts, graft while dormant.

### Problem 2: Scion Grows Then Dies

**Symptoms:** Initial spring growth, then sudden wilting and death.

**Causes:** Incomplete vascular connection, scion exhausted reserves before union completed.

**Solution:** Usually too late. Rootstock may resprout for future grafting.

**Prevention:** Strong graft technique, whip and tongue provides best connection, don't remove wrapping too early.

### Problem 3: Rootstock Suckers Dominate

**Symptoms:** Vigorous growth from below graft, scion weak or dying.

**Causes:** Rootstock more vigorous, competition for resources, suckers not removed promptly.

**Solution:** Remove all suckers immediately when seen. Cut, don't pull.

**Prevention:** Regular inspection, prompt sucker removal, ensure scion is growing well.

## Pro Tips

1. **Match diameters precisely** - The most critical factor in grafting success is cambium alignment. Using scion and rootstock of identical diameter means cambium aligns all around, dramatically improving success rates.

2. **Collect more scion than needed** - Scion wood is free, rootstocks cost money. Collect plenty of scion in winter and store in the refrigerator. Healthy stored scion can last 3-4 months.

3. **Label everything obsessively** - An unlabeled scion is a mystery tree. Use permanent marker on tape wrapped around bundles, and keep written records.

4. **Rootstock determines tree size** - M27 for tiny trees, M9 for dwarf, M26 for semi-dwarf, MM106 for semi-vigorous, MM111 for vigorous. Choose based on your space and management style.

5. **Family trees for small gardens** - Graft multiple varieties onto one rootstock. Ensures cross-pollination and variety in minimal space. Match vigour levels for balanced growth.

## Mother Plant Care

### Maintaining Scion Sources

Healthy mother trees provide the best scion:

- **Virus-free status:** Source from certified nurseries when possible.
- **Pruning regime:** Good pruning produces ideal scion wood - vigorous one-year shoots.
- **Nutrition:** Balanced feeding. Avoid excess nitrogen (soft growth).
- **Identification:** Know your varieties. Mislabeling propagates errors.

### Selecting Scion Wood

Choose material that displays:

- One-year-old wood (current season's hardened growth)
- Pencil thickness (5-8mm diameter)
- Healthy, plump buds
- No disease symptoms (canker, mildew)
