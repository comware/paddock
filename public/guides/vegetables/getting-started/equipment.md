# Equipment

Growing vegetables needs far less equipment than the shops would like you to believe. This
page separates what you actually need to sow your first bed from what is worth adding once
you know you are continuing.

Prices are rough Australian retail and are there to give a sense of scale, not to be quoted
back at anyone.

## The starter kit

Nine things. Everything in **Your First Bed** is achievable with this list and nothing else.

### 1. A spade or garden fork

For breaking up the bed and turning compost in. A fork is the better single choice on heavy
or stony ground because it loosens without lifting and turning everything. Buy one with a
solid socket where the handle meets the head -- that joint is where cheap tools fail. About
$40 to $80, and it will outlast you.

### 2. A rake

For levelling the bed and breaking clods down to a fine surface. Fine-seeded crops sown at
3 to 5 mm need real contact with soil, and a cloddy surface is why they come up patchy. A
rigid steel garden rake, not a springy leaf rake. About $25.

### 3. A probe thermometer

The most under-bought tool in vegetable growing and the one that most improves results. Every
crop guide here leads with a soil temperature range and you cannot use any of them without
it. A kitchen probe thermometer is fine. About $10 to $20.

### 4. A watering can with a rose, or a hose with a gentle spray

The rose -- the perforated head -- is the point. A hard stream washes seed out of a bed you
have just sown and knocks small seedlings flat. About $20 for a can, $30 for a decent hose
fitting.

### 5. Seed

Buy from a supplier that dates its packets and choose varieties suited to your season. Seed
is the cheapest input in this whole exercise and a fresh packet of a suitable variety
outperforms bargain seed of the wrong one. Two or three dollars a packet, and one packet is
several seasons of a succession crop.

### 6. Compost or aged manure

The one input that does the most work. You want enough for five to ten centimetres over the
bed. Bagged compost from a nursery is convenient; bulk delivery is far cheaper if you have
somewhere to put it. Homemade is free and takes six months to start.

### 7. A hand trowel

For planting seedlings and small transplanting jobs. About $15.

### 8. Secateurs or garden snips

For harvesting and for thinning at ground level. Cut-and-come-again harvesting is done with
these, not by pulling. Snips are more useful than full-size secateurs for vegetable work.
About $20 to $40.

### 9. Labels and a pencil

Genuinely necessary, not a nicety. Two rows of brassica seedlings are indistinguishable at
two weeks, and a succession bed has three sowing dates in it. Pencil rather than marker --
marker fades to nothing in a season of UV. A few dollars.

**Starter kit total: roughly $150 to $250**, and most of it is bought once.

## Worth adding in the second season

**A hoe.** Once you have more than one bed, weeding by hand stops being viable. A hoe used
weekly on weeds that are barely visible is a fraction of the work of pulling established
ones.

**Shade cloth.** Thirty to fifty per cent shade cloth over a summer bed extends the useful
season of leafy crops considerably by taking the edge off the heat that triggers bolting.

**Insect netting.** The single most effective answer to cabbage white butterfly on
brassicas, and it is physical rather than chemical. Several crop guides here recommend it by
name for leaf miner and cabbage moth.

**Seed-raising trays and mix.** Only needed once you are growing the transplanted crops --
tomato, capsicum, brassicas, leek. Seed-raising mix is a genuinely different product from
potting mix: finer, lower in nutrient, and it matters for germination.

**A soil pH test kit.** Most vegetables want a pH of roughly 6.0 to 7.0. A cheap kit is
about $20 and is worth doing once per bed, because a pH problem looks exactly like a
nutrient problem and gets treated with fertiliser that cannot work.

**A compost bay.** Turns garden and kitchen waste into the amendment you would otherwise
buy every season.

## What you do not need

**A rotary hoe or tiller.** It destroys soil structure, chops perennial weed roots into
hundreds of new plants, and no bed described in this library is large enough to justify one.

**Complete fertiliser for a first bed.** Compost worked in before planting covers a first
season for nearly everything here. Feed during the crop only for the heavy feeders the
guides identify by name.

**Seedling heat mats and grow lights.** Real tools for raising transplants out of season,
irrelevant to a direct-sown first bed.

**Anything sold as a kit.** Vegetable starter kits reliably cost more than the same items
bought separately and include things you will not use.

## A note on seed quality

Seed viability drops with age and with poor storage, and "the seed was bad" is the most
commonly reached and least commonly correct diagnosis when a sowing fails. In practice, cold
soil, wrong depth and a dried-out surface account for far more failures.

Store seed cool, dark and dry -- a sealed container in the fridge is ideal. Most vegetable
seed keeps three to five years that way; parsnip and onion are the notable exceptions and
are best bought fresh each year.
