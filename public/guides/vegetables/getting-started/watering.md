# Watering

Watering is the habit that decides most outcomes in a vegetable bed. It is also where the
most confident wrong advice circulates, because "water every day" is memorable and mostly
wrong.

## The rule

**Water deeply and less often, at the roots, in the morning.**

Every part of that sentence is doing work.

**Deeply, less often.** A light daily sprinkle wets the top centimetre and evaporates. Roots
grow toward water, so shallow watering produces a shallow root system that cannot cope with
a hot week. A long soak twice a week drives roots down and makes the plant far more
resilient than the same volume of water applied in seven small doses.

**At the roots.** Wet leaves overnight are how most fungal problems start. Water the soil,
not the plant.

**In the morning.** The bed has the whole day to draw the water down, and any splash on
foliage dries within the hour. Evening watering leaves foliage wet through the coldest,
dampest part of the night.

## The exception: seed and seedlings

The rule above is for established plants. Between sowing and emergence, it inverts.

Seed needs the surface consistently damp to germinate, and a seed that starts to germinate
and then dries out is dead -- there is no second attempt. During that window, light and
frequent is correct: a gentle misting once or twice a day, enough to keep the top centimetre
from drying, not enough to puddle.

This matters most for the shallow-sown crops. Rocket at 5 mm, lettuce at 3 mm and corn salad
at 5 mm are sitting in the layer that dries fastest. Broad bean at 40 mm is far more
forgiving.

Switch to deep-and-infrequent once seedlings have their first true leaves.

## How to tell if it needs water

**Push a finger in.** Down to the second knuckle, roughly five centimetres. Damp at that
depth means leave it alone. Dry means water. This is more reliable than anything you can see
from standing up, and it is the whole technique.

**Do not diagnose from wilting.** Many crops wilt in the afternoon heat of a hot day and
recover on their own by evening with no water applied. Wilting that is still there in the
morning is a real signal. Watering a plant that was merely hot is how beds get waterlogged.

**Watch the soil colour, but do not trust it alone.** A surface can look bone dry over soil
that is perfectly damp two centimetres down, particularly on a mulched bed.

## Roughly how much, roughly how often

Adjust to your own weather and soil -- these are starting points, not prescriptions.

| Conditions | Established bed |
|---|---|
| Mild spring or autumn | A deep soak once or twice a week |
| Hot, dry summer | A deep soak every second day |
| Cool winter | Often nothing; rainfall usually covers it |
| Sandy soil | More often, less each time |
| Heavy clay | Less often, more each time, and watch for pooling |

A deep soak means wetting the soil to about fifteen centimetres. The first time, water for
a while and then dig a small hole at the edge of the bed to see how far it actually got --
almost everyone underestimates this, usually by half.

## Consistency, and why bolting is a watering problem

Several crop guides here name water stress as a bolting trigger, alongside heat and day
length. Radish in particular runs to seed when stressed by dryness at any point in its
short life.

The mechanism is simple: a plant that dries out and is then rescued has been told the season
is ending, and its response is to set seed. This is why *consistency* matters more than
volume. A bed watered thoroughly every third day does better than one watered heavily,
forgotten for a week, and then rescued.

If you are going away, the answer is mulch and a slow soak before you leave, not a deluge on
your return.

## Mulch

A layer of straw, sugarcane mulch or similar over the bed surface -- three to five
centimetres, kept clear of plant stems -- does more for water consistency than any change to
your watering schedule. It slows evaporation dramatically, keeps soil temperature steadier,
and suppresses the weeds that would otherwise compete for the same water.

Two cautions. Keep it off stems, where trapped moisture causes rot. And do not mulch over a
freshly sown row -- wait until seedlings are up and have their first true leaves, or you
will simply prevent emergence.

## Common watering mistakes

**Daily light sprinkling.** The most common error. Produces shallow roots and plants that
collapse in the first hot spell.

**Watering in the evening.** Leaves foliage wet all night. Morning is better in almost every
case.

**Watering the leaves.** The water needs to reach soil. Overhead sprinklers lose a lot to
evaporation and wet exactly what you would rather keep dry.

**Watering on a schedule regardless of weather.** Three days of rain does not need
supplementing. Check with a finger first, every time.

**Hard spray on a seedbed.** Washes seed out of position and out of the bed entirely. Use a
rose or a gentle spray until plants are established.

**Assuming rain was enough.** A light shower often wets only the surface. Check.
