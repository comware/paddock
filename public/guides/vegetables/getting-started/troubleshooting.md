# Troubleshooting

The problems below are the ones that actually recur across the 59 crop guides in this
library, ordered roughly by how often they turn up. Each crop guide has its own
"Common Problems" section with specifics; this page is the general version, for when you do
not yet know which crop is at fault.

Start with the diagnosis table, then read the section it points you to.

## Quick diagnosis

## 1. Bolting

**The most common failure in this library** -- it appears in more crop guides than any other
problem.

The plant stops making leaves and sends up a flower stalk. Leaves turn bitter and tough
within days. Once it has started, it cannot be reversed.

**Causes.** Heat, lengthening days, and stress -- most often water stress. Some crops also
bolt after a cold snap followed by warmth, which is why beetroot and chard occasionally run
to seed in spring.

**What to do now.** Harvest whatever is still usable immediately; quality drops fast. Then
pull the plant and resow. Waiting for it to recover wastes the bed.

**Preventing the next one.**

- **Sow in the right season.** This is most of the answer. Spinach bolts within weeks of
  emerging from a late sowing -- its guide says to treat summer as off-limits, and it means
  it. Check the crop's "When to Sow" section rather than the germination range.
- **Water consistently.** A plant that dries out and is then rescued has been told the
  season is ending. Consistency matters more than volume.
- **Harvest promptly.** Many crops bolt because they were left standing past maturity.
- **Use shade cloth in summer** for anything leafy you are pushing into the warm months.

## 2. Caterpillars and cabbage white butterfly

The most-named pest in the library, and almost entirely a brassica problem -- cabbage,
broccoli, cauliflower, kale, collards, mizuna, pak choi, rocket, radish tops.

**What you see.** Ragged holes chewed through leaves, dark green pellets of frass, and small
velvety green caterpillars that sit along the leaf veins and are genuinely hard to spot.
White butterflies flitting over the bed are the adults, laying.

**What to do now.** Check the undersides of leaves and pick caterpillars off by hand -- on a
small bed this is completely effective and takes a few minutes. Look along the midrib, which
is where they sit.

**Preventing the next one.** Insect netting over the bed from the day you sow. It is
physical, it needs no repeating, and several crop guides recommend it by name. The netting
must actually exclude the butterfly -- laid over hoops and pinned down at the edges, not
draped on the plants, since she will lay through fabric resting on a leaf.

## 3. Poor or patchy germination

**Check these in order.** The seed is almost never the problem, and is almost always blamed.

**Soil temperature.** The first thing to check, every time. Seed sown below its range
germinates slowly and unevenly, or not at all. Carrot takes about three weeks at 8C and
about one at 20C -- same seed, same bed. Take a morning reading at sowing depth and compare
it against the crop guide.

**Depth.** Too deep is the usual error. The seedling exhausts its reserves before reaching
light and dies underground, silently. Fine seed at 3 to 5 mm is unforgiving of a heavy
covering.

**Surface moisture.** A seed that begins to germinate and then dries out is dead. The top
centimetre must stay damp continuously from sowing until emergence -- this is the one time
light, frequent watering is correct.

**Patience.** Some crops are simply slow. Corn salad takes two to three weeks in cold soil
and its guide is explicit that this is normal rather than a failure. Resowing too early is a
common mistake.

**Seed age.** Last on the list for a reason. Most vegetable seed keeps three to five years
stored cool and dry; parsnip and onion are the exceptions and are worth buying fresh.

## 4. Slugs and snails

**What you see.** Seedlings gone entirely overnight, or irregular holes with smooth edges
rather than the ragged chewing of a caterpillar. Silvery trails on soil or leaves in the
morning confirm it.

**What to do now.** They feed at night and in wet weather. Go out after dark with a torch
and collect them -- unglamorous and by far the most effective response on a small bed. Clear
hiding places: boards, pots, dense weeds along the edges.

**Preventing the next one.** Keep mulch pulled back from the stems of young seedlings, water
in the morning rather than the evening so the bed surface is drier overnight, and protect
newly emerged rows in particular. Established plants shrug off damage that kills seedlings.

## 5. Bitter or unpleasantly strong leaves

**Causes, in order of likelihood.**

**Left too long.** Most leafy crops get tougher and stronger the longer they stand. Several
guides here say plainly that leaves harvested at a reasonable size taste better than large
ones from the same plant.

**Heat.** Rocket grown in summer is peppery to the point of being better cooked than eaten
raw -- its guide says so directly. Mustard greens from a hot dry planting are hotter and
more fibrous. This is a flavour cost of the season, not a fault in your growing.

**Water or nutrient stress.** An under-watered or under-fed plant produces tougher, stronger
leaves.

**Bolting.** If a flower stalk has appeared, bitterness is a symptom, not the problem. See
section 1.

**What to do.** Harvest younger and more often. Sow heat-sensitive greens in autumn rather
than pushing them through summer.

## 6. Forked, split, woody or stunted roots

Carrot, parsnip, radish, beetroot, turnip, swede, daikon.

**Forked or twisted roots.** The taproot hit something -- a stone, a clod, a lump of fresh
manure -- or was disturbed while forming. Prepare a fine, stone-free bed to depth before
sowing, and do not transplant root crops. This is why every root crop here is marked
`direct`.

**Thin, weak roots with lots of top growth.** Almost always failure to thin. Plants left at
sowing density compete and none of them bulk up. Thin early, to the spacing in the guide,
snipping at ground level rather than pulling.

**Split or cracked roots.** Irregular watering. The root grows slowly through a dry spell,
then takes up water rapidly when finally watered and splits its own skin. Consistency is the
fix.

**Woody, tough or pungent roots.** Left in the ground too long, or checked by drought during
bulking. Harvest at the size the guide gives rather than waiting for something bigger.

## 7. Aphids

**What you see.** Clusters of small green, grey or black insects on growing tips and leaf
undersides. Curled or distorted new growth. A sticky residue, sometimes with black sooty
mould growing on it. Ants running up and down the stems -- they farm aphids and their
presence is a reliable tell.

**What to do now.** A firm jet of water knocks them off and is usually enough on a small
scale. Squash colonies by hand. Badly infested growing tips can be pinched out entirely.

**Preventing the next one.** Aphids concentrate on soft, sappy growth, so avoid
over-feeding with nitrogen. Ladybirds, hoverflies and lacewings will do most of the work if
you have not sprayed them out -- flowering herbs like dill and coriander left to flower at
the bed edge bring them in.

## 8. Yellowing leaves

**Lower leaves yellowing first, plant otherwise growing.** Usually nitrogen -- the plant is
moving it from old leaves to new. Top-dress with compost or a liquid feed. Most likely on
the heavy feeders the guides name.

**Yellowing with soggy soil.** Waterlogging. Roots cannot breathe, and the symptom looks
like starvation because the plant cannot take up nutrients. Stop watering and check
drainage. See **Beds and Soil**.

**Yellowing between the veins, veins staying green.** A trace element issue, and often a pH
problem rather than an absence of the nutrient. Worth a pH test before adding anything.

**Oldest leaves only, on a mature plant.** Often just age. If new growth is healthy, ignore
it.

---

## 9. Powdery mildew

**What you see.** White or grey powdery coating on leaf surfaces, usually late in the
season. Most common on cucurbits -- zucchini, pumpkin, cucumber -- and on peas.

**What to do now.** Remove and dispose of the worst-affected leaves, do not compost them.
Improve air movement by thinning crowded growth.

**Preventing the next one.** Space plants at the distances the guides give; crowding is the
main contributor because it stops air moving. Water at the roots in the morning rather than
over the foliage in the evening. Accept that on a late-season cucurbit it is often simply
the end of the crop rather than a rescuable problem.

---

## 10. Rot at soil level

**Seed rotting before emergence.** Cold, wet soil. Bean seed in particular rots rather than
germinating if sown before the soil has reached temperature -- bush bean wants 16C and its
guide is explicit that early sowing means rot rather than slow germination.

**Seedlings collapsing at the base after emerging.** Damping off, a fungal problem of wet,
crowded, poorly ventilated conditions. There is no cure for affected seedlings. Sow more
thinly, water less, improve air movement.

**Established plants rotting at the crown.** Waterlogging, or mulch heaped against stems.
Pull mulch back a few centimetres from every stem. Corn salad in heavy winter soil is
specifically prone to this and its guide says to improve drainage before sowing.

---

## 11. Tall, pale, floppy seedlings

Leggy growth. The seedling has stretched toward insufficient light.

**On a windowsill.** Almost always the cause. A windowsill is far darker than open sky, and
seedlings raised there stretch. Move them outside into full light as soon as conditions
allow, or use a grow light.

**In the bed.** Sown too thickly, so seedlings shade each other. Thin promptly.

Leggy seedlings do not recover their compactness. Some -- tomato in particular -- can be
planted deeper to compensate. Most cannot. Start again.

Open the crop's own guide from the library. Every one has a **Common Problems** section
written around the specific failures of that crop, and it is far more precise than anything
on this page.
