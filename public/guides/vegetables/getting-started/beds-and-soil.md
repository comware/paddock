# Beds and Soil

A vegetable bed is the one part of this that is genuinely hard to fix later. Seed is cheap
and a failed sowing costs you six weeks; a badly sited or badly drained bed costs you every
crop you put in it, for years, and you will blame the seed each time.

This page is worth doing properly once.

## Siting: three things, in order

**Sun.** Most vegetables want six hours of direct sun a day, and fruiting crops -- tomato,
capsicum, zucchini, beans -- want more. Leafy crops and herbs will accept four and are the
right choice for a part-shaded spot. Watch the space across a full day before you commit;
the shade you have in June is not the shade you have in December.

**Drainage.** Water that pools after rain will rot seed and kill roots, and it is the
failure mode behind a surprising number of "the seed was bad" conclusions. Test it: dig a
hole the depth of a spade, fill it with water, and let it drain. Fill it again and time the
second drain. Under four hours is fine. Over twelve and you should be building a raised bed
rather than digging into what you have.

**Water access.** A bed you have to carry water to is a bed that gets watered
inconsistently, and inconsistent watering is the single biggest cause of bolting. Within
hose reach is not a luxury.

## Size: start much smaller than you want to

A first bed of about 1.2 metres by 2.5 metres is plenty. It is enough for the two or three
crops in **Your First Bed** plus a succession, and small enough that you will actually keep
up with the weeding and thinning.

The 1.2 metre width matters more than the length. It is roughly two arm-lengths, so you can
reach the middle from either side without standing on the bed. Compacted soil is a real
problem and the fastest way to create it is walking on your own bed to reach the centre.

Beginners almost always build too big, lose control of it in the first eight weeks, and
conclude they are bad at gardening. Build small, succeed, extend next season.

## Raised bed or in-ground?

**Build a raised bed if** your drainage test failed, your soil is heavy clay or builder's
rubble, you cannot easily bend to ground level, or you are on a site where contamination is
a possibility. Twenty to thirty centimetres of height is enough for everything in this
library except parsnip and daikon, which want closer to forty.

**Dig in-ground if** the drainage test passed and the soil is workable. It is cheaper,
holds moisture far better through summer, and lets roots go as deep as they want.

Neither is better in the abstract. Raised beds are frequently built by people whose ground
was fine, at real cost, and then need watering twice as often all summer.

## Preparing the bed

**Clear it.** Remove weeds roots and all, especially anything running -- couch and kikuyu
will come back through a new bed within a season and are far easier to deal with now.

**Loosen, do not pulverise.** Break the soil up to about a spade's depth so roots and water
can move through it. Turning soil over and over destroys the structure that holds water and
air, and leaves you with something that sets hard after the first heavy rain.

**Add compost.** This is the step that does most of the work. Spread five to ten centimetres
of compost or well-aged manure over the bed and work it into the top fifteen. It feeds the
crop, it helps sandy soil hold water, and it helps clay soil drain -- the same amendment
fixes opposite problems.

"Well-aged" matters. Fresh manure will burn seedlings and can carry weed seed; it wants six
months or more, until it is dark, crumbly and smells of soil rather than of anything else.

**Rake it level and fine.** Seed needs contact with soil, and a surface full of clods leaves
small seed sitting in air pockets where it dries out and dies. This matters most for the
fine-seeded crops -- rocket and corn salad at 5 mm depth, lettuce at 3 mm.

**Then wait two weeks if you can.** A bed left to settle and watered once will sprout its
own weed seed, which you can hoe off before sowing. Sowing into a freshly dug bed means your
seedlings and the weeds come up together, and telling them apart at that size is genuinely
difficult.

## Soil temperature: how to actually take it

Every crop guide leads with a soil temperature range, and it is the number that decides
whether you sow this week or in three weeks.

Push a probe thermometer to the crop's sowing depth -- 5 mm for rocket, 40 mm for broad
bean -- first thing in the morning, before the sun has been on the bed. Do it three days
running and average the readings.

The morning reading is the one that matters because it is the low the seed sits through
overnight. An afternoon reading in spring can be five or six degrees higher and will talk
you into sowing a fortnight early.

Sow when the three-day morning average is inside the guide's range. In spring, wait for it
to be climbing rather than just touching the bottom of the range.

## Drainage problems you can still fix

If the bed you have already sits wet:

- **Raise it.** Even fifteen centimetres of added height above the surrounding ground moves
  roots out of the waterlogged zone and is usually enough.
- **Add organic matter, not sand.** Compost improves clay structure. Sand added to clay in
  small quantities makes something closer to concrete, and the amount needed to actually
  change the texture is far more than people expect.
- **Do not work it wet.** Digging or walking on saturated clay destroys its structure and
  makes the problem permanent. Wait until it crumbles rather than smears.

## Rotation: set it up now, not later

Do not plant the same botanical family in the same ground in consecutive seasons. Pests and
diseases that specialise in a family build up in the soil, and the family draws the same
nutrients repeatedly.

The practical version for a small garden: split the bed into three or four sections, keep a
note of what family went where, and shift each group along one section next season. Every
crop guide names its family in the "In the Bed" section, and **Core Concepts** has the full
table.

The trap is that families are not obvious from the kitchen. Radish and cabbage are both
brassicas. Tomato and potato are both nightshades. Following radish with cabbage looks like
rotation and is not.
