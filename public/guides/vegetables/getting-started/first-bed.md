# Your First Bed

There is no single right first crop for vegetables. Unlike a tray of microgreens, which
behaves the same in July as in January, a vegetable bed is governed by the season you are
standing in. Sowing the right crop at the wrong time of year is the most common way a first
attempt fails, and it fails slowly — the seed comes up, everything looks fine for three
weeks, and then it bolts.

So start here: **what month is it?** Find your season below and follow that path only.

Written for temperate southern Australia — cool-temperate, winter frosts, southern-hemisphere
seasons. Warmer coastal and subtropical districts run earlier at both ends; cold inland and
high-altitude districts run later. Where a window is tight, the crop's own guide says so.

## Which season am I in?

| Months | Season | Verdict for a first bed |
|---|---|---|
| Sep, Oct, Nov | Spring | Good. Warming ground, long list of options. |
| Dec, Jan, Feb | Summer | Hardest. Start small, plan for autumn. |
| Mar, Apr, May | **Autumn** | **Best.** Start here if you have the choice. |
| Jun, Jul, Aug | Winter | Quiet. Build the bed, sow a little, sow properly in late August. |

If you are reading this in autumn, you have picked the easiest possible time to start a
vegetable garden in this climate, and the rest of this page will be short.

---

## Spring — September to November

Ground is warming, day length is increasing, and almost everything is available. The catch
is at the end of the window: a crop sown in late spring finishes into summer heat, and heat
is what makes leafy crops bolt.

**Sow now: radish and rocket, together, in one bed.**

Both are direct-sown, both are fast, and they fail in visibly different ways, which is what
makes them a good teaching pair.

| Crop | Sow | Ready | Spacing | Depth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Radish | Direct | 25–35 days | 5 cm apart, 15 cm rows | 10 mm |
| Rocket | Direct | 30–40 days | 10 cm apart, 20 cm rows | 5 mm |

Radish is the fastest thing in this library and gives you a result inside a month, which
matters when you are not yet sure you have done any of it right. Rocket is cut-and-come-again,
so it teaches the harvest habit that half the guides here assume.

**The weekend's work.** Take the soil temperature at 10 mm depth on three consecutive
mornings; you want the average above about 10°C. Mark two shallow rows 20 cm apart. Sow
radish along one and rocket along the other, thinly. Cover to depth, water gently, and keep
the surface damp — not wet — until seedlings appear, which is three to seven days.

**Then thin, and actually do it.** At about two weeks, radish seedlings need to end up 5 cm
apart. Snip the surplus at ground level rather than pulling. Skipping this step is the
single reason most first radish crops produce leaves and no bulb.

**Sow again in two weeks.** Both crops carry a 14-day succession interval. A second short
row a fortnight behind the first is what turns one harvest into a run of them, and it costs
you five minutes.

**Watch out for:** late-spring bolting. Radish sown into the lengthening days and rising
heat of late spring runs to seed instead of forming a bulb, and dryness at any point makes
it worse. If you are sowing in November rather than September, keep the water up and expect
to harvest promptly.

**If you want one more thing:** lettuce, sown August to November, is the natural third crop
and also cut-and-come-again.

## Summer — December to February

The honest answer is that summer is the worst time to start a vegetable bed in this
climate, and no crop guide here will pretend otherwise. Most of the fast, forgiving crops
that suit a beginner are cool-season crops that bolt in heat. Starting now means starting
with the ones that do not.

Do two things: sow one crop that genuinely likes the heat, and use the season to build the
bed properly so your autumn sowing — six weeks away, and the best window of the year — goes
in on time.

**Sow now: bush bean.**

| Crop | Sow | Ready | Spacing | Depth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bush bean | Direct | 50–60 days | 10 cm apart, 45 cm rows | 25 mm |

Bush bean wants soil at 16°C or above and grows well up to 30°C, so summer is genuinely its
season rather than something it tolerates. It is direct-sown, the seed is large enough to
place by hand one at a time — which removes thinning from your first attempt entirely — and
it fixes its own nitrogen, so it is forgiving of a bed that is not yet in good heart.

**The weekend's work.** Mark rows 45 cm apart. Push beans in 25 mm deep at 10 cm spacing.
Water in well. Do not water again heavily until they are up — bean seed sitting in cold wet
soil rots, and while summer soil is not cold, waterlogged is waterlogged.

**Sow again in two weeks.** Bush bean carries a 14-day succession interval, and it is worth
using: a single sowing crops heavily over a couple of weeks and then stops.

**Also worth a row:** mustard greens is the one leafy green here with a genuine summer
window (the guide gives December–January). Expect hotter, more fibrous leaves than the same
variety grown in autumn — a flavour cost, not a crop failure.

**Meanwhile, prepare for autumn.** In March you will want to sow rocket, spinach, lettuce
and broad bean, and the bed will need to be ready. Read **Beds and Soil** now and do the
work over summer. An autumn sowing that goes in on time beats a spring sowing that goes in
rushed.

**Watch out for:** water. Everything sown in summer needs consistent moisture, and
inconsistent watering is what triggers bolting in anything leafy you attempt alongside the
beans.

## Autumn — March to May

The best season to start, and by a clear margin. The ground is still warm from summer so
seed germinates fast, but the heat that makes leafy crops bolt has gone. Nearly every
forgiving crop in this library has an autumn window, and several have their *only* good
window here.

**Sow now: rocket and spinach, plus a row of broad bean.**

| Crop | Sow | Ready | Spacing | Depth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rocket | Direct | 30–40 days | 10 cm apart, 20 cm rows | 5 mm |
| Spinach | Direct | 40–77 days | 15 cm apart, 30 cm rows | 10 mm |
| Broad bean | Direct | 80–100 days | 20 cm apart, 45 cm rows | 40 mm |

Rocket gives you something to eat in a month. Spinach is worth sowing specifically because
autumn is one of only two windows it has — its guide is explicit that summer is off-limits
and that a late sowing bolts within weeks of emerging. Getting spinach right is a real
gardening result, and autumn is when it is easy.

Broad bean is the interesting one and worth the row even though it will not feed you until
spring. It is sown in autumn to crop in spring — it establishes before the cold, grows
slowly through winter, and flowers as the weather turns. Its guide flags this as running
opposite to almost everything else here, and understanding why is most of what crop timing
is about.

**The weekend's work.** Rocket and spinach go in shallow — 5 mm and 10 mm — so sow into a
finely raked surface and firm gently. Broad bean is a large seed at 40 mm; place them
individually, 20 cm apart. Water in.

**Then thin.** Spinach to 15 cm, rocket to 10 cm. Broad bean was placed at spacing and does
not need it.

**Sow again in three weeks.** Rocket runs on a 14-day interval and spinach 21. Two or three
successions through the autumn window will keep greens coming through most of winter.

**Also worth doing now:** if you want to grow garlic, autumn is the window — March to May,
planted from cloves, not seed. It occupies its bed until summer, so give it a corner rather
than the middle.

**Watch out for:** very little, honestly. This is the forgiving season. The main risk is
sowing too late — push spinach into June and it will sit stalled rather than growing away.

## Winter — June to August

Cold soil, short days, and slow growth. Not much germinates, and what does germinates
slowly. This is not a season to force.

It is, however, the right time to build the bed, and late August is the front edge of the
spring window — so winter is mostly a season of preparation with one crop worth sowing.

**Sow now: corn salad, if you want something growing.**

| Crop | Sow | Ready | Spacing | Depth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Corn salad (mâche) | Direct | 40–50 days | 10 cm apart, 20 cm rows | 5 mm |

Corn salad has the coldest soil temperature range of anything here — 5°C to 20°C — and will
germinate and grow through cold that stalls lettuce and spinach outright. Its guide gives
a March-to-July window, so June and July are genuinely in season for it.

Expect slow, patchy germination: two to three weeks in cold soil is normal, not a failure,
and resowing too soon is the classic mistake. Be patient.

**The real winter job: build the bed.** Read **Beds and Soil** and do the work now. Compost
worked in during winter has time to break down before spring sowing, frost helps break up
heavy ground you have turned over, and the bed will be ready the week the soil hits
temperature rather than three weeks after.

**Then watch the thermometer from late August.** The spring window opens earlier than most
people sow. Rocket goes in from August, spinach has a late-winter window of August to
September, and lettuce runs from August. Take morning soil readings through the second half
of August and sow the moment the three-day average clears 10°C — a fortnight's head start
in spring is worth more than any other timing decision you will make.

**Also worth doing now:** pea and snow pea both germinate from 5°C and are sown from about
April through September. A row put in during winter crops in spring.

**Watch out for:** waterlogging rather than cold. Winter beds in heavy ground sit wet, and
corn salad in particular rots at the base in soil that pools. If your ground holds water,
raise the bed before you sow anything.

## What to do after the first harvest

Whatever season you started in, the same three habits matter more than the crop you picked.

**Sow the succession.** Nearly everyone sows once, harvests, and then has an empty bed and
a six-week wait. The interval is on every crop guide's Quick Facts table.

**Write down what happened.** Sowing date, weather, what came up, what did not. The second
season is where the gains are, and only if you know what you did the first time. Tracking a
planting in Paddock does this for you.

**Read the crop guide properly before the next sowing.** Each one carries a "Common
Problems" section written around the failures that crop actually has, and it is far more
useful once you have grown the thing once and have something to compare against.
