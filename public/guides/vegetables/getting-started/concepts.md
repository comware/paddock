# Core Concepts

The crop guides in this library are written for someone who already gardens. This page is
the vocabulary they assume. Read it once and the other 59 guides stop being cryptic.

Everything here is written for temperate southern Australia — cool-temperate, with winter
frosts. Seasons are southern-hemisphere: spring is September to November.

## Soil temperature, not air temperature

Every crop guide leads with a **soil temperature** range, and it is the single most useful
number on the page. Seed responds to the ground it is sitting in, not to a warm afternoon.

Air temperature swings ten degrees between morning and afternoon. Soil at sowing depth
barely moves across a day and lags the air by weeks — soil is still cold in early spring
long after the air feels mild, and still warm in autumn long after the air has turned.
This is why sowing "when it feels warm enough" fails: it is usually a month early in spring
and a month late in autumn.

Measure it with a probe thermometer pushed to sowing depth, first thing in the morning,
for three days running. The morning reading is the honest one — it is the low the seed
actually sits through. If the three-day morning average is inside the guide's range, sow.

A $10 kitchen probe thermometer does this perfectly well. You do not need a soil-specific
instrument.

## Germination range is not a sowing calendar

This trips up nearly everyone, so it is worth stating plainly.

Radish germinates from 8°C to 30°C. That does **not** mean radish is a good idea in
January. It means the seed will sprout in January — and then bolt in the heat before it
forms a bulb. Rocket is the same story: it germinates happily in summer and tastes
unpleasantly hot when it does.

The germination range tells you when seed will **start**. Whether the crop will
**finish well** is a separate question, and that is what each guide's "When to Sow" section
is for. Read both.

## Direct sowing vs transplanting

**Direct sowing** means putting seed straight into the bed where the plant will grow.
**Transplanting** means starting seed in a punnet or tray and moving the seedling into the
bed weeks later.

Each crop guide states which it prefers under **Sowing Method**:

| Value | Means |
|---|---|
| `direct` | Sow into the bed. Usually a root crop or something that resents disturbance. |
| `transplant` | Start in a punnet, plant out later. Usually a long-season crop. |
| `either` | Both work. Direct is simpler; transplant buys you a head start. |
| `clove` / `bulb` | Planted from vegetative material, not seed. Garlic and shallots. |

Root crops — carrot, parsnip, radish, daikon — are direct-sown for a physical reason: the
taproot is the crop, and disturbing it while it is forming produces the forked, twisted
roots that beginners assume are a soil problem.

## Punnet, seedling, transplant, hardening off

A **punnet** is the small cell tray you raise seedlings in — the thing nurseries sell
seedlings in. A **seedling** is the young plant. **Planting out** is moving it to the bed.

**Hardening off** is the week before that move. A seedling raised inside or in a greenhouse
has never met wind or direct sun, and going straight into a bed will check its growth
badly or kill it. Put it outside in shade for a couple of hours the first day and build up
to a full day over about a week, then plant it out.

Nursery-bought seedlings have usually been hardened off already. Ones you raised on a
windowsill have not.

## Days to maturity

The number of days from sowing (or from planting out, for transplanted crops) to first
useful harvest. It is always a **range**, because it depends on warmth: the low end is a
good season, the high end a cool or stressed one.

Treat it as a planning figure, not a countdown. A crop guide saying 45–85 for lettuce is
telling you a spring sowing will be ready in about six weeks and an autumn one in about
twelve.

## Bolting

**Bolting** is when a plant abandons leaf production and sends up a flower stalk to set
seed. It is the single most common way a beginner's crop is lost, and it appears in nearly
every leafy-green and root-crop guide here.

Once a plant bolts, it is finished as a vegetable. Leaves turn bitter and tough within days
and no amount of watering or feeding brings them back. Harvest what you can and replace it.

The triggers are heat, lengthening days, and stress — usually dryness. This is why spinach
is an autumn crop and not a summer one, and why the guides push so hard on consistent
watering.

## Cut-and-come-again

A harvest method, not a plant type. Instead of pulling the whole plant, you take the outer
leaves and leave the central growing point intact, and the plant keeps producing for weeks
or months.

The rule across the guides is consistent: **take no more than about a third at once, and
never touch the centre.** Chard, kale, lettuce, rocket and silverbeet all work this way. It
is the difference between one salad and a season of salads from the same plant.

## Succession sowing

Sowing the same crop again every few weeks so it matures in a rolling sequence rather than
all at once. Without it, thirty lettuces are ready in the same week and twenty-five of them
bolt while you eat five.

Each guide carries a **Suggested succession** interval. Read the note that goes with it:
unlike the other figures, succession intervals are planning defaults rather than sourced
measurements, and they are worth adjusting to how fast your household actually eats the
crop. Crops that hold a bed for a whole season — pumpkin, garlic, asparagus — say
*Not a succession crop*, which means exactly that.

## Thinning

Sowing more seed than you need and removing the surplus once seedlings are up, so the
survivors get the spacing the guide asks for.

It feels wasteful and beginners routinely skip it. It is the reason for most disappointing
root crops: carrots and radishes left at sowing density compete for room and produce
nothing but tops. Thin while seedlings are small, and with root crops snip at ground level
rather than pulling, so you do not disturb the neighbours you are keeping.

## Plant spacing and row spacing

Two different numbers in every guide. **Plant spacing** is the gap between plants along a
row; **row spacing** is the gap between the rows. Chard at 25 cm × 50 cm means plants a
quarter-metre apart in rows half a metre apart.

Crowding rarely kills a plant. It just makes everything smaller — the guides are blunt
about this — and it keeps air from moving between plants, which is how fungal problems get
started.

## Sowing depth

Millimetres, not centimetres, for most things. The working rule is roughly two to three
times the seed's own diameter, which is why chard sits at 15 mm and rocket at 5 mm.

Too deep is the more common error: the seedling exhausts its reserves before reaching light
and never emerges. Fine seed sown too deep simply fails, silently, and gets blamed on the
seed.

## Feeding, and what top-dressing means

Most vegetables want compost or aged manure worked into the bed before planting. Some —
the guides call them **heavy feeders** — also want feeding during the crop, because they
are producing continuously for months.

**Top-dressing** is spreading compost or fertiliser on the surface around established
plants rather than digging it in, and letting water carry it down. You do this when the
plant is already in the ground and digging would damage roots.

## Crop rotation and plant families

Growing the same family in the same ground year after year builds up the pests and diseases
that specialise in it, and drains the same nutrients repeatedly. **Rotation** is moving
families around the beds season to season.

Each guide's "In the Bed" section names the crop's botanical family so you can group them:

| Family | Crops here |
|---|---|
| Brassicaceae | Cabbage, broccoli, kale, rocket, mizuna, radish, turnip, swede |
| Solanaceae | Tomato, potato, capsicum, chilli, eggplant |
| Amaranthaceae | Beetroot, chard/silverbeet, spinach |
| Apiaceae | Carrot, parsnip, parsley, coriander, dill |
| Fabaceae | Peas and all beans |
| Cucurbitaceae | Pumpkin, zucchini, cucumber, squash, rockmelon |
| Alliaceae | Onion, garlic, leek, shallot, chives, spring onion |

The names matter more than they look. Rocket and radish seem unrelated and are both
brassicas; chard and spinach look like the same crop and are the same family but different
genera. Following radish with cabbage is a rotation mistake that looks like good practice.

## Frost, and the two frost dates

Two dates govern the calendar in a frost-prone district: the **last frost** in spring and
the **first frost** in autumn. Between them is the frost-free window.

Warm-season crops — beans, tomato, zucchini, capsicum, cucumber — are killed outright by a
light frost at either end. Cool-season crops are unbothered, and some are improved by it.
Your local dates vary by district and elevation more than by latitude; a nursery or garden
club near you will know them.
