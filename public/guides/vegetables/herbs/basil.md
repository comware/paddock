## Overview

Basil is the one herb here that will not forgive a cold night. It is a true tropical annual doing a
season in a temperate climate, and everything about growing it well comes back to that: it wants
warmth in the soil and warmth in the air, and it sulks or dies the moment either drops away. Grown
well it is fast, leafy, and productive from midsummer through to the first cool nights of autumn,
when it will go downhill fast whether you've picked it or not.

## When to Sow

Wait for soil temperature to sit consistently at 18°C or above before direct sowing or planting out
— this is well after the last frost in most of temperate southern Australia, typically from late
spring into summer (November onward in Central Victoria, earlier in warmer coastal zones). Basil
sown or planted into cold soil simply stalls; it doesn't die outright but it won't grow until the
soil warms up regardless, so there's nothing gained by rushing it. If you want a head start, raise
seedlings indoors or under cover from about four weeks before your last expected frost, and don't
plant out until night temperatures are reliably above 12°C.

## Sowing

Sow seed 5 mm deep, direct or into trays, at 25°C if you can manage it under cover — basil
germinates fastest in real warmth and can take two weeks or more in cool soil. Thin or space plants
to 25 cm each way; basil wants airflow around it more than most herbs, since crowded plants are the
first to get fungal problems in humid weather. Keep the seed bed moist but not wet until
germination, which is typically 5-10 days in warm conditions.

## Growing On

Feed basil reasonably well — it's a leafy crop growing fast in warm weather and it will use the
nitrogen you give it. Water at the base rather than overhead where you can; wet leaves in warm humid
weather are how downy mildew gets started, and it spreads through a basil bed fast once it does.
Mulch to keep the root zone from drying out, since basil goes limp and bitter under moisture stress.
Pinch out the growing tip once plants have 3-4 sets of true leaves — this is not optional if you
want a bushy plant rather than one leggy stem heading straight for flower.

## Harvest

Harvest by pinching stems just above a leaf pair, not by stripping individual leaves. Cutting above
a node forces the plant to branch from that point, which is what keeps it producing new growth
instead of running up to flower. Once flower buds appear, pinch them out — basil that's allowed to
flower stops putting energy into leaf and the leaves already on the plant start to taste harsher.
Regular harvesting is itself the main technique for keeping a basil plant productive for its whole
season.

## Succession

Basil is a genuine succession crop — sow again every 21 days through the warm months so you have
young, vigorous plants coming on as older ones tire or bolt. This matters more for basil than for
most herbs because a single planting won't carry you through summer: heat stress, flowering, and the
general decline of an annual mean plants slow down well before the season ends. The other reason to
keep sowing is risk management — basil is frost-tender at both ends of the season, so a late spring
cold snap or an early autumn one can wipe out a planting overnight, and having a younger batch
coming on covers the gap.

## Common Problems

The most common failure is planting too early — basil put out while soil is still cool just sits
there, yellows, and often rots rather than growing away, and by the time it recovers a later,
correctly timed sowing has usually caught up and overtaken it. Downy mildew, showing as yellowing on
top of leaves and grey fuzz underneath, follows overhead watering and poor airflow in humid weather;
water at the base and give plants room. Basil also goes to flower readily under any kind of stress —
heat, dryness, or simple neglect of pinching — so if you're seeing flower spikes before you expected
them, look at watering and harvest frequency first.

## In the Bed

Basil is one of the better companion plants in a vegetable garden and is commonly grown alongside
tomatoes, both because they want the same warm conditions and because basil's scent is thought to
discourage some pest insects, though the evidence for the latter is more folklore than proven. It's
a hungry, thirsty plant for its size, so treat it more like a leafy vegetable than a set-and-forget
herb, and don't tuck it into poor, dry corners expecting perennial-herb toughness — that's a
different group of plants entirely, covered elsewhere in this guide set.
