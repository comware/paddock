## Overview

Thyme is a small, woody Mediterranean perennial built for poor, dry, sun-baked ground, and that
background matters more with thyme than with most herbs. Planted once into the right conditions it
will crop for years with almost no attention; planted into rich, damp garden soil meant for
vegetables, it grows soft, leggy, and rarely lasts more than a season or two before rotting off. The
"moderate" difficulty here isn't about the growing itself — it's about resisting the urge to treat
it like a vegetable.

## When to Sow

Plant out once soil temperature is reliably between 15°C and 25°C — spring is the standard window in
temperate southern Australia, giving the plant a full warm season to establish its root system
before its first winter. Thyme is usually grown from nursery seedlings or cuttings rather than seed,
since seed-grown plants are slow and variable; a cutting or small plant will establish and start
cropping well within its first season.

## Sowing

If starting from seed, sow shallowly — about 3 mm deep — since thyme seed needs light to germinate
well and shouldn't be buried deeply. Space plants 25 cm apart in rows 30 cm apart, giving each plant
room to spread into its own low, spreading dome without competing with its neighbours for the good
drainage it needs. Most gardeners will do better starting from a nursery seedling or a cutting taken
from an established plant, which roots readily in a gritty, free-draining mix.

## Growing On

Give thyme the leanest, driest, sunniest spot you have. A raised bed, a rockery, or a spot near a
path where soil tends to be poorer and drains fast all suit it better than a rich vegetable bed.
Don't feed it — thyme grown in fertile soil puts on soft, floppy growth that has less flavour and is
more prone to fungal rot, which is the single most common way people lose thyme plants. Water only
enough to establish new plants; once settled in, thyme in temperate southern Australia often needs
no supplementary watering beyond normal rainfall, and erring on the dry side is safer than erring on
the wet side.

## Harvest

Harvest by snipping whole stem tips rather than stripping individual leaves, which are too small to
pick one at a time in any case. As with all woody perennials here, never take more than about a
third of the plant's growth at any one time — thyme regrows from the cut points but a hard, repeated
overharvest weakens the plant and can kill it, since it doesn't have the vigorous regrowth response
of a soft-stemmed annual. The best flavour comes just before flowering, though thyme's small purple
flowers are also worth leaving on some stems for the bees.

## Succession

Thyme is a long-lived perennial and not a succession crop — a well-sited planting will produce for
several years. The main ongoing task is renewal rather than resowing: woody perennial herbs get
leggy and less productive with age, and thyme in particular benefits from an occasional light trim
after flowering to keep it compact, plus eventual replacement every four to six years once the
centre of an old plant has become bare and woody.

## Common Problems

Root rot from wet feet is the dominant failure mode and is almost always a drainage or overwatering
problem rather than a disease that arrived from outside — if a thyme plant is going soft, yellowing,
or dying back from the centre, suspect the soil and site before anything else. Legginess and poor
flavour both point to soil that's too rich or too wet. Thyme can also struggle through a wet, humid
summer more than through winter cold, since it's the combination of warmth and moisture that favours
the fungal problems that kill it.

## In the Bed

Thyme's low, spreading habit and tolerance of foot traffic make it a good choice along path edges or
between pavers where soil is naturally poor and well-drained, and its flowers are a strong drawcard
for bees. It pairs well in a planting scheme with rosemary, oregano, and sage — the other
Mediterranean perennials in this group — since they all want the same lean, dry, sunny conditions
and can be grouped together away from the water and feeding regime that vegetables and leafy herbs
need.
