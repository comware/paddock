## Overview

Read this before you plant mint anywhere: it runs. Mint spreads by underground runners that travel
well beyond where you put the original plant, sending up new shoots at a distance and forming a
dense mat that will crowd out anything nearby. Grown in the open ground, mint doesn't stay where you
planted it — it takes over the bed, and by the time you notice it's usually well established under
neighbouring plants too. This isn't a maybe; it's what the plant does, and it's the single most
important thing to know before you plant it.

## When to Sow

Mint is grown from transplant, not seed — named varieties don't come true from seed and cuttings or
nursery plants establish far faster anyway. Plant out once soil temperature is between 10°C and
25°C, which covers most of the year outside the coldest weeks of winter in temperate southern
Australia; spring is the easiest window since the plant has a full season of warmth ahead to
establish.

## Sowing

Because this is a transplant crop, "sowing" here means planting a rooted cutting or nursery seedling
at the depth it was already growing, spaced 30 cm apart in rows 40 cm apart if you're planting more
than one — though for most home gardens, one contained mint plant is enough. Mint roots so readily
from stem cuttings in a glass of water that buying more than one plant is rarely necessary; a few
cuttings from an existing plant will establish just as well.

## Growing On

The single most important growing decision with mint is containment. Grow it in a pot — either
freestanding or sunk into the bed up to its rim with the bottom cut out to allow drainage while
still blocking runners — rather than planting it directly into open ground unless you genuinely
don't mind it spreading and are prepared to give it the space. A sunk pot needs its rim to sit
slightly above soil level, since runners will happily go up and over the edge and root wherever they
touch soil. Mint likes consistent moisture and part shade in hot climates, tolerating more shade
than most herbs here, and general garden soil suits it without special feeding.

## Harvest

Harvest by cutting stems, not individual leaves, taking them just above a leaf pair the same way as
basil — this keeps the plant bushy rather than leggy. Mint tolerates hard cutting extremely well; a
whole clump can be cut back to a few centimetres above ground in the growing season and will regrow
quickly. Regular harvesting is good management, not just leaf collection — an uncut mint patch gets
leggy, flowers early, and the leaf quality drops.

## Succession

Mint is a spreading perennial, not a succession crop — one planting, properly contained, will supply
you for years. There's no resowing schedule; the ongoing task is control of its spread, not renewal
of the planting.

## Common Problems

The overwhelming majority of mint problems are really about failing to contain it early — a runner
escaping a pot or garden edge and establishing in the lawn or a neighbouring bed can take a full
season to notice and considerably longer to remove completely, since any piece of root left behind
will resprout. If you're dealing with an escaped patch, dig out as much root as you can find and
expect to be pulling up strays for a while afterward. Rust can affect mint in humid, crowded
conditions, showing as orange pustules on leaf undersides — thin the clump for airflow and remove
affected growth. Mint can also decline in the centre of an old, congested pot; lift and divide it
every couple of years the same as you would chives.

## In the Bed

Never plant mint directly among other perennials or in an open vegetable bed unless you are
genuinely happy to let it spread through that whole area — it is one of the few herbs in a home
garden capable of outcompeting established plantings over a season or two. Contained in a pot, it's
excellent near a doorway or outdoor kitchen where you want it within easy reach, and its strong
scent is sometimes used to help deter some pests from nearby plants, though this is a minor benefit
next to the containment issue.
