## Overview

Rosemary is a large, long-lived, woody Mediterranean shrub — one plant can eventually reach well
over a metre in height and width, which is why it needs more room than most herbs here. Like thyme,
it comes from poor, rocky, sun-baked hillsides, and it wants those same conditions in a garden: lean
soil, sharp drainage, and full sun. Give it that and a single plant will outlive most other things
in the garden; give it rich, damp ground instead and it grows soft, sprawling, and prone to the root
rot that kills it outright.

## When to Sow

Plant out once soil temperature is between 15°C and 25°C, which in temperate southern Australia
means spring is the standard and safest window — it gives the plant a full warm season to build a
root system before facing its first winter. Rosemary is almost always grown from a nursery plant or
a cutting rather than seed, since seed is slow to germinate and slower again to reach a useful size;
cuttings taken from semi-hardwood growth root reliably and are the standard way most gardeners
propagate it.

## Sowing

If growing from seed, sow shallowly at around 3 mm — rosemary seed is small and benefits from light
for germination — and expect a slow and often uneven strike over several weeks. Space plants a full
60 cm apart in rows 80 cm apart; this looks like too much room for a small seedling but rosemary
grows into a substantial shrub over several years, and crowding it now means struggling to prune
around it later. Most gardeners will do far better starting from an established nursery plant.

## Growing On

Full sun and free-draining, lean soil are non-negotiable. If your soil is heavy clay, plant rosemary
on a mound or in a raised position rather than fighting the drainage in place — waterlogged roots
are the main way this plant dies. Don't feed it and don't water it once established beyond what
normal rainfall provides in temperate southern Australia; a mature rosemary plant is genuinely
drought-tolerant and resents being pampered. Prune it after flowering to keep it in shape and stop
it becoming woody and bare in the centre — this is more about long-term shape than about harvest,
since rosemary tolerates a proper structural prune better than a light, constant nibbling.

## Harvest

Harvest by cutting stem tips, which is also effectively a shaping prune at the same time. Never take
more than about a third of the plant's growth in one go — rosemary is slower to recover than soft
annual herbs, and old, thick wood doesn't reliably resprout if cut back too hard, so a plant that's
been overharvested can end up permanently bare in patches. Flavour is strongest in stems that have
had a chance to mature rather than the very newest soft growth.

## Succession

Rosemary is a woody perennial that will crop for many years from a single planting and isn't grown
on any resowing schedule. The main long-term task is structural pruning to stop the plant becoming a
mass of bare, woody stems with foliage only at the tips — start shaping it while it's young rather
than waiting until it's overgrown and hard to correct.

## Common Problems

Root rot from poor drainage or overwatering is the single most common way rosemary dies, and it's
almost always avoidable — if you're losing plants, look at drainage and water frequency before
anything else, since rosemary in the right spot in temperate southern Australia often needs no
irrigation at all once established. Legginess and sparse foliage usually mean too much shade, too
much water, or too rich a soil. Rosemary can also suffer in very heavy frost in its first year or
two before it's fully established, so in the coldest parts of the zone a sheltered position or some
protection over the first winter is worth the trouble.

## In the Bed

Rosemary's size and permanence mean it's better treated as a small garden shrub than a bed herb —
plant it somewhere it can stay for a decade or more rather than in the middle of an annual vegetable
rotation. It's a genuinely good pollinator plant when in flower, and its lean-soil, low-water needs
make it a natural companion for thyme, oregano, and sage rather than for water-hungry vegetables or
leafy herbs.
