## Overview

Coriander is the herb everyone complains about, and the complaint is always the same: it bolts to
flower and seed almost as soon as it's worth eating. That's not bad luck or bad soil — it's what the
plant does. Coriander is a cool-season annual with a genetically short fuse for heat, and the single
biggest thing you can do to get more leaf out of it is stop fighting that and start working around
it: sow little and often, sow somewhere cooler than full summer sun, and don't expect one planting
to last.

## When to Sow

Direct sow once soil temperature is between 10°C and 25°C — in practice this means autumn through
spring in temperate southern Australia (March-May and again August-October in Central Victoria),
skipping the hottest run of summer altogether unless you can offer it real afternoon shade.
Coriander does not transplant well, so direct sowing is the reliable method; its taproot resents
disturbance and a transplanted seedling will often bolt out of sheer stress rather than establish
normally.

## Sowing

Sow 5 mm deep directly where the plants will grow, spacing seed to end up with plants 10 cm apart in
rows 20 cm apart. Coriander seed is actually a small round fruit containing two seeds, and it
germinates better if lightly crushed first to split the halves — press firmly with the back of a
spoon or roll under a jar before sowing. Germination takes 7-14 days depending on soil temperature
and is often patchy; sow a little more thickly than you think you need and thin later rather than
sowing sparsely and getting gaps.

## Growing On

Keep the soil evenly moist — coriander stressed by drying out bolts faster than almost anything else
in the garden, so this is not a crop to forget to water for a few days. A part-shade position,
especially for anything sown from mid-spring on, buys real time before flowering by keeping the
plant a few degrees cooler than it would be in full sun. If you're growing coriander specifically
for leaf rather than seed, look for slow-bolt or "leaf" strains when buying seed — they're bred for
exactly this problem and genuinely last longer in the ground than standard strains before running
up.

## Harvest

Harvest by cutting outer leaves and stems with scissors, leaving the central growing point intact so
the plant keeps producing from the middle. Once a flower stem starts to form in the centre of the
plant, leaf production for that plant is effectively over — the energy has shifted to seed — so at
that point either accept it's finished as a leaf crop or let it go to seed on purpose. Fresh young
leaf, picked before any sign of flowering, has the best flavour and is least prone to the bitter,
soapy edge older bolting plants can develop.

## Succession

This is the crop succession sowing was built for. Sow every 14 days through the cool months so a
fresh, non-bolted planting is always coming on behind whichever one has run up to flower — treat
each sowing as a short-lived batch rather than expecting any single planting to feed you for the
whole season. Don't fight a bolted plant by cutting it back hard and hoping for regrowth; it rarely
returns to good leaf production and you're better off pulling it and relying on the next sowing.

## Common Problems

Bolting is the defining problem with coriander and the cause is almost always heat, day length, or
moisture stress rather than anything you did wrong in soil prep. If a planting bolts fast even in
cool weather, it may simply have been sown too late in the warming part of the season — shift your
sowing window earlier next time. The upside worth mentioning: a bolted plant isn't a wasted plant.
Let it flower and set seed and you get coriander seed for the kitchen, plus it self-sows readily, so
a bolted patch left alone often reseeds itself into the next cool season without any further effort.

## In the Bed

Coriander's flowers, when you let it go to seed, are attractive to hoverflies and other small
beneficial insects, so a few plants deliberately allowed to bolt at the back of a bed can do useful
pest-control work even though they've stopped being a leaf crop. Keep coriander away from fennel,
which it's said to inhibit and be inhibited by, though the practical effect in a home garden bed is
usually smaller than the folklore suggests.
