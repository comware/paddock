## Overview

Chives are the mildest and easiest member of the onion family you'll grow, and unlike most of the
herbs in this group they're not woody or Mediterranean in origin — they're a clumping, grass-like
perennial that dies back in hard winter cold and regrows from the same clump for years. Plant once,
divide occasionally, and cut as needed; this is about as low-maintenance as herbs get.

## When to Sow

Sow or plant when soil temperature is between 10°C and 25°C — autumn or spring in temperate southern
Australia, avoiding the depths of winter when growth stalls and the height of summer when
germination from seed is less reliable. Chives are slow from seed, often taking two to three weeks
to germinate and a full season to bulk up into a useful clump, so many gardeners start with a
purchased potted clump or a division from an established plant instead.

## Sowing

If sowing seed, sow 5 mm deep in trays or direct, spacing plants 15 cm apart in rows 25 cm apart —
chives are usually grown in small clusters of several seedlings together rather than as single
spaced plants, since a cluster bulks up into a usable clump faster than isolated single plants. Keep
the seed bed moist until germination. If planting divisions instead, simply separate an existing
clump into smaller sections with a few bulbs each and replant at the same spacing.

## Growing On

Chives are unfussy about soil as long as it isn't waterlogged, and unlike the woody Mediterranean
perennials in this group they're happy with regular water and reasonably good soil rather than
wanting things lean and dry. Established clumps benefit from division every two to three years — dig
up the whole clump, split it into smaller sections by hand, and replant; this keeps the centre of
the clump from becoming congested and declining, which is the main way chives lose vigour over time.

## Harvest

Harvest by cutting leaves to about 2-3 cm above the base with scissors, never pulling individual
leaves out, which damages the surrounding growth. Cut from the outside of the clump first and let
the centre keep growing. Regular cutting actually encourages fresh, tender growth and is good for
the plant rather than something to go easy on — an established clump can be cut hard and will regrow
within a couple of weeks in the growing season.

## Succession

Chives are a perennial clump crop, not something you resow on a schedule — one planting, divided
every couple of years as it bulks up, will supply you indefinitely. There's no succession interval
to manage here; the plant itself is the ongoing supply.

## Common Problems

The main way chives disappoint is neglect of division — an old, congested clump left untouched for
many years will thin out in the centre and produce less over time, so treat division as routine
maintenance rather than something only needed if problems appear. Chives can also self-sow more than
expected once they flower, with their attractive purple pom-pom flowers setting viable seed that
turns up as volunteer clumps nearby; deadhead spent flowers if you don't want this. Rust, a fungal
disease showing as orange-brown pustules on the leaves, can appear in humid conditions with poor
airflow — cut affected foliage back hard and improve spacing.

## In the Bed

Chives' purple flowers are genuinely good for attracting bees and other pollinators, and unlike most
alliums they can be left to flower without much cost to the leaf harvest, since you'll always have
new growth coming from the clump. They're a traditional companion near roses and other plants prone
to fungal problems, on the theory that the sulphur compounds in the alliums help suppress issues
like black spot and mildew nearby — a modest effect at best, but there's no real downside to the
planting.
