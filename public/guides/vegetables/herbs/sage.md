## Overview

Sage is a woody Mediterranean perennial with soft, felted grey-green leaves, and it shares the same
fundamental preference as rosemary and thyme for lean, well-drained soil over rich, damp ground —
though its bigger, softer leaves make it slightly more prone to rot in humid or overwatered
conditions than either of them. Grown in the right spot it's a long-lived, reliable plant; grown wet
and crowded it can decline within a season or two, usually from fungal problems rather than simple
starvation.

## When to Sow

Plant out once soil temperature is between 15°C and 25°C — spring in temperate southern Australia is
the standard window, allowing the plant to establish a root system before its first winter. Sage can
be raised from seed but is commonly grown from a nursery plant or a cutting instead, since cuttings
from an established plant root reasonably easily and get you a known flavour and habit faster than
seed does.

## Sowing

If sowing seed, sow shallowly at around 3 mm deep, since sage seed benefits from light and shouldn't
be buried too deep. Space plants 40 cm apart in rows 50 cm apart — sage forms a substantial rounded
shrub over time and, like rosemary, looks over-spaced as a young plant but will fill the gap. Seed
germination is often slow and uneven, so don't be discouraged by a patchy first few weeks.

## Growing On

Full sun and sharp drainage matter more for sage than for oregano, closer to what thyme and rosemary
need. Avoid heavy, wet soils, and if that's what you have, raise the bed or mound the soil under the
plant rather than accepting standing moisture around the roots. Sage's big, soft, slightly fuzzy
leaves are more prone than the fine leaves of thyme or rosemary to fungal spotting and mildew in
humid, still air, so give it more space than feels necessary to keep airflow moving through the
plant, and resist the urge to water it on a schedule the way you would a vegetable bed.

## Harvest

Harvest by cutting whole stems rather than picking single leaves, which encourages bushier regrowth
from the cut point. As with the other woody perennials, don't take more than roughly a third of the
plant at any one time — sage is reasonably tolerant of regular light picking but a hard, repeated
overharvest thins the plant out and slows its recovery, especially going into winter. A light trim
after flowering each year helps keep the plant compact and productive rather than leggy.

## Succession

Sage is a long-lived woody perennial, not a succession crop, and a single well-sited planting will
produce for several years. It does tend to get woody and less productive with age faster than
rosemary — many gardeners plan to replace a sage plant every four or five years once the centre has
gone bare and bald, propagating a cutting from the old plant ahead of time so there's no gap.

## Common Problems

Fungal rot and leaf spot in humid or overwatered conditions are the main issues, and they're a soil
and spacing problem far more often than a disease that arrived from outside — improve drainage and
airflow before treating the symptom. Legginess with bare lower stems is common as plants age and
usually calls for a harder prune after flowering rather than more feeding, since more feeding tends
to make it worse. Sage can also struggle through a genuinely wet, humid summer more than through a
dry winter, which is the reverse of what people often expect from a "cool-season-looking" grey-
leaved plant.

## In the Bed

Sage's flowers, when the plant is allowed to bloom, are a strong drawcard for bees and other
pollinators, and its grey-green foliage works well as a textural contrast planted alongside the
darker green of rosemary and thyme in a dedicated dry, sunny herb bed. Keep it away from anywhere
that gets regular irrigation meant for thirstier vegetables — the mismatch in water needs is a more
common cause of a struggling sage plant than any pest or disease.
