## Overview

Oregano is a low, spreading Mediterranean perennial and, of the woody herbs in this group, one of
the easier and more forgiving — it's more tolerant of average garden conditions than rosemary or
thyme, though it still does best and tastes strongest when grown a little on the hard side rather
than lush and well fed. Left alone, a clump will spread steadily by low, rooting stems and can be
divided to make new plants once established.

## When to Sow

Plant out once soil temperature is between 15°C and 25°C — spring is the reliable window in
temperate southern Australia, giving the plant a full season to establish before winter. Oregano can
be grown from seed, but named culinary varieties are often better started from a nursery plant,
division, or cutting, since seed-grown plants can vary in flavour strength and some are noticeably
milder than others.

## Sowing

If sowing seed, sow shallowly at around 3 mm, since oregano seed is fine and germinates better with
light rather than being buried. Space plants 30 cm apart in rows 40 cm apart to allow for its
spreading habit — a single plant will fill more space than its initial size suggests within a season
or two. Cuttings and divisions from an established plant root easily and are the more reliable route
to a plant you know the flavour of.

## Growing On

Oregano wants full sun and, like its Mediterranean relatives, does best in free-draining soil that
isn't kept constantly wet — but it's more tolerant of ordinary garden soil than rosemary or thyme
and doesn't demand the same lean, rocky conditions to thrive. That said, the same rule still applies
in spirit: don't over-feed or over-water it chasing bigger leaves, since oregano grown too soft
loses the concentrated flavour that's the whole reason to grow it rather than buy it dried.
Established plants are quite drought-tolerant.

## Harvest

Harvest by cutting stem tips just above a leaf pair, the same principle as basil and mint, which
encourages the plant to branch and stay bushy rather than sprawl thin. As with the other woody
perennials here, avoid taking more than about a third of the plant at once, though oregano is
generally a faster and more forgiving regrower than rosemary or thyme. Flavour is strongest in the
leaf just before the plant flowers.

## Succession

Oregano is a spreading perennial and not sown on a succession schedule — a single planting will
spread and persist for years. The main maintenance task is division rather than resowing: lift and
split an established clump every few years to keep it vigorous and to make new plants for other
spots, since old clumps can get woody and thin in the centre in the same way as chives.

## Common Problems

Weak or bland flavour is the most common complaint and almost always traces back to soil that's too
rich, too much water, or too much shade — oregano concentrates its flavour under a bit of stress,
and it's worth deliberately underwatering and underfeeding an established plant rather than
pampering it. Powdery mildew can appear on crowded plants in humid conditions with poor airflow;
thin the clump and improve spacing rather than reaching for a spray first. Oregano can also spread
further than expected in good conditions and crowd out smaller neighbouring plants, so give it room
or be ready to divide and contain it.

## In the Bed

Oregano's spreading, mat-forming habit makes it a useful low edging plant along a sunny path or bed
border, and its summer flowers are attractive to bees and a wide range of small beneficial insects.
It groups naturally with thyme, rosemary, and sage as a low-water, low-fuss planting, and unlike
mint it doesn't spread by invasive underground runners — its spread is above ground and visible, so
a plant getting too large is easy to see coming and simply trim back.
