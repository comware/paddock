## Overview

Dill is a fast, easy annual with the same basic weakness as coriander: it runs to flower at the
first real heat, and once it does the fine feathery leaf you grow it for stops coming. It's a
taprooted plant that dislikes being moved, which makes direct sowing the reliable path, and it's
fast enough from sowing to usable leaf that treating it as a short-lived, repeatedly-sown crop
rather than a permanent bed occupant is the easiest way to keep a steady supply.

## When to Sow

Direct sow once soil temperature is in the 10-25°C range — cooler weather in temperate southern
Australia, so autumn and spring are the reliable windows, with summer sowing best avoided unless you
can give it part shade and can accept a shorter useful life before it bolts. Dill germinates
reasonably quickly in the cooler end of that range, typically 10-14 days.

## Sowing

Sow seed 5 mm deep directly in position, thinning to 20 cm between plants in rows 30 cm apart.
Because dill's taproot resents disturbance, sow it where it's going to grow rather than starting it
in trays for transplant — a transplant shock at the seedling stage is a common trigger for premature
bolting later on. Keep the seed bed moist until germination; dill seed sown into dry soil will
simply sit and wait, sometimes indefinitely.

## Growing On

Dill wants a warm, sunny position but benefits from the same heat-avoidance logic as coriander —
plant it somewhere it isn't baking in full summer sun if you're sowing anywhere near the warm end of
its range. It grows tall and can become top-heavy once mature, especially in a windy position, so a
bit of support or a sheltered spot helps keep plants upright. It doesn't need rich soil or heavy
feeding; average garden soil with reasonably even moisture is enough.

## Harvest

Harvest leaf by snipping the feathery fronds from the outside of the plant, taking what you need as
you go rather than cutting the whole plant at once. Once the flat yellow flower heads appear, leaf
production is essentially over for that plant — the plant has switched to seed production and the
remaining foliage coarsens. If you want dill seed for pickling or cooking, let a few plants flower
and go to seed deliberately rather than treating every bolt as a failure.

## Succession

Sow every 21 days through the cooler months to keep a run of young, leafy plants coming through,
since any single dill planting has a limited useful life before it bolts. This matters especially in
a climate with a short cool window either side of summer — staggered sowings mean you're not left
with a gap in supply once the first planting has run to seed.

## Common Problems

Bolting under heat or moisture stress is the main issue, exactly as with coriander, and the fix is
the same: sow in cooler weather, keep water even, and don't expect one planting to last through
summer. Dill can also flop over once it's grown tall and set flower buds, particularly in exposed
spots — stake it or plant it where neighbouring crops give it some shelter from wind. Aphids will
sometimes cluster on the flower heads; in a home garden this is rarely serious enough to need
intervention and often draws in predatory insects that help control aphids elsewhere in the bed.

## In the Bed

Dill's flowers are excellent for attracting hoverflies, parasitic wasps, and other beneficial
insects, so letting a portion of a planting run to flower is a reasonable trade even if it costs you
leaf from those particular plants. It's a poor companion for carrots specifically — the two are
related and cross-pollinate, and dill is also thought to inhibit carrot growth — so keep some
distance between them, and be aware dill self-sows readily once it's gone to seed, which can be
either a bonus or a nuisance depending on how much volunteer dill you want turning up next season.
