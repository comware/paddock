## Overview

Parsley is a biennial grown here as an annual, and that distinction explains most of its habits. In
its first year it puts its energy into leaf, which is what you want; in its second year, once it's
been through a winter, it bolts to flower and seed and the leaf quality drops away. Grown as a one-
season crop and replaced before it hits that second-year switch, it's an easy, reliable, slow-
growing herb. The "moderate" difficulty rating is really about patience with slow, uneven
germination rather than any fussiness once it's up.

## When to Sow

Sow when soil temperature is between 10°C and 25°C, which in temperate southern Australia covers
most of the year outside the coldest weeks of winter and the peak of summer — autumn and spring are
the most reliable windows. Parsley can be direct sown or raised as seedlings and transplanted;
either works, but germination is slow and often patchy either way, commonly taking three to four
weeks. Don't assume a bare patch means failure before a month has passed.

## Sowing

Sow seed 5 mm deep, spacing to end up with plants 20 cm apart in rows 30 cm apart. Soaking seed
overnight before sowing can speed germination slightly, though it won't make it fast — parsley seed
contains compounds that inhibit its own germination and simply needs time to leach them out in moist
soil. Keep the seed bed consistently moist through this long germination period; it's the single
biggest thing you can do to improve your strike rate, since parsley seed that dries out partway
through germinating will not recover.

## Growing On

Once established, parsley is undemanding — regular water and an occasional feed of something
nitrogen-leaning will keep leaf production steady. It tolerates part shade well, more so than most
herbs, which makes it a useful filler in a bed position that doesn't get full sun all day. In cold
districts it will often sit through winter with minimal growth rather than dying outright, then
resume in spring — that resumption is usually the first sign it's about to think about flowering,
which is the cue to plan its replacement rather than wait for it to bolt outright.

## Harvest

Harvest by cutting outer, older stems at the base, always leaving the central growing point and
newer inner growth to keep producing. This is a cut-and-come-again herb in the same way as many
leafy greens — never strip a plant bare, and never take more than about a third of the visible
growth at once if you want it to keep cropping steadily rather than needing weeks to recover.

## Succession

Sow again roughly every 30 days if you want a continuous supply without relying on any one planting
to carry the whole season, and especially to have young plants ready to take over from an older
planting that's heading toward its second-year bolt. Because parsley is biennial rather than a fast
bolter like coriander or dill, succession here is less about outrunning heat stress and more about
staggered replacement — keeping a younger generation coming through so you're never solely dependent
on a plant that's about to run to seed.

## Common Problems

Slow, uneven germination is the most common frustration and is normal, not a sign of bad seed or
poor technique — give it the full month before deciding a sowing has failed. Yellowing leaves
usually point to overwatering or waterlogged soil rather than underfeeding, since parsley's roots
don't tolerate sitting wet. If a plant sends up a tall flowering stem, it has moved into its second-
year cycle; the leaf below the stem will get coarser and more bitter from this point, so treat it as
finished and rely on your next sowing.

## In the Bed

Parsley is a magnet for swallowtail butterfly caterpillars in many parts of Australia — worth
knowing so you're not alarmed when a plant is stripped, and worth planting a spare if you'd rather
share than net it. It's also one of the more shade-tolerant herbs here, so it earns a place under
the edge of taller crops like tomatoes or climbing beans where full-sun herbs would struggle.
