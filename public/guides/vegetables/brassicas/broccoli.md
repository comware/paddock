## Overview

Broccoli is the brassica most people grow first and the one most people get wrong first, usually by harvesting too late or too early and ending up with a head that's already loosening into yellow flowers. It's a cool-season crop that will put up with a surprising range of conditions once established, but the window between "ready" and "blown" can be a matter of days in warm weather, so it rewards a grower who checks the bed regularly rather than one who plants and forgets.

The plant puts most of its early energy into leaves, then commits to one central head. That head is actually a mass of unopened flower buds, which is why timing matters so much — leave it a week too long in a warm spell and those buds start to separate and yellow. The upside is that broccoli isn't a one-shot crop: once you cut the main head, most varieties throw side shoots for weeks afterwards, so a single planting keeps paying out.

The single thing that catches people out most is planting too late in spring and having the plant hit maturity right as the first real heat arrives, which pushes it to bolt (flower prematurely) or produce a small, loose, bitter head instead of a tight one.

## When to Sow

Sow into trays once soil temperature is reliably in the 7-30°C range, though germination is fastest and most even in the 15-25°C band. In southern Australia that's a spring sowing (September to November) for a summer harvest, or an autumn sowing (March to May) for a cool-season harvest through winter into early spring — broccoli handles a light frost well once established, so the autumn window is often the more reliable one here.

Sow too early in spring and cold nights while the transplant is still small can trigger premature "buttoning" — a tiny head forms on an undersized plant instead of a proper crown. Sow too late and the plant matures into early summer heat, which causes the head to open loosely, bolt to flower, or turn bitter before it's worth eating. If you're not sure which side of the window you're on, err toward the cooler side.

## Sowing

Broccoli is grown as a transplant almost everywhere it's grown commercially and at home, because starting it in trays gives you a strong, uniform seedling before the ground has properly warmed and lets you dodge the worst of a cold snap that would otherwise stall or bolt a direct-sown seedling. Sow seed 10 mm deep into trays or punnets and keep evenly moist; seedlings are usually ready to go out at four to six weeks, once they have two or three true leaves.

Space plants 45 cm apart with 60 cm between rows. Broccoli genuinely needs this room — crowd it and the heads come in noticeably smaller because the plant can't build the leaf canopy it needs to feed a full-sized crown, and tight spacing also cuts airflow around the lower leaves, which is where fungal problems like downy mildew tend to start in a wet season.

## Growing On

Broccoli is a heavy nitrogen feeder, particularly in the leafy growth stage before the head forms — a side dressing of compost or a nitrogen-rich feed a few weeks after transplanting makes a visible difference to head size. Keep the soil consistently moist; broccoli that dries out and is then watered heavily tends to bolt or produce hollow, woody stems.

The thing worth knowing that most guides skip: after you cut the central head, don't pull the plant. Most varieties will push out a flush of smaller side shoots from the leaf axils over the following weeks, and a well-fed plant can keep producing these for a month or more. Keep feeding and watering after the main harvest if you want this second act.

## Harvest

Cut the central head while the buds are still tight, dark, and closed — before you see any yellow petals starting to show between the buds, which means it's already flowering. Head size varies a lot by variety and conditions, so judge readiness by bud tightness rather than diameter. Cut with a sharp knife about 10-15 cm down the stem, angled so water doesn't pool on the cut surface.

Once the main head is off, watch the leaf axils for side shoots and keep harvesting them every few days as they reach a usable size — they'll be smaller than the main head but just as good eating, and picking them regularly encourages more to form.

## Succession

Succession sow every 21 days through the sowing window to spread the harvest rather than getting one glut. In a typical southern Australian spring or autumn window you can usually fit in two to three sowings before the season turns against the crop (heat in late spring, hard frost in winter), which combined with the side-shoot habit gives a reasonably long harvest period from a modest number of plantings.

## Common Problems

**Cabbage white butterfly caterpillars** — small green caterpillars chew ragged holes in the leaves and can hollow out the developing head if left unchecked. They're the larvae of the white butterflies you'll see fluttering around the bed in spring and summer. Check the underside of leaves regularly and squash eggs and caterpillars by hand, or net the bed with insect exclusion mesh from transplanting onward — netting is far more reliable than any spray.

**Aphids** — grey-green clusters on the underside of leaves and around the developing head, often accompanied by sooty mould growing on the honeydew they leave behind. A strong jet of water knocks most of them off; for a bad infestation, a soap spray applied to the underside of leaves works without harming the crop.

**Loose, bolting, or blown heads** — the head opens up, separates, or shows yellow flower petals before you've had a chance to harvest it. This usually comes down to heat stress late in the growing cycle, a cold check early on, or too much nitrogen too late (which pushes rapid, loose growth). Harvest promptly once heads tighten up, and time sowings to avoid the head maturing into a heatwave.

## In the Bed

Broccoli is a Brassicaceae, the same family as cauliflower, cabbage, brussels sprouts, kohlrabi, romanesco, kale, and collards. Don't follow any of these with another — rotate brassicas through a bed no more often than once every 3-4 years if you can manage it, since they share pests and diseases, most seriously clubroot, which persists in soil for years and can't be treated once established. Follow broccoli with legumes where possible; brassicas are heavy feeders and legumes help rebuild the nitrogen they draw down. Broccoli's height and canopy can shade smaller neighbours, so give it space on the sunnier side of mixed beds.
