## Overview

Kohlrabi is the odd one out in this family — instead of a head of leaves or flower buds, it swells a section of stem just above the soil line into a round, crisp bulb that looks a bit like it landed from somewhere else. It's also one of the quickest brassicas to mature, which makes it a useful bed-filler between slower crops, and one of the least fussy about exact conditions as long as it grows fast and without interruption.

That last point is the whole game with kohlrabi. It's a crop that needs to be grown quickly and steadily from start to finish; a plant that's checked by drought, crowding, or a cold snap doesn't just grow slower, it produces a bulb with a woody, fibrous texture instead of the crisp, juicy one you actually want. Speed and consistency matter more here than in almost any other vegetable in the garden.

The single thing that most often goes wrong is simply letting the bulb get too big. Kohlrabi left in the ground past its ideal size turns tough and woody fast, and unlike a cabbage that holds reasonably well once mature, kohlrabi has a narrow window where it's genuinely at its best.

## When to Sow

Sow once soil temperature is in the 10-25°C range. In southern Australia, that's spring (September to November) for an early summer harvest, or late summer to autumn (February to April) for a harvest through autumn into early winter — kohlrabi handles a light frost fine and often does better in the cooler window, since the fast growth it needs is easier to sustain without the stress of summer heat.

Sow too early into cold soil and germination is slow and uneven, giving the crop a sluggish start it may never fully make up. Sow too late into hot weather and the plant is more likely to bolt to flower before the bulb has properly formed, or the bulb that does form will be smaller and tougher from heat stress. Because this is a fast crop with a short window, getting the sowing timing close to right matters more than the exact date.

## Sowing

Kohlrabi can be direct sown or started in trays and transplanted — both work well, which is unusual among the heading brassicas in this family. Direct sowing suits kohlrabi's fast cycle and avoids any transplant shock that could check its growth right when speed matters most; starting in trays can be useful if you want a head start on the season or need to fit it into a bed that's not free yet. Sow 10 mm deep either way, and keep the soil consistently moist for even, fast germination.

Space plants 20 cm apart with 35 cm between rows — noticeably tighter than the heading brassicas, since kohlrabi's bulb stays compact and doesn't need the wide leaf canopy that broccoli or cauliflower rely on. Crowd it further than this recommended spacing, though, and the bulbs stay smaller and the crop takes longer to size up, working against the whole point of growing a fast crop.

## Growing On

Kohlrabi needs to be grown fast, without any checks, more than it needs any specific feeding regime. Keep the soil consistently moist from sowing through to harvest — a dry spell followed by heavy watering, or any real interruption to steady growth, is what turns the bulb woody rather than crisp. Feed lightly but regularly rather than with a single heavy application, since the goal is even, uninterrupted growth rather than a late growth spurt.

Because the whole crop is over in a matter of weeks, there's less time for problems to compound than with a slower brassica, but there's also less time to correct a mistake once it happens — a kohlrabi that's been stressed early rarely fully recovers within its short season.

## Harvest

Harvest once the bulb reaches about 5-8 cm across, depending on variety — check the seed packet, since some varieties are bred to stay tender at a larger size than others. Bulbs left much beyond this size, particularly in warm weather, turn woody and fibrous fast, so don't wait for "bigger" the way you might with other root or bulb crops. Cut the bulb off just below its base with a sharp knife, or pull the whole plant and trim afterward.

Kohlrabi doesn't regrow after harvest — pull or cut the whole plant and clear the bed for the next crop.

## Succession

Succession sow every 21 days through the sowing window. Because kohlrabi matures so quickly and clears the bed fast once harvested, it's one of the easier brassicas to run several successive sowings of in a single season, giving a steady rolling supply rather than a single glut — and it slots neatly into gaps in a rotation where a slower crop isn't ready to go in yet.

## Common Problems

**Woody, fibrous bulbs** — the texture is tough and stringy instead of crisp, usually because the bulb was left too long past its ideal size, grew too slowly, or was checked by drought stress at some point in its short cycle. Harvest promptly at the right size and keep watering consistent from sowing to harvest to avoid this outcome.

**Cabbage white butterfly caterpillars** — chewed leaves, which matter less for kohlrabi than for a leafy or heading brassica since it's the bulb you're after, but a heavily defoliated plant still can't feed a developing bulb properly. Net young plants with insect exclusion mesh or check leaves regularly and hand-pick caterpillars, especially early in the crop's short life when leaf loss does the most damage.

**Splitting bulbs** — the bulb cracks, usually from an inconsistent water supply similar to what causes cabbage heads to split. Keep watering even, and don't let the crop sit in dry soil for an extended stretch followed by a heavy soak.

## In the Bed

Kohlrabi is a Brassicaceae, the same family as broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, brussels sprouts, romanesco, kale, and collards. Rotate brassicas through a bed no more often than once every 3-4 years to manage clubroot and shared pests, and avoid following one brassica directly with another even when the crop was as quick as kohlrabi. Legumes make a good follow-on to rebuild nitrogen. Kohlrabi's biggest advantage in bed planning is speed — it frees the bed fast, making it a useful gap-filler between longer-season crops rather than something that ties up space for months the way brussels sprouts or a slow cabbage does.
