## Overview

Cabbage is one of the more forgiving heading brassicas, which is probably why it's been a staple crop for so long — it tolerates a wider range of temperatures than cauliflower, doesn't need blanching or protecting like a curd crop, and holds in the ground longer than broccoli once it's ready. What it does need is consistent water, because the one failure mode almost every cabbage grower eventually hits is a split head from a heavy watering after a dry spell.

The plant forms a tight ball of overlapping leaves around a central growing point, and once that head is fully formed and firm, the plant keeps growing — the outer leaves keep expanding and the head keeps swelling with water. If growth has been steady, that's fine. If the plant has been under water stress and then gets a big drink (a heavy rain event after a dry run is the classic trigger), the head can absorb water faster than the outer leaves can stretch to accommodate it, and it splits open.

The single thing that most often goes wrong is exactly that: an otherwise perfect, tight head left in the ground a week too long, splitting after a downpour, or growers over-correcting for a dry patch with a sudden heavy soak.

## When to Sow

Sow into trays once soil temperature is reliably in the 7-30°C range, with the fastest even germination around 15-25°C. In southern Australia, sow in spring (September to November) for a summer harvest, or in late summer to autumn (February to April) for a harvest through winter into early spring. Cabbage tolerates a light frost well once established, and many growers find the cooler-season crop less prone to pest pressure and bolting than the summer one.

Sow or transplant too early into cold soil and growth stalls, leaving a small, slow plant that's more vulnerable to pests before it can outgrow them. Leave the crop in the ground too long after the head has firmed up, especially into a spell of heavy rain, and heads split — once split, they don't reseal and are prone to rot, so they need to be used or harvested promptly. In hot weather, cabbage can also bolt to flower if it experiences a period of cold followed by a return to warmth, so timing the sowing window matters more than it might seem for a crop this tolerant.

## Sowing

Cabbage is grown as a transplant because it gives the plant a strong head start before the bed is ready, letting you raise seedlings in controlled conditions while the soil outside is still too cold or too occupied by a previous crop. Sow 10 mm deep into trays and keep evenly moist; seedlings are usually ready to transplant at four to six weeks with several true leaves.

Space plants 40 cm apart with 50 cm between rows. Cabbage is more tolerant of tight spacing than cauliflower or brussels sprouts, but crowd it too much and heads come in smaller and looser, with more disease pressure from reduced airflow around the lower leaves — a particular risk in humid weather, when fungal leaf spots and rots take hold fastest in a dense, poorly ventilated planting.

## Growing On

Cabbage wants consistent water above almost everything else. Set up a regular watering schedule and stick to it, because the swings between dry and wet are what cause split heads, not the total amount of water the crop gets over its life. Feed with a nitrogen-rich fertiliser through the leafy growth stage to build a strong plant before it starts to head up, tapering off as the head firms so growth doesn't get so fast and soft that it invites splitting or pest damage.

Cabbage is a fairly heavy feeder like the rest of the brassica family, but it's more forgiving of a missed feed than cauliflower is of a missed watering — water discipline is the higher-value habit to build here.

## Harvest

A cabbage is ready when the head feels firm and dense when you squeeze it gently from the sides — a head that still gives noticeably under light pressure needs more time. Cut at the base with a sharp knife, leaving a few outer wrapper leaves on for protection if you're not using it immediately. Once cut, cabbage stores well in a cool spot for weeks, which takes some of the urgency off harvest timing compared to broccoli or cauliflower.

If you leave the stalk and a few outer leaves in the ground after cutting the main head, some varieties will produce a handful of small secondary heads from the cut point over the following weeks — not a reliable second crop, but worth leaving the stalk in for a few weeks to see.

## Succession

Succession sow every 21 days through the sowing window for a rolling supply rather than a single glut of heads all firming up at once. Because mature cabbage holds reasonably well in the ground (within reason — don't push your luck through a wet spell), a slightly longer succession run of three to four sowings is more workable here than with cauliflower, giving a steadier spread of harvest-ready heads over several weeks.

## Common Problems

**Split heads** — the head cracks open, usually after a heavy rain or deep watering following a dry period. This is a water-consistency problem, not a disease. Keep watering even through the growing cycle, and harvest promptly once heads firm up rather than leaving them to "hold" through an uncertain weather forecast.

**Cabbage moth and cabbage white caterpillars** — chewed, ragged leaves and caterpillars tucked between the outer leaves or working their way into the head itself. These are the most persistent pest on cabbage specifically, because the tight head gives caterpillars somewhere protected to feed once they're inside it. Net the bed with insect exclusion mesh from transplanting, and check under leaves for the small white or yellow egg clusters regularly — once caterpillars are inside a forming head they're much harder to deal with.

**Clubroot** — stunted, wilting plants with swollen, distorted roots when pulled, usually in acidic or poorly drained soil. There's no cure once it's in the soil, so prevention matters: lime acidic soil before planting brassicas, improve drainage, and hold to a strict rotation so the pathogen doesn't build up.

## In the Bed

Cabbage is a Brassicaceae, the same family as broccoli, cauliflower, brussels sprouts, kohlrabi, romanesco, kale, and collards. Keep brassicas out of the same bed for 3-4 years where you can, since they share soil-borne pests and diseases, clubroot chief among them. Legumes make a good follow-on crop, restoring nitrogen that cabbage draws down heavily during its leafy growth phase. Cabbage's broad, low habit means it doesn't shade neighbours much, but its wide outer leaves do compete hard for space right at ground level, so don't crowd it against smaller companions.
