## Overview

Collards are a leafy brassica that never bothers forming a head — you're growing the leaves themselves, picked continuously over a long season, which puts them closer to kale than to cabbage or broccoli in how you use them in the garden. They're also one of the most heat-tolerant brassicas here, handling conditions that would send cauliflower or broccoli straight into bolting or bitterness.

Because there's no curd or head to protect, time, or blanch, collards are largely free of the timing anxiety that runs through the rest of this family. The plant just keeps producing new leaves from the growing point as you harvest the older ones, which makes it one of the lowest-maintenance crops in the brassica family once it's established.

The one thing that does catch growers out is treating collards as completely indestructible and neglecting them past the point of good eating — leaves left too long on the plant, especially in hot weather, get tough and strongly flavoured, and a plant that's stripped too hard without any feeding will slow down and produce smaller, less tender leaves over time.

## When to Sow

Sow or direct sow once soil temperature is in the 10-30°C range. In southern Australia, collards can go in from spring right through to autumn (September to April), which is a wider window than almost any other brassica in this family — they'll tolerate the heat of a mild summer better than broccoli or cauliflower, and a light frost doesn't trouble them either.

Sowing too early into cold, wet soil can lead to slow, patchy germination, but this is a minor risk compared to most brassicas since collards are broadly tolerant. Sowing into the height of summer heat can push leaves toward a tougher, more bitter flavour, and a hard frost will eventually damage the plant, so the main timing consideration is picking a window that avoids the very extremes rather than hitting a narrow ideal.

## Sowing

Collards can be direct sown or started in trays and transplanted — both approaches work well. Direct sowing is simple and collards don't sulk much from the disturbance of transplanting either, so the choice mostly comes down to convenience: transplants let you get a head start on the season or slot the crop into a bed that isn't free yet, while direct sowing saves a step. Sow 10 mm deep and keep moist until germination.

Space plants 45 cm apart with 60 cm between rows. Collards build a fairly wide rosette of leaves over their long harvest life, and crowding them cuts down on airflow, which raises the risk of fungal leaf problems in humid weather — a real consideration for a crop that's often in the ground for months of repeated picking.

## Growing On

Collards are among the most forgiving crops in this guide and tolerate a fair amount of neglect, cold, and inconsistent watering better than any other brassica here. That said, "forgiving" isn't the same as "needs nothing" — a plant that's fed and watered reasonably well will keep producing tender, good-tasting leaves for much longer than one left to fend entirely for itself, which tends to slow down and toughen up early.

Feed lightly every few weeks through the harvest period, since you're continuously removing leaf material and the plant needs to keep replacing it from the same root system for a long time. Consistent moisture keeps new leaves tender; a plant left to dry out repeatedly produces smaller, tougher growth even though it won't outright fail the way a fussier brassica might.

## Harvest

Harvest leaf by leaf, cut-and-come-again style, taking the larger outer leaves first and leaving the smaller inner leaves and the central growing point to keep producing. Leaves are best picked while still a reasonable size and before they get tough, dark, and strongly flavoured with age — don't wait for the whole plant to "finish," since there's no single point of maturity the way there is with a heading crop.

Keep harvesting regularly rather than stripping the plant all at once. Regular picking encourages steady new growth and keeps the leaves you're getting tender, while an occasional heavy strip-down followed by weeks of neglect tends to produce a flush of tough regrowth rather than a steady supply of good eating leaves.

## Succession

Succession sowing at 21-day intervals is useful if you want a rolling supply of young, tender plants coming on, but because collards produce continuously over a long season from a single planting, you don't need to succession sow nearly as aggressively as you would with a fast, one-shot crop like kohlrabi. One or two plantings, picked from regularly, can carry a household through most of the growing season.

## Common Problems

**Cabbage white butterfly caterpillars** — chewed and holed leaves, sometimes heavily, since collards offer plenty of leaf surface over a long season for caterpillars to work through. Net young plants with insect exclusion mesh where practical, and check leaf undersides regularly for eggs — a bit of damage on outer leaves you're about to harvest anyway matters less here than on a crop where you need pristine leaves.

**Aphids** — clusters on the underside of leaves and around new growth, which can build up over the plant's long life if not checked. A strong water jet dislodges most infestations; for a persistent problem, a soap spray on the affected undersides works without harming the harvestable leaves.

**Tough, bitter leaves** — leaves that have gone strongly flavoured and fibrous, usually from being left on the plant too long, from heat stress in summer sowings, or from a plant that's been under-fed or under-watered for an extended period. Harvest leaves at a moderate size rather than waiting for them to get as large as possible, and keep up a basic level of water and feed even though the crop tolerates neglect reasonably well.

## In the Bed

Collards are a Brassicaceae, the same family as broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, brussels sprouts, kohlrabi, romanesco, and kale. Even a tolerant crop like this one shares clubroot risk and other soil-borne brassica problems, so keep to the same rule: don't follow one brassica with another, and leave 3-4 years between brassica plantings in a given bed where possible. Legumes are a good follow-on to rebuild nitrogen after collards have been drawing on the soil over their long harvest period. Because collards can stand productively in a bed for months, they hold that ground for a long stretch — plan the rest of your rotation around that rather than expecting to reclaim the space quickly.
