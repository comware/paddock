## Overview

Romanesco looks like a novelty — the pale lime-green head is made up of spiralling cones that repeat their pattern at smaller and smaller scale, and it's easy to grow it once just to see it. But it's essentially a cauliflower with all of cauliflower's fussiness intact and slightly worse, plus a longer time to maturity, which makes it a crop for a grower who's already comfortable with cauliflower and wants the extra challenge.

Like cauliflower, romanesco forms a single terminal head of undeveloped flower buds, and any interruption to steady growth — heat, drought, cold, transplant shock — shows up later as a small, loose, or discoloured head. The narrower recommended soil temperature range compared to cauliflower reflects just how little tolerance this crop has for heat stress in particular; it wants a genuinely cool, steady run from transplant to harvest.

The single thing that most often goes wrong is treating it like a slightly unusual cauliflower and applying the same margin for error — romanesco has less of that margin, not more, and a grower who's used to cauliflower's fussiness can still be caught out by how much less forgiving this crop is of a hot patch or a missed watering.

## When to Sow

Sow into trays once soil temperature is in the 10-25°C range, aiming for the middle of that band for the fastest and most even germination. In southern Australia, sow in late summer to autumn (February to April) for a winter harvest, which is generally the more reliable window here since it keeps the head-forming stage in cool weather. A very early spring sowing can work for a late spring harvest, but the risk of the head maturing into early heat is higher than with cauliflower.

Sow or transplant too early and expose the young plant to a cold snap, and it can button — forming a tiny premature head on an undersized plant. Time the harvest into hot weather, or subject the plant to any prolonged heat stress while the head is forming, and the spiralling structure that makes romanesco worth growing falls apart into a loose, uneven mass instead of holding its tight geometric form. Given how narrow the tolerance is, err toward the cooler end of the sowing window rather than pushing for an earlier harvest.

## Sowing

Romanesco is grown as a transplant for the same reason as cauliflower — it needs a strong, unchecked start in controlled conditions before the ground is reliably warm enough and stable enough to support it. Sow 10 mm deep and keep the mix evenly moist throughout, since even a brief dry-out at the seedling stage sets up a check that can echo through the whole growing cycle. Seedlings are usually ready to transplant at five to six weeks.

Space plants 50 cm apart with 60 cm between rows, matching cauliflower's spacing needs. Romanesco builds a substantial leaf canopy to support and shade its single head, and crowding cuts into both the size of that canopy and the airflow around the lower leaves — airflow that matters even more here than with other brassicas, since a stressed, humid canopy is exactly the kind of check this fussier crop can't easily shake off.

## Growing On

Consistent conditions matter more for romanesco than for any other crop in this family. Water on a steady schedule and never let the bed dry out hard, especially once the head starts to form — a dry spell at that stage does more damage here than it would to a cauliflower, and the recovery is worse. Feed regularly with a balanced fertiliser through the leafy growth stage, tapering as the head develops.

There's no special blanching step here the way there is with white cauliflower — romanesco's colour holds fine in the sun — but the underlying discipline of steady water and steady feed matters even more than it does for cauliflower, precisely because this crop has less capacity to shrug off a lapse.

## Harvest

Cut the head while the spiralling cones are still tight and the whole head is dense and firm, before any separation or yellowing appears between the segments. Romanesco heads are generally smaller than a typical cauliflower curd, so judge readiness by tightness rather than expecting cauliflower-scale size. Cut through the stem below the head with a sharp knife, leaving a few wrapper leaves for protection.

As with cauliflower, this is a one-cut crop — don't expect a useful second flush of side shoots once the main head is harvested.

## Succession

Succession sow every 21 days through the sowing window, but given how narrow romanesco's tolerance for heat is, two sowings three weeks apart is generally more sensible than stretching a longer run — it limits how much of the harvest is exposed to a late-season heat risk, which is the main way this crop goes wrong at the finish line.

## Common Problems

**Small or loose heads that lose their spiral structure** — the head doesn't bulk up properly or the distinctive geometric pattern falls apart into an uneven mass. This is a growth-check problem, usually heat stress, drought, or an interruption at transplant, and romanesco shows it more readily than cauliflower does. Keep water and feed steady from transplant onward and time the harvest to land in cool weather.

**Bolting or premature flowering** — the plant runs up to flower before forming a proper head, usually from a cold check early on followed by a return to warmth, or from being pushed too hard toward an early harvest. Stick to the recommended sowing window and avoid transplanting stressed or root-bound seedlings.

**Cabbage white butterfly caterpillars** — the same caterpillar pressure as any other brassica, with the added risk that a head this fussy about conditions can't easily recover lost leaf area the way a hardier crop might. Net the bed with insect exclusion mesh from transplanting and check leaf undersides regularly.

## In the Bed

Romanesco is a Brassicaceae, the same family as broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, brussels sprouts, kohlrabi, kale, and collards. Rotate brassicas through a bed no more often than once every 3-4 years to manage clubroot and shared pests, and never follow one brassica directly with another. Legumes are the standard follow-on to rebuild the nitrogen this crop draws down over its growing season. Give romanesco full sun and don't let taller crops shade it — its narrow tolerance for stress means it needs the best conditions in the bed, not whatever's left over.
