## Overview

Kale is the brassica to start with if the rest of this family sounds like more trouble than you want. There's no curd to protect, no head to time perfectly, no risk of splitting or buttoning — you're growing leaves, picking them as you need them, and the plant just keeps making more. It tolerates cold better than almost anything else in the vegetable garden, actually improving in flavour after a frost, and shrugs off a fair bit of neglect that would set back a fussier crop.

The trade-off for all that toughness is that kale isn't forgiving of being left completely unmanaged for months on end — leaves get large, tough, and strongly flavoured if they're never picked, and a plant that's never fed will still survive but will produce noticeably smaller, less tender growth over time. Kale rewards a little bit of regular attention far more than it demands intensive management.

The one thing that occasionally trips people up isn't really a growing mistake — it's over-thinking a crop that mostly just wants to be left to get on with it, while forgetting the basic habit of picking regularly, which is what actually keeps the leaves at their best.

## When to Sow

Sow or direct sow once soil temperature is in the 7-30°C range, which covers most of the growing season here. In southern Australia, kale can go in from late winter through to autumn (August to April), and it's genuinely one of the most flexible crops in this guide for timing — it tolerates cold far better than heat, so autumn sowings for a winter harvest tend to produce the sweetest, most tender leaves, while spring and summer sowings will still grow but may need more frequent watering and produce slightly tougher leaves in hot stretches.

There's no sharp penalty for sowing a little early or late the way there is with cauliflower or brussels sprouts — kale's main limits are a hard frost eventually damaging very young seedlings, and sustained extreme heat toughening and slowing growth. Within the wide window this crop tolerates, timing is more about matching the harvest to when you want tender leaves than avoiding a failure.

## Sowing

Kale can be direct sown or started in trays and transplanted, and both work well since kale doesn't suffer much transplant shock. Direct sowing is the simpler option for most home growers; starting in trays is worth it if you want an early jump on the season or need to fit the crop into a bed that's still occupied. Sow 10 mm deep and keep moist until germination, which is usually quick and reliable across most of the temperature range this crop tolerates.

Space plants 40 cm apart with 50 cm between rows. Kale forms a fairly open, upright rosette rather than a dense head, so it's more tolerant of tighter spacing than most of this family, but crowding still reduces airflow and can encourage fungal leaf spotting in humid weather, plus it caps how large and productive each individual plant can get over its long harvest life.

## Growing On

Kale is the most forgiving crop in this family and will tolerate a fair amount of cold, neglect, and inconsistent watering without failing outright. That doesn't mean it doesn't respond to good care — a kale plant that's fed every few weeks and watered reasonably consistently produces noticeably more tender, better-flavoured leaves over a longer period than one left entirely to its own devices.

Feed lightly through the harvest period since, like collards, you're continuously removing leaf material that the plant needs to keep replacing. Kale actually improves in flavour after exposure to frost, so don't rush to protect it from cold in a way you would with a heat-loving crop — a bit of winter chill is doing the plant's flavour a favour.

## Harvest

Harvest leaf by leaf, cut-and-come-again style, taking the larger outer leaves and leaving the smaller inner leaves and central growing point to keep producing. Pick regularly rather than letting leaves sit and get large — younger, smaller leaves are more tender, and older leaves left too long on the plant get tough, fibrous, and stronger in flavour.

Because kale keeps producing from the same growing point for a long time, regular picking is what keeps a planting productive. A plant that's picked steadily will keep pushing out new tender growth for months; one that's ignored for long stretches and then stripped all at once tends to bounce back more slowly.

## Succession

Succession sowing every 21 days is useful mainly if you want a rolling supply of young, tender leaves specifically, or if you're growing kale as a baby-leaf salad crop rather than a mature cut-and-come-again plant. For the more typical approach of growing a smaller number of plants and picking from them steadily over a long season, one or two plantings will carry most households through several months without needing to succession sow aggressively.

## Common Problems

**Cabbage white butterfly caterpillars** — chewed, holed leaves, sometimes extensively, since kale's long season and abundant leaf area give caterpillars plenty to work with. Net young plants with insect exclusion mesh where practical, and check leaf undersides for eggs — minor damage on leaves you'll harvest soon anyway is more tolerable here than on a crop where every leaf needs to look pristine.

**Aphids** — grey-green colonies clustering on the underside of leaves and around new growth, which can build up steadily over a long-standing planting if ignored. A strong water jet dislodges most infestations early; for a persistent problem, a soap spray applied to the underside of affected leaves works without harming leaves you plan to eat.

**Tough, bitter leaves from letting the plant go too long unpicked** — leaves that have grown large, dark, and strongly flavoured because they weren't harvested at a reasonable size. This isn't a disease or pest problem, just a habit issue — pick regularly at a moderate leaf size rather than letting the plant accumulate large, ageing leaves.

## In the Bed

Kale is a Brassicaceae, the same family as broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, brussels sprouts, kohlrabi, romanesco, and collards. Even though kale is tough, it still carries the same clubroot and soil-borne disease risk as the rest of the family, so hold to the same rotation discipline: don't follow one brassica with another, and leave 3-4 years between brassica plantings in a bed where you can manage it. Legumes make a good follow-on crop to rebuild nitrogen after kale's long feeding period. Kale can hold a bed productively for months at a time, so factor that into your rotation planning rather than expecting to reclaim the space quickly.
