## Overview

Cauliflower has a reputation for being the fussy brassica, and it earns it. Unlike broccoli, which will forgive an uneven watering schedule or a cold snap with a slightly smaller head, cauliflower punishes any interruption to steady growth — a check from cold, drought, transplant shock, or a nutrient gap can show up weeks later as a small, discoloured, or bitter curd, sometimes with no other warning sign along the way.

The curd itself is undeveloped flower tissue, tightly packed and, in white varieties, protected from the sun by the plant's own leaves. That protection is also the grower's job for part of the cycle: white curds exposed to direct sun will yellow and toughen, so most white varieties need their outer leaves tied or folded over the curd once it starts to show.

The single thing that most often goes wrong is exactly the "check" problem described above — a gardener transplants seedlings that got a bit root-bound in their punnet, or lets the bed dry out hard once during head formation, and ends up with a curd the size of a fist instead of a dinner plate. Cauliflower rewards consistency more than any other input.

## When to Sow

Sow into trays once soil temperature sits in the 10-30°C range; germination is most reliable in the middle of that band, around 20-25°C. In cool-temperate southern Australia the sowing window is a continuous run from late winter through to early autumn rather than two separate blocks. What changes across it is the risk: an early spring sowing matures into rising heat, while a late summer to autumn sowing matures through winter, and most growers here find the winter-maturing crop much easier because it avoids the curd forming just as the heat arrives. Given how little cauliflower tolerates a check, that is the end of the run to favour.

Sow or transplant too early and a cold snap while the plant is small can trigger "buttoning" — a tiny premature curd on an undersized plant that never develops properly. Transplant too late, or let maturity fall in a hot spell, and the curd can turn grainy and loose or bitter and discoloured from heat stress. Cauliflower has less tolerance for either mistake than broccoli does, which is most of why it has the harder reputation.

## Sowing

Cauliflower is raised as a transplant because it needs a strong, unchecked start before it goes into the ground, and starting in trays lets you control moisture and temperature far more tightly than you can in open soil early in the season. Sow 10 mm deep and keep the mix evenly moist without ever letting it dry out — this is the first point in the crop's life where a check can cost you the final curd. Seedlings are usually ready to transplant at five to six weeks, and should go out before they become root-bound in their cell or punnet.

Space plants 50 cm apart with 60 cm between rows, slightly more room than broccoli, because cauliflower plants build a larger leaf canopy relative to their curd size and need it to protect and feed that single curd properly. Crowded plants produce noticeably smaller curds and are more prone to disease from poor airflow, since the dense canopy holds humidity around the lower leaves.

## Growing On

Cauliflower needs steady, uninterrupted growth from transplant to harvest more than it needs any particular feeding regime — even moisture and even nutrition matter more here than in any other brassica. Feed regularly with a balanced fertiliser through the vegetative stage, and never let the bed dry out hard, especially once the curd starts forming; a single bad dry spell at that stage is enough to stunt or discolour the curd for good.

As soon as the curd becomes visible — a small white or coloured patch appearing in the centre of the plant — check white varieties daily and tie or fold the surrounding leaves loosely over the top to keep sun off it. Sun-exposed curd yellows, toughens, and can develop an off flavour. Coloured and purple varieties don't need this step; some are bred specifically to not require blanching.

## Harvest

Cut the curd while it's still tight, dense, and fully closed, with no separation between the individual florets and no yellowing — once you can see gaps opening up between segments it's past its best and will only get looser and more bitter from there. Size varies with variety and conditions, so judge by tightness and colour rather than diameter alone. Cut through the stem below the curd with a sharp knife, leaving a few wrapper leaves attached for protection until you're ready to use it.

Unlike broccoli, cauliflower doesn't reliably produce useful side shoots after the main curd is cut — this is a one-cut crop, so harvest at the right moment and don't expect a second flush.

## Succession

Succession sow every 21 days through the part of the run you are using. Because cauliflower is less forgiving of a mistimed harvest falling into heat, two sowings spaced three weeks apart in the same window is usually more reliable in this climate than trying to stretch a longer run, since it gives you a shorter overall harvest period to manage rather than a long tail exposed to more weather risk.

## Common Problems

**Small, loose, or grainy curds** — the curd never bulks up properly, or it's separated and coarse rather than dense. This is almost always a growth check somewhere along the line: drought stress, root disturbance at transplant, a cold snap, or a nutrient gap. Prevent it by hardening off transplants gently, watering consistently from the day they go in, and feeding on a regular schedule rather than sporadically.

**Yellowed or discoloured curd** — the curd has gone from white to yellow or brown-tinged before harvest. In white varieties this is almost always sun exposure once the curd was visible and not yet covered. Tie or fold outer leaves over the curd as soon as it appears, checking every couple of days as the plant grows and the cover can shift or open up.

**Cabbage white butterfly caterpillars** — ragged holes in the leaves and, in bad cases, caterpillars burrowing into the curd itself, which ruins it outright. Check leaf undersides regularly for eggs and caterpillars, or net the bed with insect exclusion mesh from transplanting — this is more reliable protection for the curd than trying to spray or hand-pick after damage starts.

## In the Bed

Cauliflower is a Brassicaceae, sharing the family with broccoli, cabbage, brussels sprouts, kohlrabi, romanesco, kale, and collards. Rotate brassicas through any given bed no more often than once every 3-4 years to manage clubroot and other family-specific soil pests, since none of these crops should follow each other directly. Legumes are the classic follow-on crop, rebuilding the nitrogen that cauliflower — a heavy feeder for its whole cycle — draws down heavily. Give cauliflower full sun and don't crowd it under taller crops; its large leaf canopy needs light to keep the curd developing evenly.
