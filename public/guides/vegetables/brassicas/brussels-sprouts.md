## Overview

Brussels sprouts are a commitment crop. They take longer in the ground than any other brassica here, grow into a tall, top-heavy plant that needs physical support, and reward patience with a harvest that keeps giving through the coldest part of the year, which is exactly when fresh vegetables are otherwise thin on the ground. This is not a crop for a small or short-season bed.

The sprouts themselves are small buds that form in the leaf axils along the main stem, starting from the bottom and working up as the plant grows taller over months. Frost doesn't damage them — it improves them. A touch of cold converts some of the plant's starches to sugars, which is why sprouts picked after the first proper frosts of the season taste noticeably better and less bitter than sprouts picked from a plant that never experienced real cold.

The single thing that most often goes wrong is impatience or under-preparation for the length of the crop: growers plant too late for the plant to size up before winter, don't stake a tall, top-heavy plant that then blows over in wind, or crowd several plants into a space that can only really support one properly finished stalk.

## When to Sow

Sow into trays once soil temperature is in the 10-25°C range, ideally toward the cooler half of that band — brussels sprouts don't want summer heat during their long establishment phase. In southern Australia, sow in late spring to early summer (November to December) for transplanting out in mid to late summer, giving the plant a long cool autumn and winter to bulk up and produce sprouts.

This is a crop where getting the timing right matters more than almost any other brassica, because the whole point is a long, cool growing season. Sow or transplant too late and the plant won't have time to build the height and leaf mass it needs before cold weather slows growth right down, leaving you with a short stalk and few usable sprouts. Sow too early into hot conditions and the plant can struggle, stall, or bolt before it's built a proper framework — and without a long cool run through autumn and winter, the sprouts that do form tend to stay loose and never tighten up properly.

## Sowing

Brussels sprouts are grown as a transplant because the long total time in the ground makes it worth giving the plant a strong, controlled start in trays before committing bed space to it for months. Sow 10 mm deep and keep evenly moist; seedlings are usually ready to go out at five to six weeks.

Space plants 60 cm apart with 70 cm between rows — more room than any other brassica here, and for good reason. Brussels sprouts grow tall and need real room to develop a sturdy stem and a full leaf canopy over a long season; crowd them and you get thin, weak stalks, competition for light and nutrients over months rather than weeks, and sprouts that stay small and loose instead of tightening up. Given how long this crop occupies the bed, the spacing cost of getting it wrong compounds for the entire season.

## Growing On

This crop needs a long, steady season more than any single input — consistent feeding and watering matter, but the biggest factor in success is simply giving it enough time in cool conditions. Feed regularly through the growing season since the plant is building both height and a full crop of sprouts over months, and keep the soil moisture even; big swings between dry and wet stress a plant that's already carrying a heavy load.

Because brussels sprouts grow tall and carry increasing weight as sprouts form along the stem, staking is close to essential in most gardens — a mature plant loaded with sprouts is genuinely top-heavy and will blow over in wind or lean under its own weight without support. Firm the soil well at planting and stake early rather than waiting until the plant is already leaning. Some growers pinch out the growing tip a few weeks before the first expected harvest to redirect the plant's energy into finishing the existing sprouts rather than continuing to grow taller.

## Harvest

Sprouts mature from the bottom of the stalk upward, so start picking the lowest ones once they're firm, tight, and about 2-3 cm across — don't wait for the whole stalk to finish, since the top sprouts will still be small while the bottom ones are ready. Twist or cut each sprout off cleanly at the stem. As mentioned, sprouts harvested after the first real frosts of the season are noticeably sweeter than sprouts picked before any cold has hit the plant, so it's worth holding off the first pick if a frost is close.

You can keep picking up the stalk over several weeks to a couple of months as more sprouts mature, which is what makes this crop worth the long wait — a single well-grown plant can produce a genuinely large harvest spread over the coldest months.

## Succession

Brussels sprouts are not a succession crop. The whole growing season in this climate is really only long enough to take one planting from transplant through to a full stalk of mature sprouts, so there's no room to stagger multiple sowings the way you would with a faster crop like broccoli. Plant once, at the right time for a long cool run, and harvest progressively from the same plants over an extended period instead of planning repeat sowings.

## Common Problems

**Loose, blown sprouts that never tighten** — the sprouts stay open and leafy instead of forming tight, firm buttons. This is most often caused by too much heat during the growing season, inconsistent growth from a check somewhere along the way, or too much nitrogen pushing soft, fast growth instead of steady development. Prevent it with correct timing so the crop's main growth period falls in cool weather, and keep feeding steady rather than heavy.

**Plants blowing over or leaning heavily** — a tall, sprout-laden stalk toppling in wind or leaning under its own weight, sometimes snapping. This comes down to inadequate support for a crop that gets genuinely top-heavy. Stake plants early, before they lean, and firm the soil well around the base at planting time.

**Aphids in the leaf axils** — grey-green aphid colonies build up in the same axils where sprouts are forming, which is hard to treat once sprouts have started to close around them. Check regularly from early in the season and treat with a strong water jet or soap spray before sprouts form, since it's much harder to reach aphids once they're tucked inside a forming sprout.

## In the Bed

Brussels sprouts are a Brassicaceae, the same family as broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, kohlrabi, romanesco, kale, and collards. Because this crop occupies the bed for so long, plan your rotation around it carefully — don't follow it with any other brassica, and leave 3-4 years between brassica plantings in the same spot to manage clubroot and other family-specific problems. Legumes are the standard follow-on crop to rebuild nitrogen after such a long, heavy feeder has finished. Being the tallest crop in this family, brussels sprouts can shade shorter neighbours for months, so plant it where it won't block sun from the rest of the bed, and remember it holds that ground for the better part of a year.
