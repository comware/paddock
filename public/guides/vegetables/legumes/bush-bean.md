## Overview

Bush bean is the warm-season counterpart to pea and broad bean — where those crops want cold, bush
bean wants genuine warmth and will not germinate reliably, let alone grow well, in cool soil. It's a
compact, self-supporting plant that doesn't need a trellis the way climbing bean does, which makes
it the simpler of the two common beans to fit into a small garden, and like all legumes it fixes its
own nitrogen as it grows.

## When to Sow

Bush bean needs soil temperature of at least 16°C to germinate reliably, and grows on well up to
about 30°C — this is a genuinely warm-season crop with essentially no cold tolerance, and it's cut
down by even a light frost. In temperate southern Australia that means sowing from mid-to-late
spring through summer, roughly October to February, once the risk of frost has passed and soil has
properly warmed.

Sow too early into cold spring soil and seed is likely to rot before germinating rather than simply
germinating slowly, which is a harder failure than the seed-in-cold-soil problems of cool-season
crops. There's little upside to pushing this crop early; wait for the soil to genuinely warm rather
than going by the calendar alone.

## Sowing

Direct sow. Bush bean germinates quickly and reliably once soil is warm enough, and its root system,
along with the nitrogen-fixing nodules that develop on it, establishes best without the disturbance
of transplanting. Sow at 25 mm depth into warm soil; in cooler districts, warming the bed under
cover for a week or two before sowing can help germination along.

## Growing On

Bush bean is largely self-supporting and doesn't need a trellis, though a light stake or surrounding
twiggy support can help in a very exposed site or with a heavily laden plant. Water consistently
through flowering and pod fill — bush bean is shallow-rooted compared to climbing bean and dries out
faster, and moisture stress during flowering is a common cause of poor pod set.

As with other legumes, avoid heavy nitrogen feeding, which favours leaf growth over flowers and
pods; the plant's own nitrogen-fixing capability generally covers its needs once established.

## Harvest

Pick pods while young, firm, and before the seeds inside swell and become visible as bumps along the
pod — a pod that's started to bulge with maturing seeds has usually passed its best eating stage and
turned fibrous. Check the plant every couple of days once cropping starts, since bush bean can move
from tender to tough within a few days in warm weather. Regular picking is essential here too: like
other legumes, bush bean slows or stops flowering once it has matured seed on the plant, so letting
pods go to full maturity for seed will shut down further production.

## Succession

Sow every 14 days through the warm season for a continuous supply, since each bush bean planting has
a relatively concentrated, short cropping period compared to climbing bean's longer season.
Succession sowing also provides useful insurance against the plant's frost sensitivity — if an early
or late planting is caught by an unseasonal cold snap, later or earlier sowings carry the crop
through.

## Common Problems

**Poor germination or seed rot** — caused by sowing into soil that isn't genuinely warm enough yet.
Fix by testing soil temperature or waiting until well after the last frost date before sowing,
rather than going by season name alone.

**Poor pod set** — caused by moisture stress during flowering, or by extreme heat causing flowers to
abort. Fix by watering consistently through flowering and pod development, particularly during hot
spells.

**Production stopping early** — caused by letting pods mature to full size and go stringy or tough
instead of picking regularly. Fix by picking every two to three days once the crop starts cropping.

## In the Bed

Bush bean belongs to the legume family for rotation purposes, alongside pea, broad bean, and
climbing bean — legumes generally rotate well among themselves. Its short occupancy of only 50-60
days makes it a useful warm-season gap-filler, and cutting the spent plants off at soil level after
the final harvest leaves the nitrogen-fixing roots in the ground, giving a real boost to whatever
hungry crop follows.
