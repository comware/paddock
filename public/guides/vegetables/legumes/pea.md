## Overview

Pea is a cool-season legume that fixes its own nitrogen from the air through bacteria living in
nodules on its roots, which means it needs less feeding than most crops and leaves the bed better
off than it found it. It's easy to grow but does need something to climb, since even the shorter
varieties benefit from support, and it stops producing quickly once picking lapses, which makes it
more of an ongoing commitment through harvest than a set-and-forget crop.

## When to Sow

Pea germinates from as low as 5°C, one of the coldest tolerances of any common vegetable, and grows
on comfortably up to about 20°C, after which quality drops off and the plant struggles in heat. In
temperate southern Australia this means pea is a genuinely cool-season crop, sown from late autumn
through winter and into early spring, roughly April to September depending on district, avoiding
only the frostiest depths of winter in the coldest inland areas and the warmth of summer entirely.

Sow too late into spring and rising heat will cut the harvest short and toughen the pods before
you've had much of a crop; pea does not tolerate heat the way beans do, and a late sowing that runs
into a warm spring is one of the more common ways this crop disappoints.

## Sowing

Direct sow. Pea's root system, and the nitrogen-fixing nodules that form on it, establish best
without disturbance, and the seed itself germinates reliably enough direct that there's little
reason to raise seedlings under cover except to get a slightly earlier start in a very cold
district. Sow at 25 mm depth — deeper than most seed, since pea seed is large with substantial
reserves to push up from that depth, and the extra depth also helps anchor the seedling once it
starts climbing.

## Growing On

Give pea something to climb from an early stage, even for varieties described as dwarf or bush types
— nearly all peas benefit from some support, whether that's a simple trellis of twiggy branches,
netting, or string strung between stakes. Sprawling, unsupported plants produce pods that are harder
to find, more prone to rot where they touch the ground, and more vulnerable to pests.

Because pea fixes nitrogen from the air via bacteria in its root nodules, it doesn't need heavy
feeding, particularly not with nitrogen-rich fertiliser, which can actually push the plant toward
leafy growth at the expense of flowering and pod set. A modest, balanced feed is enough; the crop is
largely self-sufficient on that front.

Keep picking. Pea, like most legumes, is genetically driven to keep flowering and setting pods only
as long as it hasn't yet succeeded in maturing seed — leave pods on the vine to mature and dry, and
the plant reads that as job done and slows or stops producing new flowers. Regular picking, even of
pods you don't immediately need, keeps the plant in production mode for longer.

## Harvest

Pick pods once they're plump and well filled but still bright green and glossy — a dull, faded pod
has usually gone past its best and the peas inside will have started to toughen and starch up. Snow
pea and snap types are picked earlier, while pods are still flat or just filling, but shelling pea
benefits from waiting until the pods feel properly full when squeezed gently.

## Succession

Sow every 14 days through the cool-season window for a rolling harvest rather than one glut followed
by nothing — pea's relatively short cropping period per sowing (a few weeks of picking once it
starts) means successive sowings matter more here than for a crop that produces steadily over a long
season.

## Common Problems

**Poor pod set with lots of flowers** — caused by heat stress, since pea flowers can abort in warm
conditions without setting pods. Fix by sowing earlier in the cool-season window so flowering
happens before the weather warms.

**Powdery mildew (white coating on leaves)** — caused by humid conditions, often appearing as the
crop ages toward the end of its season or in still, damp weather. Fix by improving airflow with
adequate spacing and support, and consider it a sign the crop is winding down rather than something
worth aggressive treatment late in the season.

**Production slowing or stopping** — caused by letting pods mature and dry on the vine instead of
picking regularly. Fix by picking every few days once the crop starts, even pods slightly past ideal
size, to keep the plant flowering.

## In the Bed

Pea belongs to the legume family for rotation purposes, alongside broad bean, bush bean, and
climbing bean — legumes can generally follow each other reasonably well since they don't share many
pests with other families, though rotating among them still helps limit legume-specific soil
diseases. After harvest, cut the spent plants off at soil level rather than pulling them, leaving
the nitrogen-rich roots in the ground to break down and feed the next crop — a hungry leafy green or
brassica planted afterward makes good use of that boost.
