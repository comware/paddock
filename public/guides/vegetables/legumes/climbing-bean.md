## Overview

Climbing bean is the taller, longer-cropping relative of bush bean, needing the same genuine warmth
to get going but rewarding the extra height and support with a longer, steadier harvest from the
same footprint of ground. Like all beans it's a warm-season crop with no frost tolerance, and like
all legumes it fixes its own nitrogen as it grows.

## When to Sow

Climbing bean needs soil temperature of at least 16°C to germinate well, and grows on happily up to
30°C. In temperate southern Australia that means sowing from mid-to-late spring through summer,
roughly October to February, once frost risk has passed and the soil has genuinely warmed — sowing
into cold soil is more likely to rot the seed outright than to simply slow it down, so there's
little to gain from rushing an early sowing.

## Sowing

Direct sow. Climbing bean's root system, including the nitrogen-fixing nodules that develop as the
plant grows, establishes best undisturbed, and the large seed germinates reliably direct into warm
soil. Sow at 25 mm depth, ideally already alongside the support structure the plant will need, since
installing a trellis around an established seedling risks damaging roots.

## Growing On

Support is not optional the way it sometimes is for bush bean or dwarf pea — climbing bean is a true
vine and will sprawl unproductively without something substantial to climb, whether that's a tall
trellis, a wigwam of stakes, or a length of netting strung between posts. Put the support up before
or at sowing so the young plant can start climbing as soon as it's tall enough, rather than flopping
over while support is added later.

Water consistently, particularly through flowering and while pods are filling, since climbing bean's
longer cropping season means moisture stress at any point during it can dent a stretch of production
rather than affecting just one flush. As with other legumes, avoid heavy nitrogen feeding, which
pushes leafy growth at the expense of flowers and pods.

## Harvest

Pick pods while young and before the seeds inside swell visibly along the pod — the same test as
bush bean, though climbing bean tends to hold at a good eating stage a little longer once conditions
are settled and warm. Check plants every two to three days once cropping starts. Regular picking
matters even more here than for the shorter-cropping bush bean, since climbing bean's whole
advantage is a long productive season, and that season shortens fast if pods are allowed to mature
to full seed.

## Succession

Sow every 21 days if a staggered start is wanted, though because a single climbing bean planting
already crops over a long period, successive sowings matter less here than for bush bean's shorter,
more concentrated harvest — one well-tended planting can supply a household for much of the warm
season provided picking stays regular.

## Common Problems

**Sprawling, unproductive growth** — caused by inadequate or late-installed support. Fix by putting
a tall, sturdy trellis or stakes in place at sowing time, not after the plant is already flopping.

**Poor pod set in hot weather** — caused by extreme heat during flowering, which can cause flowers
to abort. Fix by ensuring consistent watering through hot spells; there's limited ability to prevent
flower drop in genuinely extreme heat, but stress from drought compounds it.

**Production slowing over the season** — caused by allowing pods to mature to full seed instead of
picking regularly, which signals the plant to stop producing. Fix by picking every two to three days
throughout the cropping season, which is what sustains climbing bean's long productive advantage
over bush bean.

## In the Bed

Climbing bean belongs to the legume family for rotation purposes, alongside pea, broad bean, and
bush bean — legumes generally rotate well among themselves. Its longer occupancy of 60-70 days plus
an extended picking season means it can hold a bed and its support structure for much of the warm
months, but cutting spent plants off at soil level and leaving the nitrogen-fixing roots in place
gives a real head start to whatever crop follows, particularly a hungry leafy or fruiting crop
planted afterward.
