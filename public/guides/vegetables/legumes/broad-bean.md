## Overview

Broad bean is the cold-hardiest of the common bean and pea crops, tolerant enough of frost that it's
sown in autumn to overwinter as a young plant and crop the following spring — the opposite pattern
to bush and climbing beans, which are strictly warm-season. Like other legumes it fixes its own
nitrogen, and it's an easy, low-maintenance crop provided it's given room and, in many gardens, some
support against wind.

## When to Sow

Broad bean germinates from as low as 5°C and grows on up to about 20°C, one of the widest cold
tolerances of any common vegetable — it's genuinely happy growing through a cool winter. In
temperate southern Australia, sow in autumn, roughly March to May, so plants establish before the
coldest weather and then grow on through winter to flower and crop in spring, typically 80-100 days
after sowing but effectively spanning the whole cool season from an autumn planting.

This autumn-for-spring timing is worth being explicit about because it runs opposite to the other
beans in this set: broad bean is planted specifically to sit through the cold months, using the cool
season to build a strong plant before cropping, rather than avoiding cold the way bush and climbing
bean do.

## Sowing

Direct sow. Broad bean's root system, including its nitrogen-fixing nodules, establishes best
without the disturbance of transplanting, and the seed is large and vigorous enough to germinate
reliably straight into the ground. Sow at 40 mm depth, the deepest of the common legumes, reflecting
the seed's large size and substantial food reserve.

## Growing On

Broad bean plants grow tall and can become top-heavy, especially in a windy site, so staking or
running a string along both sides of a row to hold plants upright is worth doing in exposed gardens,
even though the crop doesn't climb the way pea or climbing bean does. Pinch out the growing tips
once the lowest flower trusses have set pods — this is a broad-bean-specific trick that both
discourages aphids, which favour the soft new growth at the tips, and redirects the plant's energy
into filling pods rather than continuing to grow taller.

Like other legumes, broad bean fixes nitrogen from the air and needs little additional feeding;
avoid heavy nitrogen fertiliser, which encourages leafy growth over flowering and pod set.

## Harvest

Pick pods once they feel full when gently squeezed but before they turn leathery and dark — young,
small pods can be cooked whole, while larger ones are shelled for the beans inside. Left too long,
pods toughen considerably and the beans inside develop a tough outer skin that many people prefer to
remove before eating. Because the crop flowers and sets pods progressively up the stem over several
weeks, pods are ready in batches rather than all at once.

## Succession

Broad bean is not a succession crop. It's sown once in autumn for a single extended spring harvest,
and its long season from sowing to cropping — spanning the entire cool season — means there's no
practical way to stagger sowings for a rolling supply the way a fast summer crop allows.

## Common Problems

**Aphid infestation on tips** — chocolate-brown or black aphid clusters on the soft growing tips, a
very common broad bean problem, especially once flowering begins. Fix by pinching out the tips once
the lowest pods have set, which removes the aphids' favoured feeding site along with the surplus
growth.

**Chocolate spot (brown blotches on leaves)** — a fungal disease favoured by humid, still,
overcrowded conditions. Fix by spacing plants adequately for airflow and avoiding overly wet, poorly
drained soil.

**Poor pod set low on the plant** — caused by cold snaps during flowering affecting pollination, or
by overly vigorous leafy growth from too much nitrogen. Fix by avoiding nitrogen-rich fertiliser and
accepting that a hard frost during flowering may reduce the lowest trusses regardless.

## In the Bed

Broad bean belongs to the legume family for rotation purposes, alongside pea, bush bean, and
climbing bean — legumes generally rotate well among themselves. Its long occupancy from autumn to
spring, roughly 80-100 days of active growth stretched across the cool season, ties up a bed for
much of the year, but cutting spent plants off at soil level and leaving the nitrogen-fixing roots
in place sets up whatever follows — typically a hungry summer crop — with a real head start.
