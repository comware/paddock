# Snow Pea

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Easy |
| **Days to Maturity** | 60-70 |
| **Sowing Method** | Direct |
| **Plant Spacing** | 5 cm |
| **Row Spacing** | 45 cm |
| **Sowing Depth** | 25 mm |
| **Soil Temperature** | 5-20°C |
| **Succession Interval** | 14 days |

## Overview

Snow pea is grown for the flat, edible pod rather than the seed inside it, which changes the harvest
timing compared to a shelling pea but very little else about how the plant is grown. Like all peas
it fixes its own nitrogen from the air and needs cool weather to crop well, and like all peas it
stops producing if picking falls behind.

## When to Sow

Snow pea germinates from as low as 5°C and grows on well up to about 20°C, the same cool-season
range as shelling pea. In temperate southern Australia that means sowing from late autumn through
winter into early spring, roughly April to September depending on district, keeping clear of the
coldest weeks in frost-prone inland areas and avoiding summer heat entirely, which toughens pods and
cuts the crop short.

A late sowing that runs into a warming spring is the most common way this crop underperforms — pods
that would have been tender in cool weather come on fibrous and tough once daytime temperatures
climb, and flowering slows or stops in genuine heat.

## Sowing

Direct sow. Snow pea's root system establishes best undisturbed, and the seed germinates reliably
enough direct that raising seedlings under cover offers little benefit except a slightly earlier
start in a very cold district. Sow at 25 mm depth, deep enough to draw on the seed's substantial
reserves and anchor the young plant before it starts to climb.

## Growing On

Provide support from early on — snow pea, like other peas, climbs by tendril and produces a cleaner,
easier-to-pick crop when trained up netting, trellis, or twiggy stakes rather than left to sprawl.
Sprawling plants hide pods, and pods left too long unnoticed on the ground are more prone to rot and
slug damage.

Because snow pea fixes its own nitrogen, avoid heavy nitrogen feeding, which pushes leafy growth at
the expense of flowers and pods. Keep picking regularly once the crop starts — like all peas, snow
pea is driven to keep flowering only until it has matured seed, so a build-up of overripe, unpicked
pods signals the plant to slow down.

## Harvest

Pick snow pea while the pod is still flat, before the peas inside have swelled noticeably — this is
the key difference from a shelling pea, where you wait for the peas to fill out. A snow pea pod left
to fill out toughens and loses the tender, crisp quality it's grown for, so check the crop every
couple of days once flowering is well underway, since pods move from ready to overripe quickly in
warm weather.

## Succession

Sow every 14 days through the cool-season window to keep a steady supply, since each sowing has a
relatively short productive picking period before heat or exhaustion slows it down. Because snow pea
needs picking so frequently at the ideal stage, staggered sowings also spread the workload rather
than presenting one large flush that all needs picking at once.

## Common Problems

**Tough, fibrous pods** — caused by leaving pods too long before picking, or by heat stress
toughening pods generally. Fix by picking more frequently, every two to three days once cropping
starts, and sowing early enough to finish before serious heat arrives.

**Poor pod set** — caused by heat stress causing flowers to abort before setting pods. Fix by sowing
earlier in the cool-season window.

**Powdery mildew** — a white coating on leaves, caused by humid, still conditions, often late in the
crop's life. Fix by ensuring good airflow through adequate spacing and support; a mildewed crop
nearing the end of its season is often not worth treating, just picking out.

## In the Bed

Snow pea belongs to the legume family for rotation purposes, alongside shelling pea, broad bean,
bush bean, and climbing bean — legumes generally rotate well among themselves, though varying which
legume follows which still helps limit legume-specific soil diseases. Cut spent plants off at soil
level after harvest rather than pulling them, leaving the nitrogen-fixing roots to break down in
place; a leafy green or brassica planted next makes good use of the nitrogen left behind.
