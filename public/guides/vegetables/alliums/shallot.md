## Overview

Shallot is planted from small bulbs rather than seed, much like garlic, and each planted bulb
multiplies into a cluster of several new bulbs by harvest rather than swelling into one large bulb
the way onion does. It shares onion's day-length sensitivity to some degree, but because the parent
bulb is already established with its own reserves, shallot is somewhat more forgiving of an
imperfect sowing date than onion grown from seed.

## When to Sow

Plant shallot bulbs once soil temperature is in the 10-20°C range, which in temperate southern
Australia is generally autumn to early winter, roughly March to June depending on district and
variety — similar timing to garlic, though some varieties can also be planted in early spring for a
later harvest. Autumn planting gives the crop a long, cool establishment period before the main
bulking-up happens through spring.

As with onion, shallot's bulbing response is influenced by day length, so a wildly mistimed planting
can affect how well the clusters divide and size up, though the effect is generally less severe than
the outright bulbing failure a badly matched onion variety can suffer, since the shallot bulb is
already dividing from established tissue rather than starting from a seedling.

## Sowing

Shallot is grown from small bulbs, not true seed. Push each bulb into loosened soil at around 30 mm
depth, pointed end up, leaving the very tip at or just below the surface — deep enough to anchor the
bulb and keep it moist, shallow enough that it isn't smothered while trying to sprout. As with
garlic, planting stock quality matters: firm, disease-free bulbs from a reliable source give a much
better result than saved bulbs from a poor previous crop.

## Growing On

Keep the bed weed-free, particularly while the young shoots are thin and easily outcompeted. Water
through active growth but ease off as the clusters begin to bulk up and mature, since — as with the
other storage alliums — wet soil late in the season promotes rot rather than helping the bulbs
finish. Each planted bulb will divide into a cluster of anywhere from a handful to a dozen new bulbs
depending on variety and growing conditions, so don't be alarmed to see multiple shoots emerging
from a single planting point.

## Harvest

Watch the foliage the way you would for onion or garlic: as the tops begin to yellow and flop, the
crop is finishing up. Lift the whole cluster together rather than trying to harvest individual bulbs
from it while still in the ground, since they're joined at the base until lifted. As with garlic,
it's generally better to lift slightly before the tops are fully dead and dried than to leave the
crop in wet soil too long afterward.

## Succession

Shallot is not a succession crop. It's planted once in autumn or early winter and harvested as a
single crop after 100-120 days, with no practical benefit to staggering plantings given the crop's
storage life once cured — a well-cured shallot harvest keeps for months, doing the job a succession
sowing would otherwise do.

## Common Problems

**Poor bulb division** — caused by mistimed planting relative to day length, or by planting stock
that was already stressed or diseased. Fix by planting within the recommended autumn window and
sourcing firm, healthy bulbs.

**Bulb rot** — caused by wet soil, especially late in the season as bulbs are finishing, or by
planting into poorly drained ground. Fix by tapering off watering as tops begin to yellow and
improving drainage if the bed holds water.

**Rust and other allium leaf diseases** — caused by humid conditions and crowded plantings. Fix by
giving plants adequate spacing and airflow.

## In the Bed

Shallot belongs to the allium family for rotation purposes, alongside onion, garlic, and leek —
avoid following any of these with another allium to limit shared soil-borne disease. Its occupancy
of 100-120 days through the cooler months leaves a reasonable stretch of the growing season free
afterward for a summer crop, and the bed is generally left in good condition for whatever follows.
