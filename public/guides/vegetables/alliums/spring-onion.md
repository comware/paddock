# Spring Onion

|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Easy |
| **Days to Maturity** | 60-80 |
| **Sowing Method** | Direct |
| **Plant Spacing** | 5 cm |
| **Row Spacing** | 15 cm |

| **Soil Temperature** | 10-25°C |

## Overview

Spring onion is grown for the whole young plant — stem and leaves — before bulbing has any chance to
happen, which sidesteps the day-length problem that makes bulb onion so unforgiving. Because you're
harvesting before the plant would even decide whether to bulb, the variety's day-length
classification barely matters here, making spring onion one of the easiest alliums to get right.

## When to Sow

Spring onion germinates from about 10°C and grows well up to 25°C, giving a fairly wide window in
temperate southern Australia — roughly August through to April, with a lull only in the coldest
weeks of winter. Because the crop is harvested young, well before bulbing, there's no critical
window the way there is for bulb onion; sowing a few weeks early or late mostly just shifts the
harvest date rather than risking a failed crop.

Sow into cold soil below 10°C and germination is slow, which mostly just delays harvest rather than
damaging the crop. Sow into peak summer heat without reliable water and growth can slow and toughen
the stems, though the crop still comes through.

## Sowing

Direct sow. Spring onion has a fine, fibrous root system and grows quickly enough from seed that
there's little to gain from transplanting, and disturbing the roots at any stage can check growth
and toughen the stems. Sow seed fairly thickly in narrow bands rather than widely spaced single
rows, since the crop is harvested as a cluster of thin stems rather than as single large bulbs.

Sow at 10 mm depth into a fine, well-prepared seed bed — the seed is small and needs reasonably good
soil contact to germinate evenly, though spring onion is not as fussy about deep or stone-free soil
as a root crop, since almost nothing happens below the surface.

## Growing On

Thin lightly, to about 5 cm, mostly to give the remaining plants enough room to bulk up into usable
stems rather than staying thread-thin. Spring onion is more tolerant of crowding than most alliums,
since a thicker clump just produces thinner individual stems rather than a failed crop, but some
thinning still improves the result. Keep the bed weed-free and evenly watered — like all alliums its
narrow leaves don't shade out competition, so weeds slow it down more than they would a leafier
crop.

## Harvest

Judge readiness by stem thickness rather than any hidden development below ground — spring onion is
ready whenever the white stem base has thickened to a usable size, typically pencil-width or a
little more, which is a visual check rather than a guess about what's happening underground. Because
there's no bulbing event to wait for, you can start harvesting the largest stems from a row and
leave smaller ones to catch up, effectively thinning as you eat.

## Succession

Sow every 21 days for a continuous supply — spring onion doesn't store well once pulled, wilting
within days, so it's a crop best grown to be used fresh and continuously rather than in one large
batch. Because each sowing matures reasonably quickly and the harvest window is flexible, it's one
of the easier crops to keep in rolling succession through most of the growing season.

## Common Problems

**Thin, weak stems** — caused by overcrowding or nutrient-poor soil. Fix by thinning to give plants
a little more room and ensuring the bed has reasonable fertility before sowing.

**Tough, fibrous stems** — caused by drought stress or letting the crop grow too long before
harvest. Fix by watering consistently and harvesting at a younger stage if toughness has been a
recurring issue.

**Rust (orange-brown spots on leaves)** — caused by humid conditions and poor air circulation,
common in crowded plantings. Fix by improving spacing and airflow, and avoid overhead watering late
in the day when foliage stays wet overnight.

## In the Bed

Spring onion belongs to the allium family for rotation purposes, alongside bulb onion, garlic, leek,
and shallot — avoid following any of these with another allium to limit shared soil-borne disease.
Its short occupancy of 60-80 days and shallow root system make it an easy crop to fit into gaps
between longer-season plantings, and it leaves the bed in good condition for almost anything that
follows.
