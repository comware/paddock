## Overview

Onion is demanding for one reason above all others: it decides how big a bulb to make based on day
length, not on how big or healthy the plant looks, and that decision is largely out of your hands
once you've chosen a variety and a sowing date. Get the variety-to-daylight match wrong and you can
do everything else right and still end up with a plant that never bulbs at all — just a thick green
stem sitting on top of a thin root.

## When to Sow

Onion seed germinates from about 10°C and grows on up to 25°C, but soil temperature here is
secondary to day length, which is the trigger the plant actually uses to decide when to stop growing
leaves and start swelling a bulb. Onion varieties are bred as short-day, intermediate-day, or
long-day types, referring to the number of daylight hours needed to trigger bulbing — short-day
varieties bulb at around 11-12 hours of daylight, long-day types need 14-16 hours.

Southern Australia's latitude means most home gardeners want short-day to intermediate-day
varieties, sown in autumn to overwinter as small plants and bulb up as days lengthen through spring
— roughly March to May sowing for an spring-to-summer harvest. Long-day varieties, bred for the much
longer summer daylight hours of higher-latitude regions like northern Europe or Canada, often fail
to bulb properly this far from the equator regardless of how well they're grown, because the days
here never get long enough to trigger them. This is the day-length trap: nothing about a poorly
bulbing onion looks wrong while it's growing, so the mismatch is only obvious at harvest, a whole
season too late to fix.

## Sowing

Either method works, and the choice is mostly about how long a head start you want. Direct sowing
suits home gardeners with a long enough season and saves the fuss of raising seedlings, sown thinly
at 10 mm depth. Transplanting from seedlings, or from purchased seedlings, gives a jump on the
season and is the more common commercial approach, especially for the intermediate and longer-season
varieties that need every week they can get before day length forces bulbing.

Unlike a true taproot, onion tolerates transplanting reasonably well because its root system is
fibrous rather than a single storage taproot — the plant doesn't fork or distort the way carrot does
from being moved. Where onion is sensitive is to root disturbance right around transplanting time
itself: handle seedlings gently and don't let roots dry out during the move, since a badly set-back
transplant simply loses growing time it won't get back before bulbing starts on schedule regardless.

## Growing On

Keep the bed weed-free, especially early — onion's narrow, upright leaves cast little shade, so
weeds compete hard for light and water and a weedy onion bed produces smaller bulbs across the
board. Water consistently through leaf growth, then taper off as bulbs begin to swell and again as
tops start to yellow and fall over, since wet conditions late in the season promote rot rather than
helping the bulb along.

Onion needs to build a reasonable amount of leaf growth before day length forces it into bulbing,
because the size of the bulb is roughly proportional to the size of the plant at the moment bulbing
starts — a small, stressed, or late-sown plant will still bulb on schedule, it'll just make a small
bulb. This is why timing the sowing early enough in the window matters as much as picking the right
day-length variety: both variety and timing need to be right for a good result.

## Harvest

Onion tells you it's ready itself: the tops yellow, then flop over and dry off, and that's the sign
to stop watering and let the bulbs finish. Don't judge onion by pulling test bulbs the way you would
a root crop — the tops falling over is a reliable and visible signal that doesn't require digging
anything up to check. Once most of the tops have gone over, lift the bulbs and let them cure.

## Succession

Onion is not a succession crop in the usual sense. Because bulbing is triggered by day length rather
than by time from sowing, staggering sowings a few weeks apart doesn't spread the harvest the way it
does with a crop that matures on its own clock — plants sown at different times within the same
season will still tend to bulb around the same calendar date, since they're all responding to the
same daylight trigger. The practical way to spread onion harvests is by growing varieties with
different day-length requirements or maturity speeds, not by succession sowing the same variety.

## Common Problems

**Failure to bulb at all** — caused almost always by growing a variety with the wrong day-length
requirement for the latitude, most commonly a long-day variety grown in southern Australia. Fix by
checking the day-length type before buying seed or seedlings next season; nothing can rescue a
mismatched crop once it's planted.

**Small bulbs** — caused by weed competition, late sowing that didn't give the plant enough leaf
growth before bulbing was triggered, or overcrowding. Fix by sowing earlier in the window, keeping
the bed weed-free, and spacing to the full 10 cm.

**Bulb rot in storage** — caused by lifting and storing bulbs before the tops have fully dried, or
by bruising bulbs during harvest and curing. Fix by curing thoroughly in a warm, dry, airy spot for
two to three weeks before storage, and handle bulbs carefully to avoid skin damage.

## In the Bed

Onion belongs to the allium family for rotation purposes, alongside garlic, leek, and shallot —
avoid following any of these with another allium, since they share soil-borne diseases like white
rot that can persist in soil for years. Its long occupancy, up to 175 days for longer-season types,
ties up a bed over autumn, winter, and into summer, so plan the following crop with that in mind;
onion leaves the bed in reasonably good structure for a follow-on root or leafy crop.
