# Leek

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Demanding |
| **Days to Maturity** | 120-150 |
| **Sowing Method** | Transplant |
| **Plant Spacing** | 15 cm |
| **Row Spacing** | 30 cm |
| **Sowing Depth** | 10 mm |
| **Soil Temperature** | 10-25°C |
| **Suggested succession** | Not a succession crop |

## Overview

Leek is demanding mainly because of how long it takes and how much handling it needs along the way
to get a long, well-blanched white shaft rather than a short green one. Unlike bulb onion, leek is
not meaningfully day-length sensitive — it doesn't bulb at all, so there's no hidden trigger waiting
to sabotage the crop. The difficulty here is patience and technique, not timing precision.

## When to Sow

Leek seed germinates from about 10°C and grows on well up to 25°C. In temperate southern Australia,
sow from late winter into spring, roughly August to October, to raise seedlings that transplant out
through spring and grow on through the warmer months into a long 120-150 day maturity, giving an
autumn to winter harvest. Because leek is one of the more cold-hardy alliums and holds well in the
ground once mature, timing errors on the early side are more forgiving than for onion — a leek can
sit and wait, whereas an onion has a bulbing deadline it can't be talked out of.

## Sowing

Transplant. Leek seed is small and slow to get going, and raising it in trays or a nursery bed lets
you protect the fine seedlings through their vulnerable early weeks before moving them out once
they're pencil-thick, typically 8-10 weeks after sowing. Unlike a true taproot, leek's fibrous root
system tolerates transplanting well, which is what makes the classic deep-planting technique
possible in the first place.

The technique that makes or breaks a leek crop is planting depth: dig a hole or trench 10-15 cm
deep, drop the seedling in, and don't backfill it — just water it in and let soil wash in gradually
over the following weeks. This buries the base of the stem in darkness and is what produces the long
white shaft leek is grown for; a leek planted shallow like an onion seedling will still grow, but
most of the stem will be green and tougher rather than white and tender.

## Growing On

As the plant grows, continue hilling soil or mulch up around the base every few weeks to extend the
blanched portion further up the stem — this is the ongoing work leek asks for that most other
alliums don't. Keep the bed weed-free and reasonably moist through the long growing season; leek's
narrow leaves don't shade the ground much, so weed pressure can be persistent over a crop that's in
the ground for four to five months.

Leek is generally untroubled by the diseases and pests that can be serious in onion, such as white
rot, though it shares susceptibility with the rest of the allium family and shouldn't be grown on
ground with a recent history of allium disease.

## Harvest

Leek doesn't have a hard maturity signal the way onion's tops falling over does — judge readiness by
shaft thickness, harvesting once the white portion is a useful diameter, generally from 120 days
onward. Because leek holds well in the ground through cold weather without deteriorating, there's no
urgency to harvest the whole bed at once; many gardeners simply pull individual leeks as needed over
winter, effectively using the garden bed as cold storage.

## Succession

Leek is not a succession crop in the usual sense, mainly because of how long each sowing takes from
seed to harvest and how well the mature crop holds in the ground. Rather than staggering several
sowings, most gardeners raise one batch of seedlings and transplant them over a period of a few
weeks, then harvest from that single planting over an extended period through autumn and winter as
needed.

## Common Problems

**Short white shaft, mostly green stem** — caused by shallow planting or insufficient hilling as the
plant grows. Fix by planting deeper next time and hilling regularly through the season; there's no
way to lengthen the white portion of an already-mature plant.

**Slow, stunted early growth** — caused by transplanting seedlings too small or too large, or by a
poor transplant that damaged the root system. Fix by transplanting at pencil thickness and handling
roots gently, keeping them moist during the move.

**Rust (orange pustules on leaves)** — caused by humid conditions and poor airflow, similar to other
alliums. Fix by spacing plants adequately and avoiding wetting foliage late in the day.

## In the Bed

Leek belongs to the allium family for rotation purposes, alongside onion, garlic, and shallot —
avoid following any of these with another allium to reduce the risk of shared soil-borne disease
building up. Its long occupancy of 120-150 days, plus the practice of leaving mature leeks in the
ground through winter, means it can effectively hold a bed from spring through to the following
spring; plan the bed's next crop with that long timeline in mind.
