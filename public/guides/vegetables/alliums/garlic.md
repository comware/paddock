## Overview

Garlic isn't grown from seed at all in practice — it's grown from cloves, each one a genetic clone
of the parent bulb, planted individually to grow into a new bulb over the cooler months of the year.
It's also one of the few common vegetables that has an explicit cold requirement built into its
biology: most varieties need a period of cold before they'll divide properly into cloves, which is
why garlic is a classic autumn planting for a summer harvest rather than something you can sow
whenever the soil is warm enough.

## When to Sow

Plant cloves once soil temperature has dropped into the 10-20°C range, which in temperate southern
Australia generally means autumn, roughly March to May depending on district — the traditional
guidance of planting around the shortest day has more to do with tradition than precision, but
autumn planting is genuinely correct. This gives the plant winter to establish roots and receive the
vernalisation (cold exposure) that many varieties need to trigger proper bulb division, then a long
run through spring and into summer to bulk up before harvest.

Plant too late in a warm autumn or into a mild winter and the crop may not get enough cold exposure
to divide well, producing a single round bulb instead of a properly segmented head of cloves — a
less severe version of the timing trap that affects bulb onion, though garlic's response to
imperfect timing is a smaller, rounder bulb rather than an outright failure to form one. Hardneck
varieties generally need more cold than softneck types, which matters when choosing what to grow in
a milder district.

## Sowing

Garlic is planted from cloves, not sown from seed, and this is worth being explicit about since
every other allium in this set is grown from true seed. Break a bulb into individual cloves just
before planting, keeping the papery skin on each clove intact, and plant with the pointed end up at
around 50 mm deep — noticeably deeper than the seed-sown alliums, since a clove is a much larger
propagule with its own food reserve to draw on.

Use the largest, healthiest cloves from the outer part of the bulb for planting and eat the smaller
inner ones — clove size at planting has a real effect on final bulb size, more so than with most
seed-grown crops, since you're essentially planting a small stored ration of energy that determines
how strong the start is.

## Growing On

Keep the bed weed-free through the long winter and spring growing period; garlic's narrow leaves
compete poorly with weeds, and a weedy bed produces smaller bulbs. Water through active growth in
spring, then taper off and stop altogether once the crop is close to maturity — like onion, wet soil
around maturing garlic promotes rot rather than helping bulb size, and dry conditions in the final
weeks are what the plant actually wants.

Garlic doesn't need much feeding beyond a reasonably fertile bed to start with, but a nitrogen boost
in early spring while leaves are actively growing can improve bulb size, tapering off well before
harvest.

## Harvest

Garlic signals readiness the way onion does: watch the leaves, and once the lower third to half have
yellowed and started to dry off, it's close to time. Unlike onion, don't wait for garlic's tops to
fall over completely and dry out on the ground — pulling slightly earlier, while a few green leaves
remain, generally gives better-wrapped bulbs with skins that store well, whereas leaving it too long
risks the bulb splitting open in the ground or the wrapper degrading.

## Succession

Garlic is not a succession crop. It's planted once a year in autumn and harvested once, generally in
early to mid summer after 180-240 days in the ground — there's no practical way to stagger plantings
for a rolling harvest given how long each planting takes and how tightly its cycle is tied to the
seasons.

## Common Problems

**Small, poorly divided bulbs** — caused by insufficient cold exposure over winter, using undersized
cloves for planting, or overcrowding. Fix by choosing a variety suited to the local winter chill,
planting the largest cloves available, and keeping to full spacing.

**Bulb rot** — caused by watering too late into the growing season, or by wet, poorly drained soil
generally. Fix by stopping irrigation several weeks before expected harvest and improving drainage
if the bed stays wet.

**Rust (orange spots on leaves)** — caused by humid, still conditions, the same as other alliums.
Fix by spacing plants for airflow and avoiding wetting the foliage, particularly late in the day.

## In the Bed

Garlic belongs to the allium family for rotation purposes, alongside onion, leek, and shallot —
avoid following any of these with another allium, since white rot and other soil-borne allium
diseases can persist in soil for many years once established. Its long occupancy, 180-240 days
spanning autumn through to summer, means the bed is effectively unavailable for most of the year; a
fast-growing summer crop like a leafy green or a legume is a good way to make quick use of the
ground once garlic comes out.
