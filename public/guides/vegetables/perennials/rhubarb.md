## Overview

Rhubarb is a long-lived perennial that, once established, comes back
reliably from the same crown every year for a decade or more with little
attention. It's grown for its stems only — the leaves are toxic and
should never be eaten, which is worth stating clearly since the plant is
otherwise treated casually as a low-maintenance, almost set-and-forget
crop.

Like asparagus, it takes real time to establish, but the wait is
noticeably shorter — a light first-year picking is reasonable, with a
full harvest from the second year onward.

## When to Sow

Rhubarb is most reliably started from a dormant crown division rather
than seed, since crowns establish faster and grow true to variety, while
seed-raised plants are more variable and slower to reach a productive
size. Plant crowns in late winter to early spring once the ground can be
worked and soil temperature sits around 10°C and climbing.

Established rhubarb is genuinely frost-hardy through winter dormancy —
in fact it benefits from a proper cold winter, which many varieties need
to trigger vigorous spring growth, unlike the frost-sensitive crops
elsewhere in this collection.

## Sowing

Plant crowns with the growing buds just at or barely below the soil
surface, roughly 25 mm deep, in soil worked over well beforehand with
compost — since this ground won't be dug over again for years, it's
worth getting the preparation right the first time.

Space plants a generous 90 cm apart with around 100 cm between rows. This
looks like a lot for what starts as a small crown, but a mature rhubarb
plant is genuinely large — a clump a metre or more across at full size is
normal, and this is one of the sizes people most reliably underestimate
when planting.

## Growing On

Feed well each spring as growth resumes, and again through summer — this
is a hungry plant over a long season, and a well-fed crown produces
noticeably thicker, more abundant stems than a neglected one. Water
during dry spells, particularly through the stem-producing months, since
rhubarb wilts and stalls in prolonged drought.

Remove any flower stalks that appear as soon as they're noticed. Flowering
draws energy away from stem production and, left to run to seed, can
weaken the crown's output for the rest of the season.

## Harvest

Take only a light picking in the first year after planting, to let the
crown build up reserves. From the second year, harvest more freely by
pulling stems from the outside of the clump with a gentle twist rather
than cutting, leaving the central, newest growth to keep the plant
producing.

Always twist or cut off the leaf and discard it — the leaf is toxic and
should go in the compost or bin, never the kitchen. Only the stem is
eaten.

## Succession

Rhubarb is not a succession crop. It's a single permanent planting that
crops through a defined spring-into-summer season each year, dies back
over winter, and regrows from the same crown the following spring — a
cycle repeated for the life of the plant rather than something restarted
by new sowings.

## Common Problems

Thin, weak stems over several seasons usually mean the crown is
overcrowded and needs dividing — lifting and splitting an established
clump every four to five years, replanting a healthy division and
discarding the tired centre, generally restores vigour.

Crown rot, most often from waterlogged winter soil, is the main serious
threat — good drainage at planting matters more for rhubarb's longevity
than almost any other factor, since a rotted crown can't be salvaged and
the years already invested in it are lost with it.

## In the Bed

Rhubarb needs a permanent corner it won't be moved from, given the years
it takes to reach full size and the disruption involved in relocating an
established crown. Its size and permanence make it a poor fit for a
rotating annual bed — treat it more like a perennial herb or a small
shrub in the garden's layout than like a vegetable.
