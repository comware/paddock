## Overview

Asparagus asks for patience before it asks for anything else. Planted
now, it gives essentially nothing this year, a light and restrained
harvest next year, and then — from year three onward — a proper harvest
each spring for fifteen to twenty years or more from the same crowns.
That trade is the entire point of the crop, and it needs to be understood
going in, because a gardener expecting spears in the first season will be
disappointed and may pull out perfectly healthy, establishing plants in
frustration. Nothing has gone wrong if year one gives you nothing to eat.

Because it occupies the same ground for decades, its bed deserves more
upfront thought than an annual crop would — soil preparation, drainage,
and placement all matter more here, since none of it can be corrected
without disturbing established crowns later.

## When to Sow

Asparagus is most commonly planted as one-year-old dormant crowns rather
than raised from seed, since crowns save a full year against the already
long establishment period. Plant crowns in late winter to early spring
once the soil has warmed to around 10°C and the worst of the frost risk
has passed, though established asparagus is frost-hardy once dormant for
the season.

Seed-raised asparagus follows the same soil temperature window but adds
a full extra year to reach a size worth transplanting, on top of the
two-to-three-year wait already ahead — worth knowing before choosing that
route over crowns.

## Sowing

Plant crowns in a trench roughly 150 mm deep, spreading the roots out
over a small mound at the trench base, then cover with only a few
centimetres of soil initially — not the full depth. Fill the trench in
gradually over the following weeks as the emerging shoots grow, rather
than burying the crown all at once.

Space crowns 40 cm apart with 90 cm between rows — genuinely wide, since
each crown will spread into a permanent clump over years and the bed
needs airflow for the life of the planting.

## Growing On

Let the ferny top growth run through the first two summers without
cutting any spears at all — this foliage is what feeds the crown for
next year's growth, and removing it early undermines the very
establishment the plant needs. In year three, a light harvest of a few
weeks is reasonable; from year four the bed can be cut for its full
six-to-eight week spring season each year.

Feed well each spring before spears emerge and again after the harvest
window closes, to support the summer foliage that rebuilds the crown for
next year. Keep the bed weeded, since asparagus doesn't compete well
against weeds while young and shares the same root zone with them for
its whole life once established. Cut the dead foliage back hard each
winter once it yellows.

## Harvest

Once established, cut spears at or just below soil level when they reach
15-20 cm, using a knife angled to avoid damaging the crown below.
Harvest daily or every second day in the season's peak, since spears
grow fast in warm weather and quickly pass the tender stage.

Stop harvesting after six to eight weeks each spring regardless of how
vigorously the bed is still producing, and let the remaining spears grow
out into ferny foliage for the rest of the season — this is what rebuilds
the crown for the following year's harvest.

## Succession

Asparagus is not a succession crop in any sense — it's a single
permanent planting, and the entire growing pattern each year is a fixed
cycle of a spring harvest window followed by a summer of foliage growth
that recharges the crown, repeated for the life of the bed.

## Common Problems

The single most common problem is a gardener's own impatience — pulling
out a bed in year one or two because it "isn't producing," when doing
nothing is exactly what the crop needs at that stage. This is worth
saying plainly: no visible harvest for the first one to two years is
normal, not a sign of failure.

Asparagus beetle, a small black-and-orange beetle, can defoliate the
ferny summer growth if left unchecked, which matters because that
foliage is what feeds next year's crop — check plants through summer and
remove beetles and their dark eggs by hand if numbers build. Thin,
spindly spears in an otherwise established bed usually point to
overharvesting in a previous season or crowding that needs thinning.

## In the Bed

Asparagus needs a permanent, undisturbed spot — not a rotating annual
bed — since the crowns are not moved once planted and the whole point of
the crop is a harvest that returns for decades from the same ground.
Underplanting with shallow-rooted, low-growing herbs alongside mature
asparagus is workable since asparagus roots run deep, but avoid anything
that would need the bed dug over or disturbed.
