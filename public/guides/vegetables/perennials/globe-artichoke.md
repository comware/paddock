# Globe Artichoke

## Overview

Globe artichoke is a large, architectural perennial thistle grown for
its immature flower buds, and its size is the thing most people
underestimate before planting one. A mature plant is genuinely enormous —
a metre or more across and nearly as tall, with silvery, deeply cut
foliage — and the 90 by 120 cm spacing here describes the minimum room a
single plant needs, not a generous allowance. Plant one where a small
shrub could go, not where a vegetable would.

Like the other perennials here, it takes a full season or more to
establish before cropping well, then continues to produce from the same
plant for several years.

## When to Sow

Globe artichoke can be raised from seed or, more reliably for a
particular named variety, from a rooted offset or division of an
established plant. Sow seed or plant divisions once soil temperature
sits between 15°C and 25°C, generally spring in temperate southern
Australia.

Seed-raised plants typically don't produce a worthwhile harvest until
their second year, closer to the long end of the maturity window here;
divisions from an established plant, carrying more stored energy
already, can crop more quickly, sometimes within the first year.

## Sowing

Sow seed 15 mm deep, or plant divisions at the same depth they were
growing at previously, taking care not to bury the crown. Whichever
method, give each plant the full 90 cm spacing with 120 cm between rows —
underplanting this crop is one of the most common regrets gardeners
report once the plant reaches its full size in year two or three.

## Growing On

Feed generously through the growing season; this is a large, fast-growing
plant for its size and rewards good feeding with a heavier crop of
larger buds. Water deeply and consistently, particularly through
summer — artichoke doesn't tolerate prolonged drought well despite its
tough, thistle-like appearance.

In genuinely cold districts, cut plants back after autumn and mulch the
crown heavily over winter for frost protection; in milder temperate
areas established plants generally handle winter with little more than a
tidy-up of dead foliage.

## Harvest

Cut flower buds while still tight and closed, before the scales begin to
loosen or any purple flower colour shows through — a bud left too long
toughens and becomes fibrous rather than tender. Cut with several
centimetres of stem attached. The central, terminal bud on each stem is
usually the largest; smaller side buds that follow are still worth
harvesting, just noticeably smaller.

## Succession

Globe artichoke is not a succession crop. It's a single perennial
planting that produces a defined spring-into-summer harvest each year
from an established plant, repeating annually from the same root system
for around five to seven years before productivity typically declines
and the plant is best replaced with a fresh division.

## Common Problems

Small or absent bud production in an otherwise healthy-looking plant
most often points to insufficient feeding or an immature plant still in
its first year — patience and consistent feeding resolve this more often
than any other intervention.

Aphids commonly colonise the tightly packed bud scales, hiding well
enough that they're easy to miss until the bud is cut open — checking
buds closely and treating early, before scales are fully packed, is more
effective than trying to dislodge an established colony later. Slugs and
snails are drawn to the soft new spring growth on emerging plants and can
set a young plant back significantly if left unchecked.

## In the Bed

Globe artichoke's size makes it a poor fit for a shared vegetable bed
except at the very back or a dedicated corner, where its shade and root
spread won't crowd out smaller neighbours. Treat it in the garden's
layout more like a large ornamental perennial than an annual vegetable,
and plan for its full mature size from the outset rather than the modest
seedling or division it starts as.
