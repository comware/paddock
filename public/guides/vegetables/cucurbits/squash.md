## Overview

Squash covers a wide range of shapes and sizes — button squash and
pattypan behave much like zucchini and are picked young; the longer-season
winter squashes (butternut and similar) behave more like pumpkin and are
grown for a single autumn harvest. This guide covers the general
cucurbit-family care both share; check the maturity window on your
particular variety to know which pattern you're growing.

Whichever type, one planting carries the season — squash isn't sown in
succession.

## When to Sow

Squash wants soil at 18°C and rising, slightly warmer than zucchini
prefers, which in temperate southern Australia usually means late spring
rather than mid-spring — from around November in most districts.

The frost line bounds the season at both ends:

- Cold soil at sowing time stalls or kills seedlings outright.
- The first autumn frost ends the plant, ripe or not.

For the longer-season winter squash types, that autumn end date matters
enormously — count back from the expected first frost using the maturity
figure on the seed packet, and don't sow later than that allows.

## Sowing

Direct sow, or transplant young seedlings raised a few weeks ahead in
cooler districts, disturbing roots as little as possible.

- Sow 25 mm deep, two or three seeds per position, thinning to the
  strongest.
- Bush-type summer squash (pattypan, button squash) wants the full 90 cm
  in both directions, the same as zucchini.
- Vining winter squash types run much further and are better grown along
  a fence or given pumpkin-style room if left to sprawl on the ground.

## Growing On

Feed well through the season and keep water consistent, particularly as
fruit begins to size — as with the rest of the cucurbit family, irregular
watering shows up as blossom end rot and misshapen fruit before it shows
up anywhere else.

Pollination is bee-dependent with separate male and female flowers, and
the same early aborted fruit seen across cucurbits is normal here too
while flower numbers and pollinator visits build up through the season.

## Harvest

Summer squash types — pattypan, button squash — are picked young and
tender, ideally under 10 cm across, and picked often; like zucchini, a
squash left too long turns coarse and stops the plant producing more.

Winter squash types are left to mature fully on the vine, following the
same signs as pumpkin: a hard skin, a dry and browning stem, and a
hollow sound when tapped. They then benefit from the same one-to-two
week cure in a warm, dry spot before storage.

## Succession

Squash is not a succession crop. Summer types crop continuously from one
planting for two to three months if kept picked; winter types are grown
for a single harvest timed to finish before the first frost. Neither
benefits from staggered sowing dates the way a fast salad crop would.

## Common Problems

Powdery mildew is as much a certainty here as elsewhere in the cucurbit
family — expect it by mid-to-late summer and manage rather than chase a
cure, through spacing, airflow, and watering the soil rather than the
leaves.

Blossom end rot and misshapen fruit point to inconsistent watering.
Squash vine borer is a particular risk for the vining winter types, which
have thick hollow stems the borer favours — a foil collar at the stem
base near planting is a simple deterrent worth the minute it takes.

## In the Bed

Bush-type summer squash needs the same room and the same isolation from
crops it would shade as zucchini. Vining winter squash is best sent along
a fence or the edge of a garden where its run doesn't compete with
anything else. Don't follow other cucurbits in the same ground the next
season, to limit carryover of soil-borne mildew and pest pressure.
