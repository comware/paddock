# Pumpkin

## Quick Facts

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Easy |
| **Days to Maturity** | 90-120 |
| **Sowing Method** | Either |
| **Plant Spacing** | 100 cm |
| **Row Spacing** | 200 cm |
| **Sowing Depth** | 25 mm |
| **Soil Temperature** | 18-35 °C |
| **Suggested succession** | Not a succession crop |

## Overview

Pumpkin is the cucurbit that most tests the "far more room than a seed
packet suggests" rule. A vigorous vine can run six to eight metres in a
season, and the 100 by 200 cm spacing here describes where the plant is
started, not the ground it will eventually cover. This is a long-season
crop grown for a single harvest, not a plant that's picked from
repeatedly through summer — one planting is the whole season's crop.

## When to Sow

Pumpkin wants the warmest soil of the common cucurbits: 18°C and rising,
which in temperate southern Australia usually means late spring, from
around November. It is not worth rushing.

The frost line applies at both ends and matters more here than for
faster crops, because the long days-to-maturity leaves less slack:

- Sown too early into cold soil, seed rots before it germinates rather
  than merely sitting still.
- Sown too late, fruit may not finish ripening before the first autumn
  frost cuts the vine down.

Getting the sowing window right is largely about not rushing the start —
the season is long enough that a week or two of patience in spring costs
little against the 90-120 days ahead.

## Sowing

Direct sow into mounds or hills, which warm faster and drain better than
flat ground — useful given how much water a pumpkin vine eventually asks
for.

- Sow 25 mm deep, three or four seeds per hill, thinning to the
  strongest one or two.
- Allow the full 100 cm between hills and 200 cm between rows — and mean
  it. This is not a spacing that can be tightened for a small garden;
  pumpkin simply needs a lot of ground, or should be grown up something
  very sturdy instead.

## Growing On

Feed well at planting and again as vines start to run — this is a heavy
feeder over a long season. Water deeply and consistently while fruit is
sizing; a vine that dries out and recovers repeatedly produces smaller,
less consistent fruit.

Pollination follows the same pattern as the rest of the cucurbit family —
separate male and female flowers, bee-dependent, with early female
flowers often aborting before enough pollinator activity builds up. Once
a vine has set two or three fruit that are clearly sizing up, some
gardeners pinch off the growing tip to direct the plant's energy into
finishing those fruit rather than setting more than it can ripen.

## Harvest

Wait for full maturity — pumpkin doesn't ripen further once cut, unlike
some other cucurbits. The reliable signs are a hard, dull-rather-than-glossy
skin, a dried and browning stem, and a hollow sound when tapped.

- Cure fruit in a warm, dry, well-ventilated spot for one to two weeks
  after harvest, which hardens the skin further and improves storage
  life substantially.
- Leave a length of stem attached; a pumpkin without its stem stores
  poorly and rots faster from that point.

## Succession

Pumpkin is not a succession crop. Given the 90-120 day season and the
ground it occupies, one planting is the practical maximum for most home
gardens, and it's timed to ripen before the first frost rather than
staggered for continuous harvest.

## Common Problems

Powdery mildew appears on pumpkin foliage in the same predictable way as
the rest of the family, usually late in the season by which point the
fruit is largely sized and the vine's job is mostly done. It's rarely
worth treating aggressively this late.

Fruit that rot on the ground where they sit are usually a moisture and
contact problem rather than disease — slipping a board, tile, or straw
pad under developing fruit keeps them dry and prevents slug and insect
damage from the underside. Vine borer, where present, is the more
serious threat, entering the stem near the base and wilting the whole
vine with little warning; a foil collar at the stem base at planting is
the standard deterrent.

## In the Bed

Pumpkin needs a corner of its own, or a fence line to run along and
sprawl over rather than across the bed. It's a poor companion for
anything it can shade or crowd out over a season, but it works well
planted at the edge of a garden where its vine can run out onto lawn or
unused ground. Avoid repeating cucurbits in the same ground each year.
