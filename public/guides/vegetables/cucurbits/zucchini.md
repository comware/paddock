## Overview

Zucchini is the cucurbit that convinces beginners they have a green thumb,
right up until one plant is producing more than a household can eat. It's
fast, vigorous, and forgiving of mediocre soil, though it rewards good soil
with a much longer season of fruit.

Two or three plants is plenty for most families. This is not a crop that
needs succession sowing — a single planting keeps cropping for months once
it gets going, so the effort goes into keeping it fed and picked, not into
staggering more sowings.

## When to Sow

Wait until the soil has warmed to 16°C and is climbing, which in temperate
southern Australia usually lines up with mid-to-late spring — October into
November, later again at altitude or further south.

This is a frost-line crop at both ends:

- A late cold snap will kill young seedlings outright.
- The first proper autumn frost ends the planting overnight.

Getting the start date right matters more than it looks. A fortnight sown
too early doesn't get you a fortnight's head start — it gets you a dead or
stalled seedling, replaced later by one sown on time and no further ahead
for the trouble. Wait for the soil, not the calendar.

## Sowing

Direct sow, or raise seedlings for a few weeks' head start in cooler
districts — zucchini transplants reasonably well if root disturbance is
kept to a minimum.

- Sow 25 mm deep, two or three seeds per hill.
- Thin to the strongest single seedling once true leaves appear.
- Space plants a full 90 cm apart in both directions.

That spacing looks excessive next to the seed packet, but it isn't. A
mature zucchini plant is closer to a small shrub than a vegetable, and
crowding it invites the powdery mildew it's prone to regardless. Give it
the room from the start rather than fighting for it later.

## Growing On

Feed reasonably well through the season — this is a hungry, fast-growing
plant — and keep water consistent once fruit starts setting, since
irregular water shows up in the fruit well before it shows up in the
leaves. Mulch generously; the huge leaves shade their own root zone, but
the soil at the plant's edges dries out fast in a hot spell regardless.

Zucchini flowers are either male or female, and the plant depends entirely
on bees moving pollen between them. It's normal — not a fault — for the
first few female flowers to swell slightly, then rot and drop instead of
developing into fruit. That's usually incomplete pollination: not enough
male flowers open yet, not enough pollinator activity, or a cool spell
keeping bees at home.

This generally corrects itself within a week or two as flower production
picks up. If it doesn't, hand pollination with a small brush — or simply
touching a freshly opened male flower to a female one — is a reliable fix.

## Harvest

Pick young and often. Fifteen to twenty centimetres long is the sweet spot
for flavour and texture, and cutting fruit regularly is what keeps the
plant producing rather than setting seed and slowing down.

A zucchini left on the plant for a week turns into a marrow — edible, but
coarse, seedy, and no longer what was actually being grown. Check the
plant every day or two once it's cropping; it hides fruit under those
huge leaves, and a missed one turns into a baseball bat overnight.

## Succession

Zucchini is not a succession crop. One planting, kept picked and
reasonably fed, produces continuously from early summer through to the
first autumn frost. There's no benefit to staggering sowings the way you
would with a fast, one-shot crop like radish — the plant is doing that
job on its own.

## Common Problems

Powdery mildew isn't a possibility with zucchini, it's an inevitability —
a white dusting across the leaves that turns up reliably by mid-to-late
summer as nights cool slightly and humidity rises.

- It rarely kills an established, well-fed plant outright.
- By the time it appears, most plants have already given most of their
  crop.
- Manage it rather than chase a cure: space plants well for airflow,
  water the soil rather than the leaves, and remove the worst-affected
  leaves once it's well established.

Blossom end rot and oddly shaped fruit both point back to inconsistent
watering rather than a soil problem. Squash vine borer and pumpkin beetle
can attack the base of the stem in some districts — a simple collar of
foil or mesh around the stem at planting deters both.

## In the Bed

Zucchini's spreading habit and heavy feeding make it a poor neighbour for
anything that needs the light or nutrients it claims for itself — give it
its own patch, or the edge of a bed where its sprawl doesn't matter.

It follows leafy greens or legumes well, since legumes leave nitrogen
behind that a hungry zucchini plant puts straight to use. Avoid following
other cucurbits in the same spot in consecutive years, to cut down on
carryover of soil-borne mildew spores and pests.
