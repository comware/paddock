# Rockmelon

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Moderate |
| **Days to Maturity** | 80-100 |
| **Sowing Method** | Either |
| **Plant Spacing** | 60 cm |

## Overview

Rockmelon is the fussiest of the common home-garden cucurbits — it wants
real heat, a long warm run without interruption, and reliably dry
conditions as fruit ripens, which is a harder combination to deliver in a
cool-temperate climate than for zucchini or pumpkin. It rewards a warm
north-facing bed and a hot summer, and sulks through a cool or wet one.

## When to Sow

Rockmelon wants soil at 18°C and rising, and does much better with soil
comfortably above that — this is a crop for the warmest part of the
season, sown from late spring once nights as well as days have warmed.

The frost line applies as it does to every cucurbit here, but rockmelon
is also more sensitive to cool nights generally, even well short of
frost — growth can stall in a run of cool spring nights that a zucchini
would shrug off. In a marginal or cooler district, waiting an extra
couple of weeks past the earliest safe sowing date for zucchini is not
excessive caution for rockmelon.

## Sowing

Direct sow once the soil is reliably warm, or raise short-lived
seedlings indoors for transplanting — rockmelon resents root disturbance
more than most cucurbits, so keep any transplant shock as brief as
possible.

- Sow 20 mm deep, two or three seeds per position.
- Thin to the strongest single seedling.
- Space plants 60 cm apart with 200 cm between rows, or run vines up a
  sturdy trellis to save ground, supporting developing fruit in a sling
  as they size up.

## Growing On

Feed moderately and water deeply while the vine is establishing and
fruit is sizing, then cut water back noticeably as fruit nears ripeness —
this is the one point where rockmelon's needs invert. Too much water
late in ripening dilutes sweetness and can split fruit outright.

Pollination is bee-dependent as with the rest of the family. A vine that
is setting fruit poorly in a cool run of weather is very often a
pollinator problem rather than a plant health problem — warmer weather
and more bee activity usually resolves it.

## Harvest

Rockmelon gives a clear, almost unmissable signal when ripe: the fruit
slips cleanly off the vine at the stem with light pressure, known as
"full slip." Fruit that needs to be cut or twisted off is not yet ripe
and won't sweeten further once picked — unlike pumpkin, rockmelon doesn't
ripen off the vine to any useful degree.

A sweet smell at the stem end and a slight give when pressed at that end
are supporting signs, but slip is the reliable one.

## Succession

Rockmelon is not a succession crop. Given the long maturity window and
the narrow warm season in a cool-temperate climate, one well-timed
planting is generally all a single summer allows, timed to give the
longest possible run of warmth before ripening.

## Common Problems

Powdery mildew affects rockmelon along with the rest of the cucurbit
family and is managed the same way — spacing, airflow, and watering the
soil rather than the leaves, rather than expecting to prevent it outright.

Fruit that fails to sweeten despite looking mature is usually a lack of
sustained heat rather than a growing fault — rockmelon genuinely needs a
hot summer, and a cool or overcast one will produce blander fruit no
matter how well it was tended. Splitting fruit points to a sudden heavy
watering, often after a dry spell, right as the fruit was ripening.
Fusarium wilt can be a problem in soil where melons have been grown
repeatedly; rotating away from cucurbits for a couple of seasons helps.

## In the Bed

Give rockmelon the warmest, most open, best-draining spot available — a
north-facing slope or a bed against a warm wall does real work here in a
way it doesn't for the less fussy cucurbits. Trellising saves ground in a
small garden but needs slings under the fruit to stop it tearing free as
it sizes up. Avoid following other cucurbits or melons in the same
ground in consecutive seasons.
