## Overview

Cucumber is a step up in fussiness from zucchini — it wants warmth,
consistent water, and either a trellis or a lot of ground, and it sulks
visibly when any of those are missing. Get the basics right and a single
planting will crop steadily for two to three months, which is why it
doesn't need succession sowing the way a quick salad crop does.

Vining varieties climb and want support; bush types sprawl and want the
full row spacing instead. Both are grown the same way up to that point.

## When to Sow

Cucumber wants soil at 16°C and rising before it goes in, tracking with
zucchini and the rest of the cucurbits — roughly mid-to-late spring in
temperate southern Australia, later at altitude.

It sits on the same frost line as every other cucurbit here:

- Seedlings sown into cold soil stall rather than grow, and rarely
  recover the lost time even once it warms up.
- The first autumn frost finishes the plant in a single night.

There is no reward for being early. A cucumber sown two weeks ahead of
schedule into 12°C soil will very often be overtaken by one sown on time
into 18°C soil.

## Sowing

Direct sow once the soil is warm enough, or raise a short head start
indoors in cooler districts — cucumber transplants reasonably well if
disturbed as little as possible.

- Sow 15 mm deep.
- Space vining types 45 cm apart along a trellis or fence.
- Give bush types the full 90 cm row spacing; they don't climb, and
  without support they need the ground.

Trellising is worth the extra ten minutes of setup: it keeps fruit
straight, keeps foliage off the ground where fungal disease spreads
fastest, and makes picking far easier.

## Growing On

Water is the single biggest factor in cucumber flavour. Inconsistent
watering — a dry week followed by a soak — is the most common cause of
bitter fruit, far more often than variety choice. Mulch heavily and aim
for even soil moisture rather than a big drink once a week.

Feed moderately; cucumber isn't as hungry as pumpkin or zucchini, but it
does want a steady supply rather than one heavy dose. Like all cucurbits
it depends on bees for pollination, and the same early aborted fruit seen
in zucchini shows up here for the same reason — not enough flowers or
pollinators yet. It settles down as the plant matures.

## Harvest

Pick while still firm and before the seeds inside swell and harden —
letting fruit oversize on the vine is the single fastest way to stop a
cucumber plant producing, since a ripening fruit signals the plant to
slow down and set fewer new ones.

Check vines every day or two in warm weather; cucumber grows fast enough
to go from pickable to overgrown within 48 hours. Cut rather than pull,
to avoid damaging the vine.

## Succession

Cucumber is not a succession crop. A single planting, picked regularly
and watered consistently, will produce for two to three months before
fading with the heat or the season. Staggering sowings adds work without
adding much harvest window, since the plant's own decline — not a lack of
new plantings — usually ends the season.

## Common Problems

Powdery mildew arrives on cucumber almost as reliably as it does on
zucchini, usually a little later in the season. Treat it the same way:
manage airflow and watering rather than expect to prevent it outright.

Bitter fruit, as above, is almost always inconsistent water rather than a
variety fault. Downy mildew, distinct from powdery mildew and showing as
yellow angular patches on the upper leaf surface, favours humid, still
conditions — trellising and spacing both help by keeping air moving
through the foliage. Cucumber beetle can transmit bacterial wilt in some
districts; a wilting single vine with otherwise healthy neighbours is
usually the sign.

## In the Bed

Trellised cucumber takes up very little ground space and pairs well with
low-growing crops planted at its base, provided they can tolerate some
shade as the vine fills in. Bush types need the same room and consideration
as zucchini and should be treated the same way when planning the bed.

Avoid following other cucurbits in the same ground in consecutive
seasons, for the same soil-borne disease reasons as zucchini.
