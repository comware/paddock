## Overview

Potato doesn't belong in the same pattern as the other crops in this
family. It isn't sown as seed and it isn't picked from a plant as it
ripens — it's grown from a piece of seed potato buried deep, built up
with soil as it grows, and harvested at the end by digging the whole
plant out of the ground. Everything about how it's handled follows from
that difference, so it's worth setting the tomato-and-capsicum pattern
aside entirely here.

## When to Sow

Potato is comfortable in cooler soil than the rest of this family — 10°C
and rising is enough, which in temperate southern Australia allows
planting from late winter into spring, well ahead of when tomato or
capsicum could go in. It also tolerates a wider range through the season,
up to 25°C, without the sharp heat dependence its nightshade relatives
show.

Frost can still damage emerged foliage, though the tuber itself sits
safely below ground — a late frost that blackens the leaves is a setback,
not usually a killer, and the plant often regrows from the tuber.

## Sowing

Plant whole small seed potatoes, or larger ones cut into pieces with at
least one or two "eyes" each, letting cut pieces dry and callus for a day
before planting to reduce rot risk.

- Plant 100 mm deep — far deeper than any seed in this guide, because
  this is a piece of tuber going in, not a seed.
- Space 30 cm apart with 75 cm between rows, leaving room for the
  hilling that comes later.

Only ever plant seed potatoes sold for the purpose, not potatoes from the
supermarket — these are frequently treated to prevent sprouting and can
also carry disease that persists in the soil for years.

## Growing On

Hilling is the defining task of growing potato and the main point of
difference from everything else in this guide. As shoots emerge and grow
to around 20 cm, mound soil up around the stem, burying the lower
portion and leaving only the top growing free. Repeat this two or three
times over the season as the plant continues to grow.

Hilling matters for two reasons: it encourages the plant to set more
tubers along the buried stem, and it keeps developing tubers covered, so
they don't turn green from sun exposure — green potato flesh contains
solanine and shouldn't be eaten. Water consistently while tubers are
forming; drought stress at this stage reduces yield noticeably.

## Harvest

Harvest is digging, not picking. New potatoes can be gently forked out
from the side of a hill once plants flower, for a small early crop of
thin-skinned tubers that don't store well but eat well immediately.

For the main crop, wait until the foliage yellows and dies back
naturally, then dig the whole plant. Let harvested tubers dry on the
surface for a few hours before storing, and store in a dark, cool, dry
place — light causes greening in storage just as it does in the ground.

## Succession

Potato does take succession sowing, unlike the rest of this family — a
30 day interval between plantings spreads the harvest window across the
growing season rather than delivering everything at once, useful given
how well the main crop stores compared with a one-off summer glut.

## Common Problems

Green tubers are the most common beginner problem and come directly from
inadequate hilling — any tuber that sees light greens, and green flesh
should be discarded rather than eaten. Keeping hills topped up through
the season is the whole prevention.

Blight, a fungal disease that affects potato and tomato alike since they
are closely related, spreads fast in humid or wet conditions and can
collapse foliage within days; there's little to do once established
beyond removing affected foliage promptly to slow the spread. Scab, a
rough patch on the tuber skin, is mostly cosmetic and linked to overly
alkaline or overly dry soil rather than a serious threat to the crop.

## In the Bed

Potato shares blight and other disease pressure with tomato, capsicum,
chilli, and eggplant, and should be kept on the same rotation gap of at
least two seasons away from any of them in the same ground. Its early
planting window and the disruption of hilling and digging make it a poor
fit for a permanent bed shared with other crops — it does better with a
patch of its own that can be dug over freely at harvest.
