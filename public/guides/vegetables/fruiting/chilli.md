# Chilli

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Moderate |
| **Days to Maturity** | 70-100 |
| **Sowing Method** | Transplant |
| **Plant Spacing** | 45 cm |
| **Row Spacing** | 60 cm |
| **Sowing Depth** | 5 mm |

## Overview

Chilli is capsicum's close relative and is grown the same way, with the
same heat requirement and the same transplant-from-seedling approach.
The main practical differences are size — chilli plants and fruit both
tend smaller — and a generally longer, more heat-tolerant cropping
season once established, which is why a well-sited chilli plant often
keeps producing later into autumn than capsicum does.

## When to Sow

Sow indoors 8-10 weeks before the last frost, matching capsicum's
timeline, and expect similarly slow, warmth-dependent germination — a
heat mat under the seed tray is worth using if the room is cool.

Soil for planting out wants to be reliably above 18°C. As with capsicum,
a chilli plant transplanted too early doesn't die, it stalls, often for
weeks, and shows little advantage over one planted later into properly
warmed soil and consistently warm nights.

## Sowing

Raise seedlings indoors 5 mm deep, harden off over a week before
transplanting, and space plants 45 cm apart with 60 cm between rows —
some smaller-fruited, more compact varieties can be planted closer, and
some larger bush types want more room, so check the specific variety
against these figures as a starting point rather than a fixed rule.

## Growing On

Staking helps once a plant is carrying a heavy fruit load, though chilli
generally has a sturdier, more compact structure than capsicum and needs
it less often. Water consistently rather than heavily; chilli tolerates
a slightly drier root run than capsicum does, and some growers deliberately
ease off water as fruit ripens to concentrate heat and flavour, though
this is a refinement rather than a requirement.

Feed moderately — too much nitrogen produces leafy growth at the expense
of fruit, the same trade-off as capsicum.

## Harvest

Pick once fruit reaches full size and colour for the variety, or earlier
for a milder, greener flavour on varieties that are eaten green as well
as ripe. Regular picking encourages continued fruit set, as with the
rest of the fruiting crops in this guide. Wear gloves when handling hot
varieties, and avoid touching the face until hands are washed.

## Succession

Chilli is not a succession crop. It's a single long-season planting, and
in a favourable, sheltered, warm position it will often keep cropping
later into autumn than capsicum or tomato, simply tapering off as
temperatures drop rather than stopping abruptly.

## Common Problems

Blossom end rot appears less often on chilli than on capsicum or tomato,
but when it does it traces to the same cause — inconsistent watering
disrupting calcium movement in the plant, not a soil deficiency — and
responds to the same fix of even watering rather than a spray.

Poor fruit set in prolonged extreme heat mirrors capsicum's own upper
limit and generally resolves once conditions ease. Aphids can build up
quickly on the soft new growth of chilli in particular; a strong jet of
water or an appropriate insecticidal soap early, before numbers build,
is more effective than treating an established infestation.

## In the Bed

Chilli shares the nightshade disease profile with capsicum, tomato,
eggplant, and potato, so keep the same two-season rotation gap between
plantings of any of these in the same ground. It's a reasonable
companion for basil for the same reasons capsicum is, and its more
compact habit makes it a workable pot or edge-of-bed plant where a
sprawling tomato would not fit.
