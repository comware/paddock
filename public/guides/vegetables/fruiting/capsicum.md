# Capsicum

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Moderate |
| **Days to Maturity** | 70-90 |
| **Sowing Method** | Transplant |
| **Plant Spacing** | 45 cm |

## Overview

Capsicum is a slower, more heat-hungry relative of tomato — a longer run
to maturity, a narrower comfortable temperature band, and less patience
for a cool start. It's grown from a raised seedling everywhere in
temperate southern Australia for the same reason as tomato: the direct-sown
season here is rarely long enough on its own.

## When to Sow

Sow seed indoors 8-10 weeks before the last frost, earlier than tomato,
because capsicum is slower to get going from seed and needs more lead
time to reach a plantable size. Soil for transplanting wants to be
reliably above 18°C — a couple of degrees warmer than tomato asks for.

Capsicum sown or transplanted too early doesn't die, but it stalls
noticeably harder than tomato does, sometimes not resuming real growth
for weeks. Wait for consistently warm nights, not just warm days, before
planting out — a warm afternoon followed by a cold night is enough to
hold the plant back.

## Sowing

Raise seedlings indoors, 5 mm deep, and expect slower germination than
tomato — warmth at the seed tray, from a heat mat if the room runs cool,
speeds this up noticeably.

Harden off over a week before transplanting, as with tomato, and space
plants 45 cm apart with 60 cm between rows. Capsicum plants stay more
compact than indeterminate tomato, so the closer row spacing here is
appropriate rather than a shortcut.

## Growing On

Staking is worth doing even though capsicum doesn't sprawl the way
tomato does — a heavy fruit load on a comparatively brittle stem can snap
a branch in wind or a rain-soaked moment without support.

Water consistently, for the same underlying reason as tomato: fruit
quality and the plant's ability to move nutrients depend on even soil
moisture more than on total volume. Feed moderately; too much nitrogen
produces lush leaf growth at the expense of fruit set.

## Harvest

Capsicum can be picked green — fully sized but not yet coloured — or left
to ripen to red, yellow, or orange, depending on variety and taste. Left
to fully colour, the fruit is sweeter but the plant produces less overall
volume through the season, since ripening ties up the plant's energy
longer per fruit. Cut rather than pull to avoid tearing the branch.

## Succession

Capsicum is not a succession crop. It's a single long-season planting
that crops steadily from mid or late summer through autumn, similar in
shape to tomato but on a slightly later, slower timeline.

## Common Problems

Blossom end rot shows up on capsicum for the same reason it does on
tomato — inconsistent watering disrupting calcium movement within the
plant, not a soil calcium deficiency — and is fixed the same way, through
even watering and mulch, not a spray.

Sunscald, a pale, papery patch on fruit exposed to direct hot sun, is
common on capsicum because its fruit sits more exposed than tomato's
often does; keeping foliage full through moderate, not excessive,
pruning helps shade developing fruit. Poor fruit set in extended hot
weather above roughly 32°C is common — capsicum's own upper limit rather
than a growing fault, and fruit set generally resumes once temperatures
ease.

## In the Bed

Capsicum shares the nightshade family's disease profile with tomato,
chilli, eggplant, and potato, so avoid following any of them in the same
ground for at least two seasons. It benefits from following a legume
crop, as tomato does, and pairs reasonably with basil, which shares
similar water and warmth needs without competing heavily for the same
root depth.
