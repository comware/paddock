## Overview

Tomato is grown from a raised seedling almost everywhere in temperate
southern Australia, because the season here is rarely long and warm
enough to grow it well from a direct-sown seed. It's a genuine heat
lover, and most of the problems beginners have with tomato trace back to
one thing: planting out before the plant, and the weather, are actually
ready.

## When to Sow

Sow seed indoors 6-8 weeks before the last expected frost, so seedlings
are a manageable size — not leggy, not rootbound — right as it's safe to
plant them out. Soil for transplanting needs to be reliably above 16°C
and climbing.

This is where the heat requirement matters more than the calendar. A
tomato transplanted into cold soil doesn't die outright the way a
cucurbit seedling does, but it doesn't grow either — it sits, sulks, and
is very often overtaken within weeks by a seedling planted two or three
weeks later into properly warmed soil. There's no advantage in being
early; the plant simply waits out the cold, and the wait costs more time
than it saves.

## Sowing

Raise seedlings indoors, 5 mm deep, in trays or small pots under good
light, and pot up once true leaves appear if they need more root room
before it's warm enough to plant out.

Harden seedlings off over a week or so before transplanting — a few hours
outside in a sheltered spot on day one, building up to a full day and
night by the end of the week — to avoid transplant shock from sudden sun
and wind exposure.

Space plants 45 cm apart with 75 cm between rows, more for larger
indeterminate varieties that will be staked and grow tall rather than
sprawl.

## Growing On

Staking or caging from the time of planting, not after the plant has
flopped, saves a great deal of trouble later. Indeterminate varieties
keep growing and cropping until frost or disease stops them and need
sturdy, tall support; determinate varieties grow to a fixed size, crop
in a shorter, heavier flush, and need less.

Pruning suckers — the shoots that emerge in the join between the main
stem and a leaf branch — on indeterminate varieties keeps growth
manageable and improves airflow, though it's a matter of preference
rather than a strict requirement; determinate varieties are generally
left unpruned.

Water deeply and consistently rather than little and often. This matters
more for tomato than almost any other vegetable in this guide, because of
what inconsistent water does to the fruit.

## Harvest

Pick when fully coloured and slightly soft to the touch, or a shade
earlier and ripen the last stretch on a windowsill if birds or heat are a
problem. Regular picking, like most fruiting crops, encourages the plant
to keep setting more.

## Succession

Tomato is not a succession crop in the way a fast salad green is — it's
a single, long-season planting that crops for weeks to months from mid
or late summer through into autumn. Some gardeners plant an early and a
late variety together to spread the harvest window rather than sowing
the same variety twice.

## Common Problems

Blossom end rot — a flat, sunken, leathery patch on the base of the
fruit — is the tomato problem gardeners most often misdiagnose. It looks
like a nutrient deficiency and gets treated with a calcium spray, which
rarely helps, because it is fundamentally a water-consistency problem,
not a calcium shortage in the soil. Uneven watering disrupts the plant's
ability to move the calcium it already has to where the fruit needs it.
The fix is even, consistent soil moisture through mulching and a regular
watering schedule, not a foliar spray.

Blight, in its various fungal forms, is a real risk in humid or wet
conditions and spreads fastest on foliage kept wet by overhead watering —
water at the base of the plant instead. Cracking fruit points to a heavy
rain or watering after a dry spell, the same underlying inconsistency as
blossom end rot showing up differently.

## In the Bed

Tomato is a heavy feeder and does best following a legume crop that has
left nitrogen behind. Avoid planting it where potato, capsicum, chilli,
or eggplant grew in the previous season or two — all are related
nightshades that share the same soil-borne diseases, and rotating away
from the family reduces disease carryover substantially.
