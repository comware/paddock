## Overview

Eggplant is the most demanding of the common nightshade crops covered
here — the slowest to get going, the fussiest about sustained heat, and
the least forgiving of a cool or short summer. In a marginal
cool-temperate district it can be genuinely hard to bring to a full
harvest; in a warm, sheltered spot with a long growing season it's very
rewarding. Worth knowing that reputation going in, rather than blaming
the plant or the technique when a cool season underperforms.

## When to Sow

Sow indoors 8-10 weeks before the last frost, similar to capsicum and
chilli, but germination is often slower still and benefits noticeably
from bottom heat. Soil for transplanting wants to be reliably above 18°C,
with genuinely warm, settled weather ahead rather than just a warm week.

Eggplant transplanted too early into cool soil doesn't merely stall the
way capsicum does — it can sit for a month or more without meaningful
growth, and a plant that stalls that long rarely catches up fully within
a single season. In a cool district, it is worth waiting later than
instinct suggests, even later than for capsicum or chilli.

## Sowing

Raise seedlings indoors 5 mm deep with consistent bottom heat if
possible, and expect a longer wait for germination than the other
nightshades in this guide — two to three weeks is not unusual. Harden off
gradually before transplanting, and space plants 45 cm apart with 75 cm
between rows, giving the mature bush room to branch out.

## Growing On

Stake from planting — eggplant carries heavy fruit on branches that snap
more readily than tomato's do, and support is not optional once fruit
starts sizing. Water consistently and deeply; eggplant is thirstier than
capsicum or chilli and shows stress from drying out more quickly through
wilting foliage.

Feed well through the season; this is a heavier feeder than its
nightshade relatives, particularly once fruit is setting.

## Harvest

Pick while the skin is still glossy and taut — a dulling, slightly
wrinkled skin is the sign of a fruit left too long, and overripe
eggplant develops a bitter, seedy texture that doesn't improve with
cooking. Cut with a short length of stem attached rather than pulling,
which can tear the branch.

## Succession

Eggplant is not a succession crop. It's a single long-season planting,
and given how demanding the heat requirement is, most of the effort goes
into giving the one planting the best possible start and site rather
than staggering sowings.

## Common Problems

Poor or nonexistent fruit set is the most common eggplant complaint in a
cool-temperate garden, and it is almost always a heat and timing issue —
the plant simply hasn't had enough sustained warmth to set fruit well —
rather than a pollination or nutrient problem to fix. A warmer,
more sheltered position, or a longer wait before transplanting into truly
warm soil, addresses this more reliably than any feed or spray.

Blossom end rot occurs here too, from the same inconsistent-watering
cause as tomato and capsicum, and responds to the same even-watering fix
rather than a calcium treatment. Flea beetle can riddle young eggplant
leaves with small holes; established plants generally outgrow the
damage, but seedlings can be set back badly, so protecting young plants
in the first weeks after transplanting is worthwhile.

## In the Bed

Eggplant shares the same disease profile as tomato, capsicum, chilli, and
potato, and needs the same rotation gap of at least two seasons before
returning any of them to the same ground. Give it the warmest, most
sheltered spot available in the bed — more than any other nightshade
here, its success depends on the microclimate it's given rather than on
technique alone.
