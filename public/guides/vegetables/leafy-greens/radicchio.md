## Overview

Radicchio is a chicory grown for its tight, wine-red head — bitter, crunchy, and one of the longer-season crops in this guide at 60-80 days to maturity. Unlike endive, most radicchio varieties don't need active blanching to be good eating; instead, the real trick with radicchio is a genuine frost or a solid cold snap, which sweetens the bitterness and often intensifies the red colour the plant is grown for. A radicchio head picked before any cold weather is usually a paler, greener, more one-dimensionally bitter thing than the same variety after a few frosty nights.

It's also one of the slower crops to mature here, which means committing bed space for a good two to three months, and it can be a little unpredictable about actually forming a tight head rather than staying loose and open — a mix of variety choice and getting the timing right for the season.

## When to Sow

Soil temperature range is 10-25°C. As a southern Australian guide, sow in mid-to-late summer (Jan-Feb) for heads that mature into autumn and early winter, where they'll get the cold exposure that improves flavour and colour, or in spring (Sep-Oct) for a summer crop that will be milder in colour and flavour without that frost.

Sow too early into a peak summer sowing and seedlings can struggle in the heat and be more prone to bolting before heading up. Sow too late in autumn and the plant may not have enough growing time left before winter really sets in to form a proper head — given its long 60-80 day run, radicchio needs its sowing window respected more strictly than a fast crop like rocket.

## Sowing

Radicchio can be direct-sown or transplanted; transplanting from punnets is common for the main autumn-maturing crop, giving it a head start through the tail end of summer heat. Sow shallow at 5 mm.

Space plants 25 cm apart in rows 35 cm apart to give the head room to bulk up. Crowded plants are less likely to form a solid, tight head at all, staying loose and open instead — spacing has a more direct effect on whether radicchio "works" as a crop than it does for most of the leafy greens in this guide.

## Growing On

Consistent watering matters through the head-forming stage in particular; a dry check followed by a soak can contribute to loose or split heads. Radicchio isn't a heavy feeder, but steady growth without stops and starts gives the best chance of a properly formed head. As autumn cools and the first frosts arrive, resist the urge to protect the crop — that cold exposure is doing useful work on flavour and colour, not damage.

## Harvest

Radicchio takes the full 60-80 days, and heads are ready when they feel firm and dense when squeezed, similar to a small cabbage. Cut the head at the base, leaving the outer wrapper leaves and roots in the ground — many radicchio varieties will resprout a second, smaller flush of loose leaves from the stump, so it's worth leaving the bed in place for a few weeks after the main cut rather than clearing it immediately. Left too long in the ground, especially into a warm spell, heads can split or bolt, and flavour turns harshly bitter rather than the balanced bitterness a well-timed harvest gives you.

## Succession

With a 21-day succession interval and a long time to maturity, radicchio doesn't lend itself to the tight, frequent successions of a fast green — 2-3 sowings spaced three weeks apart through the main summer sowing window is realistic, staggering harvest through autumn and into winter rather than trying to keep a continuous supply year-round.

## Common Problems

**Heads staying loose and open rather than forming tightly.** Usually down to crowding, inconsistent watering, or a variety not suited to the conditions it was grown in. Give full spacing, keep watering even through the head-forming stage, and choose a variety recommended for your sowing time if heading has been unreliable.

**Bitterness that never mellows.** A sign the crop matured without any cold exposure, common with a spring-sown, summer-harvested crop. Time the main planting for an autumn harvest instead, where frost does the flavour work for you, and treat a summer crop as always going to be sharper.

**Slugs and snails sheltering in the dense head.** The tight, moist head is an attractive hiding spot, especially as it matures. Check heads before use, keep the bed free of damp mulch right against the plants, and use a pet-safe iron-based bait if pressure is high.

## In the Bed

Radicchio is an Asteraceae, the same family as lettuce and endive, so avoid rotating straight between these three and give the bed a season with an unrelated crop in between. Its long time in the ground and moderate size mean it holds a bed for a good chunk of the season, so plan the space around that rather than expecting a quick turnover, and its resprouting habit after the first cut means it's worth leaving in place a little longer than the main harvest date suggests.
