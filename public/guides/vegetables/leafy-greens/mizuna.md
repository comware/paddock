## Overview

Mizuna is a Japanese mustard green grown for its finely divided, feathery leaves — visually distinctive in a salad mix and much milder in flavour than its mustard-family relatives. Where mustard greens can be genuinely hot, mizuna is closer to a peppery lettuce, which is why it turns up so often in bagged salad mixes.

It's one of the easiest brassica greens to grow well: fast, prolific, and notably more heat-tolerant and slower to bolt than rocket or true spinach, which makes it a useful crop for stretching the cooler-season harvest a little further into the shoulder seasons. The main thing to get right is picking method rather than timing — mizuna rewards a light touch at harvest far more than most greens.

## When to Sow

Soil temperature range is 8-25°C. As a southern Australian guide, sow from late winter through spring (Aug-Nov) and again through autumn (Mar-May); it tolerates a wider range than rocket and can often be pushed a little further into early summer or kept going a little later into autumn.

Sow too early into cold soil and germination just slows rather than fails. Sow too late into hot summer conditions and, while mizuna is more heat-tolerant than most leafy brassicas, it will still eventually bolt and the leaves toughen and lose their mild flavour as it does.

## Sowing

Mizuna suits either direct sowing or transplanting — direct sowing is simpler and cheaper for a cut-and-come-again crop, while transplants can be useful for an early head start. Sow shallow at 5 mm.

Space plants 15 cm apart in rows 25 cm apart for full-size plants, though like rocket it's commonly grown denser as a baby-leaf crop for repeated light cutting. Crowding mostly costs you leaf size rather than causing real problems, though very dense, poorly ventilated plantings are more prone to fungal issues in humid weather.

## Growing On

Mizuna is undemanding — regular watering to keep growth steady is the main requirement, since a plant that's allowed to dry out and then rewetted tends to bolt sooner and produce tougher leaves. A light feed during the growing period helps keep the rosette pushing out new leaves, but this isn't a heavy feeder and over-fertilising mostly just produces soft growth that's more attractive to pests.

## Harvest

Mizuna is ready for baby-leaf picking from around three weeks, or left to size up for full-rosette harvest closer to 35-60 days. It's a strong cut-and-come-again performer: cut leaves an inch or two above the crown, leaving the centre intact, and it regrows reliably for several cuts. The finely cut leaves bruise more easily than a solid leaf like pak choi, so handle it gently at harvest and rinse rather than scrub. Left unpicked for too long the leaves get noticeably coarser and the plant eventually bolts, at which point the flavour turns sharper and less pleasant raw.

## Succession

With a 14-day succession interval, mizuna suits the same rolling-sowing approach as rocket — a new short row every two weeks keeps a continuous supply of tender young leaves rather than one planting ageing into toughness. Across a typical southern Australian spring-to-autumn run, 4-5 successions is realistic, with a gap through the hottest weeks of summer.

## Common Problems

**Flea beetles peppering leaves with small holes.** A common brassica pest, worse on young seedlings and in warm, dry conditions. Net new sowings with fine insect mesh from sowing, and keep seedlings growing steadily rather than checked by dry soil.

**Leaves toughening and losing their mild flavour.** Usually a sign the plant is heading toward bolting, brought on by heat, long days, or a dry spell. Harvest promptly once you notice it, and treat the planting as close to finished rather than expecting the flavour to recover.

**Slugs and snails targeting young seedlings.** The soft new growth is an easy target in damp weather. Hand-pick after dark or use a pet-safe iron-based bait, and keep the area around seedlings free of the damp mulch and debris slugs shelter under.

## In the Bed

Mizuna is a Brassicaceae, sharing pests and diseases with rocket, tatsoi, pak choi, and mustard greens, so rotate it away from those crops rather than following one with another in the same spot. It's fast, low-growing, and finishes quickly, making it a handy gap-filler that frees the bed again within a month or so. Follow it with a non-brassica — a legume to rebuild nitrogen, or a root crop — to keep the rotation clean.
