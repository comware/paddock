## Overview

Rocket (not arugula — same plant, but that's not the name you'll see on a seed packet here) is about as close to indestructible as a leafy green gets. It germinates fast, grows fast, and will come back after a hard cut with barely a pause. If you want one crop to prove to yourself that growing food isn't hard, this is it.

The one real quirk is flavour, not survival: rocket gets hotter and more peppery as the weather warms and the plant comes under any kind of stress, particularly heat or dryness. A cool-season rocket leaf is mild and nutty; the same variety grown into a hot, dry spell can be sharp enough to be unpleasant raw. It's also one of the fastest brassica greens to bolt, sending up a flower stalk (still edible, and its own thing in a salad) at the first excuse.

## When to Sow

Soil temperature range is 8-25°C. As a southern Australian guide, sow from late winter through spring (Aug-Nov) and again in early autumn (Mar-Apr); it will also grow through a mild summer if kept well watered, but flavour and leaf quality both suffer in the heat.

Sow too early into cold soil and germination just slows down rather than failing — rocket is forgiving at the cool end. Sow into peak summer heat, though, and expect fast bolting and leaves so peppery-hot they're better cooked than eaten raw.

## Sowing

Rocket is direct-sown; it grows quickly enough from seed that transplanting offers little advantage and its taproot doesn't love disturbance. Sow shallow at 5 mm — rocket seed is fine and doesn't need to be buried deep.

Space plants 10 cm apart in rows 20 cm apart, though rocket is commonly grown thicker than this as a cut-and-come-again "baby leaf" crop, sown almost like a lawn and cut with scissors. At wider spacing you get larger individual leaves; crowding mostly just means smaller leaves and an earlier first cut, not disease or failure — this is one of the more crowd-tolerant crops in this batch.

## Growing On

Rocket needs surprisingly little once it's up — the main lever you have is water. Keep it consistently moist and it grows quickly and stays comparatively mild; let it dry out or sit in a hot spell and it both bolts sooner and turns hotter in flavour, sometimes within days. A light feed isn't essential but speeds regrowth between cuts if you're harvesting it hard and often.

## Harvest

Baby leaf rocket is ready in as little as three weeks; leave it longer for larger, more mature leaves closer to the full 30-40 days. Cut with scissors an inch or so above the crown rather than pulling individual leaves — this is the fastest way to harvest and the plant regrows readily from the cut point. Rocket handles repeated hard cutting better than almost anything else in this guide; expect 3-4 cuts from one sowing before quality drops or it bolts. Once it does bolt, the leaves around the flower stalk turn noticeably hotter and more bitter — pull the plant and resow rather than trying to nurse it along.

## Succession

With a 14-day succession interval and such a short time to first harvest, rocket is a genuine "keep it coming" crop — sow a new short row every two weeks through the cooler months and you'll have a rolling supply without a single planting having to carry the whole season. Expect 5-6 sowings across a typical spring-to-autumn run, skipping the hottest weeks of summer unless you're prepared for hot, quick-to-bolt leaves.

## Common Problems

**Flea beetles riddling leaves with small round holes.** These small jumping beetles are a classic brassica pest and rocket is one of their favourite targets, especially on young seedlings. Cover new sowings with fine insect netting from the day you sow, and keep plants growing vigorously — stressed, slow-growing seedlings take more damage.

**Leaves turning unpleasantly hot and bitter.** Almost always heat or moisture stress, sometimes the first visible sign of bolting. Harvest promptly, increase watering, and expect this to be a recurring issue with any sowing made too close to summer.

**Bolting within a few weeks of sowing.** Triggered by heat and long daylight hours, and rocket is quicker to bolt than most of the other greens here. Sow in the cooler part of the window, harvest hard and early rather than waiting for full size, and treat a bolted planting as done rather than something to nurse back.

## In the Bed

Rocket is a Brassicaceae, in the same family as mizuna, tatsoi, pak choi, and mustard greens, so don't stack successive plantings of these into the same bed without a break — they share pests (flea beetle, cabbage white caterpillar) and some soil-borne diseases like clubroot. It's fast-finishing and shallow-rooted, making it a good gap-filler between slower crops, and its low habit means it won't shade out neighbours. Follow a rocket crop with something outside the brassica family — a legume or a root crop — to break the pest and disease cycle.
