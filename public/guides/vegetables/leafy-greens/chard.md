## Overview

Chard is silverbeet by another name — the same plant, and in most Australian kitchens and nurseries "silverbeet" is what you'll actually hear used. It's a big, tough, heavy-cropping green that will sit in the ground and keep producing for months where lettuce or spinach would have bolted and finished long ago.

What sets chard apart from the other greens here is that it's genuinely tolerant of a wide range of conditions — it copes with heat far better than spinach and with cold far better than most brassica greens, which is why its soil temperature range is the widest in this batch. The trade-off is that it's a heavier feeder, and a chard bed that isn't fed well produces thin, pale stalks and small leaves instead of the thick, glossy ribs it's known for.

Grown well, one chard planting can realistically feed a household for the better part of a season with regular cutting.

## When to Sow

Soil temperature range for sowing is 10-30°C, one of the broadest windows of any leafy green, which is what makes chard so forgiving on timing. As a southern Australian guide, sow from spring (Sep-Nov) through to autumn (Mar-Apr), avoiding only the coldest weeks of winter.

Sow too early into cold soil below about 10°C and germination is very slow or fails outright — chard seed (technically a seed cluster, which is why it's often sown thinly and thinned after) needs real warmth to get going. Sow too late into the cold months and young plants sit stalled rather than growing away, though established chard will tolerate a light frost once it has some size on it.

## Sowing

Chard suits either direct sowing or transplanting from punnets — transplants are useful if you want a head start in a shoulder season, while direct sowing is simpler in the main spring and autumn windows. Sow at 15 mm, the deepest of any crop in this batch, reflecting the larger seed cluster.

Space plants 25 cm apart in rows 40 cm apart — chard grows big, with broad leaves and thick stalks, and crowded plants produce noticeably thinner stalks and smaller leaves as they compete for light and root room. This isn't a crop you can pack in tightly and expect full-size results.

## Growing On

Chard is a heavier feeder than most leafy greens in this batch, and it shows: skimp on nitrogen and you get pale, thin leaves rather than the deep green, glossy growth it's capable of. Work compost or aged manure into the bed before planting and follow up with a liquid feed every few weeks through the cropping period, since this is a plant you'll be harvesting from repeatedly over a long stretch. Consistent watering matters too, mostly to keep stalks crisp rather than to prevent bolting — chard is far less bolt-prone than spinach or lettuce.

## Harvest

Start picking outer leaves once they're a reasonable eating size, generally from about 8 weeks, well before the plant reaches full size. Cut or snap outer stalks near the base rather than pulling, which avoids damaging the crown. This is a genuine cut-and-come-again crop — take no more than a third of the leaves at any one time and leave the central growing point untouched, and a healthy plant will keep pushing new leaves for months. Left unpicked, older outer leaves simply get tougher and more fibrous rather than the plant bolting quickly, so there's more slack here than with spinach if you fall behind on harvesting.

## Succession

With a 21-day succession interval, chard doesn't need the same tight sowing cadence as faster crops — its long productive life means one or two well-timed plantings (spring and late summer) can carry a household through most of the year. Where a steady supply matters more than variety, 2-3 successions spaced three weeks apart across spring will stagger maturity and stretch the harvest window further.

## Common Problems

**Leaf miners leaving pale, papery blotches.** Larvae tunnel between the leaf surfaces, and chard's broad leaves are a favourite target. Squash the larva visible inside the blotch, strip and dispose of badly affected leaves, and cover young plantings with insect netting to stop the adult flies laying eggs.

**Thin, pale stalks instead of thick, glossy ones.** Almost always a feeding or crowding issue — chard needs both room and nitrogen to bulk up. Thin overcrowded rows, top-dress with compost or a nitrogen-rich liquid feed, and keep watering consistent.

**Bolting in a sudden hot spell after a cold snap.** Chard is fairly bolt-resistant but can be triggered by a cold period (vernalisation) followed by rising temperatures and long days, similar to beetroot, its close relative. Once a flower stalk appears, leaf quality declines; harvest what's usable and replant rather than waiting for it to recover.

## In the Bed

Chard belongs to the Amaranthaceae family, the same family as beetroot and spinach (though a different genus to true spinach), so avoid following it with beetroot in rotation and treat it as unrelated to the Brassicaceae greens like rocket or pak choi. Its long standing time and large size mean it holds a bed for a while, so plan around that rather than expecting a quick turnover; it also casts a fair amount of shade once mature, which can be useful for shading a shorter, heat-sensitive crop planted alongside it in summer.
