## Overview

Pak choi (also seen written as bok choy) forms a loose upright cluster of glossy, spoon-shaped leaves on pale, crisp stems — closer to a small, open cabbage than a leafy salad green, and it's usually cooked rather than eaten raw. It's a reliable, quick-cropping brassica that does well in a wide range of home gardens.

The one thing that catches people out with pak choi specifically is transplant shock: young pak choi seedlings that are stressed by a cold snap, root disturbance, or a dry spell shortly after transplanting have a strong tendency to bolt prematurely, sending up a flower stalk when the plant is still small rather than forming a proper cluster. This is more pronounced in pak choi than in most of the other Asian brassica greens, which is why it's often recommended as a direct-sown crop, or transplanted only when conditions are stable and the seedlings are handled gently.

## When to Sow

Soil temperature range is 10-25°C. As a southern Australian guide, sow from early spring (Sep-Oct) once the soil has warmed a little, and again through autumn (Mar-Apr); it's less cold-tolerant at germination than tatsoi, needing that slightly warmer minimum to get started reliably.

Sow too early into cold spring soil and, beyond slow germination, any subsequent cold snap while the seedling is small is a classic bolting trigger for this crop. Sow too late into rising spring heat, or transplant carelessly during a hot dry spell, and the same bolting response shows up from heat and moisture stress instead.

## Sowing

Pak choi can be direct-sown or transplanted, but given its sensitivity to transplant shock, direct sowing is the more forgiving option if you can manage it — it avoids the root disturbance and the check in growth that so often triggers early bolting. If transplanting, do it on a mild, overcast day and keep the seedlings well watered for the first week to minimise stress. Sow at 5 mm.

Space plants 20 cm apart in rows 30 cm apart. Crowded plants produce smaller, thinner clusters and hold more humidity around the base, which increases the risk of rot at the crown — this is a crop where giving it room pays off more than usual for a leafy green.

## Growing On

Consistent moisture is the priority — pak choi's crisp stems are mostly water, and any stress from drying out (or the reverse, a sudden soak after a dry spell) is a common bolting trigger on top of transplant shock. Feed lightly through the growing period to keep growth steady rather than starting and stopping, since irregular growth is exactly the kind of stress that pushes this crop toward premature flowering.

## Harvest

Baby pak choi can be cut whole from around three weeks for a quick stir-fry-sized cluster; full-size plants take the full 40-70 days to form a proper rosette of thick stems. Harvest by cutting the whole plant at the base just above the roots — pak choi doesn't regrow well from a partial cut the way looser-leafed greens do, so most growers treat it as a single-harvest crop per plant rather than cut-and-come-again. If left too long, the centre of the cluster starts to elongate and push up a flower stalk, and the stems that were crisp and mild turn tougher and more bitter.

## Succession

With a 14-day succession interval and a tendency to bolt if stressed, pak choi benefits from staggered small sowings rather than one large planting that all matures — and is all vulnerable to a bad stretch of weather — at the same time. Across a typical spring and autumn run in southern Australia, 3-4 successions in each season is a reasonable target, direct-sowing where you can to reduce the bolting risk that comes with transplanting.

## Common Problems

**Premature bolting in young plants.** Almost always transplant shock, a cold snap, or moisture stress hitting the plant while it's still small. Favour direct sowing, transplant only on mild days, and keep watering even through establishment; once bolting starts in pak choi there's no reversing it.

**Slugs and snails hollowing out the crisp stems.** The moist, tender base of the plant is an easy target, especially in a crowded or poorly drained bed. Hand-pick after dark, keep the base of plants clear of damp mulch, and consider a pet-safe iron-based bait if pressure is high.

**Flea beetles shot-holing the leaves.** A common brassica pest that disfigures leaves without necessarily killing the plant, but is worse on stressed or slow-growing seedlings. Net young plantings with fine insect mesh and keep growth steady to reduce vulnerability.

## In the Bed

Pak choi is a Brassicaceae, sharing the same pest and disease pressures as rocket, mizuna, tatsoi, and mustard greens, so rotate away from those crops rather than following one straight after another. It grows upright with a fairly compact footprint, so it won't smother neighbouring plants, and it finishes reasonably quickly, freeing the bed for a follow-on crop from outside the brassica family to break the pest cycle.
