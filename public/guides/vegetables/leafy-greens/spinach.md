## Overview

True spinach (not silverbeet, which is a different plant despite the shared name in some kitchens) is a fast, tender green that's at its best in cool weather and at its worst the moment it gets hot. It's less forgiving of heat than lettuce, bolting sooner and with less warning, which is why it's often treated as a short, intense cool-season crop rather than something you try to stretch across a whole year.

The thing that trips people up is timing it too optimistically — spinach wants genuinely cool soil to germinate reliably and to grow leaves rather than flower stalks, and in a lot of southern Australian gardens that's a narrower window than the seed packet's generic advice suggests.

Grown in the right months it's quick, low-maintenance, and productive in a small footprint. Grown into warmth it runs to seed almost before you've had a proper harvest.

## When to Sow

Soil temperature range for germination is 7-24°C, but spinach reliably germinates and grows leaf, not flower stalk, only in the cooler part of that band — aim for the lower half where you can. In southern Australia that means autumn (Mar-May) and late winter to early spring (Aug-Sep) are the reliable windows; treat summer as off-limits.

Sow too early into warm soil (over about 20°C) and germination is patchy and slow — spinach seed can actually go dormant in warm conditions rather than simply germinating slower. Sow too late, close to the warmth of late spring or summer, and the plant bolts to seed within weeks of emerging, before you get a meaningful harvest.

## Sowing

Spinach is direct-sown — it dislikes root disturbance and transplants poorly, so starting it in situ avoids the setback a transplant shock would cause. Sow at 10 mm, a touch deeper than most leafy greens in this batch, which suits its larger seed and helps keep it in cooler, more consistently moist soil.

Space plants 10 cm apart in rows 30 cm apart. Crowding matters here mostly for airflow — dense spinach holds humidity at leaf level and is more prone to downy mildew, though thinning is also just a way to get a first light harvest for free.

## Growing On

Spinach wants consistent moisture and a fertile bed — it's a comparatively fast grower for the size of its leaf, and that pace needs feeding to match, particularly nitrogen. A side dressing of compost or a liquid feed partway through the crop keeps leaves dark and tender rather than pale and thin. Watch soil moisture closely, as any lengthening of daylight or warm spell will push it toward bolting faster than watering alone can prevent, so this is a crop where getting the timing right matters more than getting the aftercare right.

## Harvest

Start harvesting outer leaves once plants have five or six true leaves, usually a few weeks before the crop is technically "mature." For cut-and-come-again picking, take the outer leaves and leave the central rosette to keep producing — spinach will keep pushing new leaves from the centre for several weeks if you don't strip it bare. Once you see a central stem elongating, that's the plant bolting; leaves get noticeably more bitter and thinner from that point, so harvest the whole plant at once rather than trying to keep picking.

## Succession

Sow every 14 days through the cool season to keep a steady supply, since any single sowing has a short productive window before bolting ends it. In practice that's around 3-4 sowings in autumn and another 2-3 in late winter and early spring — spinach isn't a crop to try to stretch across a whole season with wide gaps, because a late gap just means an unproductive one.

## Common Problems

**Bolting shortly after establishing.** Caused by warm soil, lengthening daylight, or a sowing made too late in the window. Sow earlier in the cool season next time and choose bolt-resistant varieties for shoulder-season sowings; once bolting starts there's no bringing leaf quality back.

**Downy mildew (yellow patches on top of leaves, grey fuzz underneath).** Favoured by humid, still conditions and overhead watering late in the day. Improve airflow with wider spacing, water at the base in the morning, and remove affected leaves promptly rather than letting spores spread through the row.

**Leaf miners tunnelling pale trails through leaves.** Larvae feed inside the leaf tissue, leaving papery blotches. Squash the larvae inside the leaf by hand where you find a trail, remove badly affected leaves, and use fine insect netting over new sowings to stop adult flies laying eggs.

## In the Bed

True spinach belongs to the Amaranthaceae family (related to beetroot and silverbeet, but a distinct species), so it's not a Brassicaceae rotation concern alongside crops like rocket or pak choi. It's fast to establish and fast to finish, making it a useful gap-filler between slower crops, and its short life in the ground means the bed frees up quickly for something else — a follow-on brassica or a summer crop once the spinach bolts or is cleared.
