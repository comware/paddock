## Overview

Tatsoi is a small, dense Asian brassica that grows as a flat, ground-hugging rosette of dark, spoon-shaped leaves — a distinctive shape that sets it apart from every other green in this batch, most of which grow more upright. That flat habit is also its most practical feature: whole young rosettes can be harvested and used more or less intact, which makes tatsoi popular both in stir-fries and as a striking addition to a salad mix.

It's one of the more cold-tolerant of the Asian greens, handling a light frost without much complaint, which makes it a genuinely useful crop for keeping something fresh and leafy going through the coolest part of the year when many other greens have stalled. Heat is where it struggles — hot weather sends it to seed quickly and coarsens the leaves.

## When to Sow

Soil temperature range is 8-25°C. As a southern Australian guide, sow in autumn (Mar-May) for a crop that will carry through winter, and again in early spring (Aug-Sep) before the weather warms; it's a poor choice for a summer sowing.

Sow too early into warm autumn soil or too late into a warming spring and it bolts quickly, the flat rosette bulging up into a flower stalk before the leaves have properly sized up. Sown at the right time, tatsoi is genuinely one of the better cold-season performers here, tolerating soil temperatures and air frosts that would stall most of the other crops in this guide.

## Sowing

Tatsoi suits either direct sowing or transplanting; direct sowing is straightforward for a crop grown at close spacing, while transplants suit an early start ahead of a cold snap. Sow shallow at 5 mm.

Space plants 15 cm apart in rows 25 cm apart for full rosettes at harvest, though tatsoi is also grown closer together for a baby-leaf style crop. Its flat, spreading growth habit means crowded plants compete directly for the light hitting the rosette, producing smaller, more upright plants rather than the classic flat form — mostly a cosmetic and yield issue rather than a disease one, though very tight spacing in humid weather can encourage rot where leaves overlap and stay damp.

## Growing On

Tatsoi is low-maintenance through the cool season it's suited to — consistent moisture and an occasional light feed are about all it asks. Because the rosette sits flat and close to the soil, keep an eye on mulch and debris around the base, since damp, still air at ground level is exactly where slugs and fungal problems get started. It doesn't need protection from cold once established, but a young seedling caught by a hard frost before it has some size can be knocked back.

## Harvest

Baby tatsoi can be picked from around three weeks; full rosettes are ready by 35-56 days, at which point they've formed the characteristic flat, dense spoon-leaf shape. You can harvest the whole rosette by cutting at the base, or take outer leaves individually and let the centre keep producing — the second approach turns it into a genuine cut-and-come-again crop, though regrowth is slower in the cold months it's usually grown in. Once the centre of the rosette starts to lift and elongate, that's the first sign of bolting; harvest the whole plant at that point rather than waiting, since flavour and texture decline quickly once flowering starts.

## Succession

With a 14-day succession interval, 2-3 successions through autumn and another 2 in early spring cover tatsoi's realistic sowing windows in southern Australia — because its main season is the cool months, the succession cadence here is naturally shorter than for crops with a longer sowing window, rather than something to stretch across the whole year.

## Common Problems

**Flea beetles putting small shot-holes in leaves.** A shared brassica pest, generally less severe in the cool weather tatsoi favours than it is on a summer-sown crop like rocket. Net seedlings with fine insect mesh if beetles are active locally, and keep plants growing steadily.

**Rot or yellowing where leaves sit against wet soil.** The flat rosette habit means the underside of the leaves is often close to or touching damp ground, especially after rain. Improve drainage, avoid overhead watering late in the day, and clear mulch away from direct contact with the leaves.

**Early bolting from a sowing made too late in spring.** Rising temperatures and lengthening days push tatsoi toward flowering fast once warm weather sets in. Stick to the cool-season windows and treat any late-spring sowing as a short-lived crop rather than expecting it to hold.

## In the Bed

Tatsoi is a Brassicaceae, alongside rocket, mizuna, pak choi, and mustard greens, so avoid rotating straight from one of these into another in the same ground. Its flat, low habit means it won't compete for light with anything planted nearby, and it finishes quickly enough to free the bed for a spring crop once it's done. Follow it with a non-brassica to keep pest and disease pressure from building up in the same patch of soil.
