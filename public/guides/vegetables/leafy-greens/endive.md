## Overview

Endive is a chicory relative grown for a head of frilly or broad-leafed greens that are naturally bitter, and that bitterness is both its defining character and the reason it needs a bit more attention than a straightforward lettuce. Curly endive (frisée) and broad-leafed escarole are the two common forms, and both benefit from the same basic trick: blocking light from the inner leaves for the last week or two before harvest to mellow the bitterness and pale the heart.

That extra step — blanching — is what earns endive its "moderate" difficulty rating here rather than "easy." Left unblanched, endive is still perfectly edible but noticeably sharper, which some cooks want and others don't. It also handles a light frost well, and a touch of cold actually improves flavour by softening the bitterness naturally, similar to what blanching achieves by a different route.

## When to Sow

Soil temperature range is 10-25°C. As a southern Australian guide, sow in late summer to autumn (Feb-Apr) for a crop that matures into the cooler, frost-touched part of the year when its flavour is best, or in spring (Sep-Oct) for a summer crop, accepting it will be more bitter without a cold snap to help it along.

Sow too early into a hot summer and the plant is prone to bolting before it forms a proper head, with heat also making the leaves more bitter than usual. Sow too late into the cold of deep winter and growth slows to a crawl; endive tolerates frost well once established but doesn't grow much through the coldest weeks.

## Sowing

Endive can be direct-sown or transplanted from punnets — transplanting is common for an autumn crop to give it a head start before the cold slows growth. Sow shallow at 5 mm.

Space plants 25 cm apart in rows 35 cm apart, giving the head room to form fully. Crowded plants produce smaller, looser heads and hold more moisture around the base, which increases the risk of the rots endive is somewhat prone to in humid conditions — proper spacing matters more here than for a simple loose-leaf lettuce.

## Growing On

Even, consistent moisture keeps growth steady and helps limit excess bitterness, much as it does for lettuce. About two to three weeks before you plan to harvest, blanch the head by loosely tying the outer leaves up over the centre, or covering the plant with a plate or an upturned pot, to exclude light from the heart — this is the single technique most associated with growing endive well, and it's what turns a sharp, tough head into something genuinely pleasant raw. Make sure the plant is dry before tying it up, since trapped moisture in a blanched head is a fast route to rot.

## Harvest

Endive is ready from about 45-77 days, sooner if you're picking outer leaves as a loose-leaf crop rather than waiting for a full head. If blanching, check under the tie or cover after a week or two — the inner leaves should have paled and softened in bitterness. Cut the whole head at the base once it's reached a useful size; unlike some looser greens, endive doesn't regrow strongly from a cut crown, so treat it as a single harvest per plant. Left too long, especially once warm weather sets in, the head will bolt and the leaves turn markedly more bitter and tough.

## Succession

With a 21-day succession interval, 2-3 successions through the main autumn window, spaced three weeks apart, will stagger maturity and stretch harvests through the cooler months when endive is at its best. A smaller spring sowing or two can bridge the gap into summer if you don't mind the extra bitterness that comes without a cold snap to soften it.

## Common Problems

**Excessive bitterness even after blanching.** Usually a sign of heat or moisture stress during growth, or a blanching period that was too short. Keep watering consistent through the growing season, and extend the blanching period by a few extra days if the first check still tastes sharp.

**Rot in the blanched heart.** Trapped moisture under a blanching cover or tie is the main cause, especially after rain. Only blanch dry plants, choose a breathable cover where possible, and check the heart every few days rather than leaving it covered for weeks unchecked.

**Bolting in a late-spring or summer sowing.** Rising temperatures and long daylight hours push endive toward flowering before a proper head forms. Favour the autumn sowing window where possible, and treat any warm-season planting as a shorter-lived, more bitter crop.

## In the Bed

Endive belongs to the Asteraceae family along with lettuce and radicchio, so avoid stacking successive plantings of any of these three into the same ground without a break, even though their pest profile is generally lighter than the brassica greens. It forms a moderately sized head and needs its full spacing to do so well, but doesn't shade out neighbours dramatically. Follow it with a heavier feeder, since endive doesn't draw hard on the soil, or rotate in a brassica to keep the bed's pest pressure spread across different plant families.
