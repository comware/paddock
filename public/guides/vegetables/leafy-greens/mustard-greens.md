## Overview

Mustard greens are the spiciest and most heat-tolerant of the Asian brassica greens grown here — a step up in both respects from mizuna and rocket, with a genuine horseradish-like sharpness that intensifies the older and more stressed the leaf gets. Some growers pick them young and mild for salads; others let them grow on and use them almost exclusively cooked, where heat mellows the sharpness considerably.

Because they tolerate warmth better than mizuna, tatsoi, or pak choi, mustard greens are a useful crop for bridging the gap into the warmer parts of the leafy-green season when the others have already bolted or gone tough. The trade-off for that heat tolerance is flavour: leaves left to grow larger, or grown under any stress, get noticeably hotter and more bitter, so harvest timing matters more here than with most greens if you want them mild.

## When to Sow

Soil temperature range is 10-25°C. As a southern Australian guide, sow from early spring (Sep-Oct) through summer (Dec-Jan) and again in autumn (Mar-Apr) — the widest practical sowing season of any brassica green in this batch, extending further into summer than rocket, mizuna, or tatsoi.

Sow too early into cold spring soil below about 10°C and germination is slow and uneven. Sowing into summer heat is generally fine for mustard greens compared to its relatives, but leaves harvested from a hot, dry planting will be noticeably hotter and more fibrous than the same variety grown in milder conditions — a flavour cost rather than a crop failure.

## Sowing

Mustard greens are direct-sown; they establish quickly from seed and there's little to gain from the extra handling of a transplant. Sow shallow at 5 mm.

Space plants 15 cm apart in rows 25 cm apart. Crowding produces smaller leaves and, in humid weather, increases the risk of fungal problems at the base — not dramatically different from the other brassica greens here, though mustard's tolerance of warmer, drier conditions makes crowding-related disease somewhat less of an issue than it is for a crop like pak choi.

## Growing On

Mustard greens need less cosseting than most of the greens in this guide — they'll grow through conditions that would stall or bolt tatsoi or pak choi. Even so, consistent watering keeps leaves milder and more tender; a plant left to dry out repeatedly produces tougher, hotter leaves faster than one kept evenly moist. A light feed during growth keeps the crop productive, but this isn't a demanding feeder.

## Harvest

Baby leaves can be picked from around three weeks for a milder, salad-ready green; left to the full 30-45 days, leaves are larger, more strongly flavoured, and generally best cooked. It's a solid cut-and-come-again crop — take outer leaves and leave the centre to keep producing, and expect several pickings from one sowing before quality declines or the plant bolts. The older and larger a leaf gets, the hotter and more bitter it becomes, so for a mild harvest pick often and pick young rather than letting the planting build up size.

## Succession

With a 14-day succession interval and the widest sowing window of the brassica greens in this batch, mustard greens suit a long run of successions from spring through to autumn — 5-6 sowings across a season is realistic where the other Asian greens might manage half that. Staggering sowings also spreads out the point at which leaves get hot and coarse, so you're not stuck eating an entire bed of over-mature, fiery leaves at once.

## Common Problems

**Leaves turning unpleasantly hot and tough.** Usually just age or heat stress rather than disease — mustard greens intensify in flavour as they grow and as conditions get harsher. Harvest younger and more often if mildness matters, and treat older plantings as a cooking green rather than a salad one.

**Flea beetles shot-holing young leaves.** As with the other brassica greens here, flea beetles are drawn to seedlings and stressed plants. Net new sowings with fine insect mesh, and keep plants growing steadily to reduce their appeal.

**Cabbage white caterpillars chewing large ragged holes.** The caterpillars of the cabbage white butterfly target mustard greens along with the rest of the brassica family. Check leaf undersides regularly for the pale yellow eggs and green caterpillars, hand-pick where practical, and net susceptible crops during the butterfly's active months.

## In the Bed

Mustard greens are a Brassicaceae, sharing pests and diseases with rocket, mizuna, tatsoi, and pak choi, so avoid running successive brassica plantings through the same ground without a break. They're upright and reasonably compact, not shading out neighbours significantly, and their tolerance of warmer conditions makes them a useful bed-filler through the shoulder of summer when other brassica greens have finished. Follow with a non-brassica crop to keep the rotation clean and reduce build-up of soil-borne brassica diseases like clubroot.
