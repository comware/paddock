## Overview

Lettuce is the crop most people start with, and for good reason — it germinates readily, doesn't demand much from the soil, and rewards you within a season rather than a year. It ranges from loose-leaf types you pick a few leaves from at a time, through to butterheads and cos/romaine that form a proper head.

The one thing that catches almost everyone out is heat. Lettuce is a cool-season plant at heart, and once daytime temperatures push into the low 30s it bolts — sends up a flower stalk — and the leaves turn bitter almost overnight. In southern Australia that means your sowing window closes earlier in the season than you'd expect, and a crop that looked fine on Monday can be inedible by Friday during a heatwave.

Grown at the right time of year, lettuce is close to foolproof. Grown into summer, it's a lesson in bolting.

## When to Sow

Germinate at 7-24°C soil temperature — lettuce seed is oddly fussy at the top end, and germination rates drop off noticeably above about 25°C soil temperature, which is one of the few vegetables where hot soil is a bigger problem than cold. As a southern Australian guide, sow from late winter through spring (Aug-Nov) and again in autumn (Mar-May); skip the height of summer unless you're growing a heat-tolerant variety in part shade.

Sow too early into cold, wet winter soil and germination is slow and patchy, with damping off a real risk. Sow too late into summer heat and the crop bolts before it sizes up, or germination fails outright because the soil is too warm for the seed to break dormancy.

## Sowing

Lettuce works either direct-sown or transplanted from punnets, and the choice mostly comes down to convenience — transplants give you a head start and let you dodge a slug-heavy establishment period, while direct sowing suits loose-leaf types you'll thin as you go. Sow at 5 mm, barely covering the seed, since lettuce needs some light to germinate well.

Space plants 25 cm apart in rows 30 cm apart for full heads; crowding produces smaller, looser heads and holds humidity around the lower leaves, which is where fungal rots tend to start.

## Growing On

Even moisture is the single biggest thing lettuce needs. Inconsistent watering — a dry stretch followed by a soak — is what produces bitter, tough leaves even outside of heat stress, because the plant treats the dry spell as a signal to bolt. Keep the root zone consistently damp, mulch to buffer against temperature swings, and feed lightly with a balanced liquid feed every couple of weeks; lettuce isn't a heavy feeder and excess nitrogen mostly just makes for soft, disease-prone growth.

## Harvest

Loose-leaf varieties are ready to pick from about 6 weeks, taking outer leaves as needed. Heading types take the full 45-85 days and are ready when the head feels firm when gently squeezed. For cut-and-come-again leaf types, take only the outer third of the plant at each pick and leave the growing centre intact — strip it back too hard and it won't recover in time for the next cut. A head left too long in warm weather will start pushing up a central flower stalk; once that starts, the leaves turn bitter fast and there's no reversing it.

## Succession

With a 14-day succession interval, sow a new short row every two weeks through the cool months rather than one big block. In a typical southern Australian season that's realistically 4-6 sowings across spring and another 3-4 in autumn, timed so you're not left with a glut of heads all wanting to be eaten (or bolting) in the same week.

## Common Problems

**Bitter, tough leaves in warm weather.** Caused by heat stress and irregular watering, sometimes the first sign of bolting. Harvest earlier than planned, increase watering frequency, and accept that late-planted lettuce in a warm spell won't be at its best — pull it and replant when temperatures ease.

**Slugs and snails shredding seedlings.** Young lettuce is soft and irresistible to slugs, especially in damp conditions. Go out with a torch after dark and hand-pick, or use a pet-safe iron-based bait; raised beds and morning watering (so leaves dry out by evening) both help.

**Bolting before the head forms.** Triggered by heat, long daylight hours, or moisture stress, and once the flower stalk starts the plant reallocates energy away from leaf growth. Choose heat-tolerant or slow-bolt varieties for late-spring sowings, and time main crops to mature before the heat arrives rather than during it.

## In the Bed

Lettuce is a member of the Asteraceae (daisy) family, unrelated to the brassica greens in this batch, so it's a useful rotation partner alongside them rather than a competitor for the same soil-borne pests. It's shallow-rooted and fast-finishing, which makes it a good filler crop between slower-growing vegetables, and its low, spreading habit means it won't shade out neighbours the way taller crops will. Follow it with a heavier feeder like brassicas or tomatoes, since lettuce leaves the bed in reasonable condition without having drawn hard on it.
