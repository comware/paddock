## Overview

Parsnip is the slowest and least forgiving of the common root crops, and most of what makes it
demanding happens before the seed even germinates. It's a long-season crop with notoriously
short-lived, slow, and erratic-germinating seed, which means the usual root-crop rules — deep loose
soil, no transplanting, thin early — matter more here than anywhere else, with almost no margin to
correct mistakes over its long time in the ground.

## When to Sow

Parsnip germinates from about 10°C, but slowly and unevenly even in ideal conditions — three to four
weeks is normal, and it can be patchier still if the seed is more than a year old, since parsnip
seed loses viability faster than most. In temperate southern Australia, sow from early spring once
soil has warmed past 10°C, roughly September into November, giving the crop the full 120-170 days it
needs to mature before the following winter or the heat of a following summer sets in.

Sow too early into cold, wet soil and seed rots before it germinates. Sow too late and the long
maturity period runs the crop into summer heat, which stresses the roots and can cause splitting or
poor flavour development — parsnip actually improves with a touch of cold at the end of its cycle,
which is why many gardeners leave roots in the ground until after the first frosts.

## Sowing

Always direct sow, and use fresh seed — this matters more for parsnip than for any other root crop,
since old seed germinates so poorly that a sowing from two-year-old seed can fail almost entirely.
Like carrot, its close relative, the taproot forms from a single unbranched growing point and any
disturbance from transplanting forks or stunts it, so transplants are never a practical option.

Prepare the bed deeply, to at least 30 cm of loose, stone-free soil — parsnip roots commonly grow
longer than carrots, and forking around an obstruction is even more likely given how long the root
has to grow. Sow at 15 mm depth, a little deeper than carrot, and sow more seed than you need since
germination rates are unreliable; some gardeners sow a fast radish alongside to mark the row while
the slow parsnip seed is still germinating underground.

## Growing On

Keep the row consistently moist for the full three to four weeks it can take to germinate — this is
the single hardest part of growing parsnip, since a dry surface during that long window will simply
stop germination rather than delay it, and by the time you notice nothing has come up it can be too
late in the season to resow.

Thin early once seedlings are established, to 10 cm apart. Because parsnip occupies the bed for so
long, an unthinned row wastes nearly four months of ground producing a tangle of undersized roots
rather than a proper crop — this is the most common way a parsnip planting disappoints. Weed
carefully in the early months while the slow-growing seedlings are easily outcompeted; once
established, parsnip's substantial foliage shades out most competition itself.

## Harvest

You can't see the root, so judge by the diameter of the foliage crown at soil level and by time —
most varieties are ready from 100 days, with shoulder diameter of 4-5 cm being a reasonable
indicator once you brush soil away from a test root. Unlike faster roots, parsnip tolerates and
often benefits from staying in the ground well past the minimum maturity date; a frost or two
converts some of the root's starch to sugar and noticeably sweetens the flavour, which is why
parsnip is traditionally a winter harvest crop.

## Succession

Parsnip is not a succession crop. Its long season means one sowing occupies the bed for three to
four months minimum, and its improvement with cold weather means the point of growing it is a single
late-season or winter harvest rather than a rolling supply — there's no practical way to stagger
sowings usefully within a single growing year.

## Common Problems

**Poor or patchy germination** — caused most often by old seed, since parsnip seed loses viability
quickly, or by the row drying out during the long germination period. Fix by buying fresh seed each
season and keeping the row damp until seedlings are visible.

**Forked or stunted roots** — caused by stony or compacted soil, or root disturbance from attempted
transplanting. Fix by digging the bed deeper and clearing debris before sowing; there's no fixing a
forked root once it's grown.

**Canker (dark, rotten patches on the shoulder)** — caused by wet, poorly drained soil, fresh
manure, or damage to the root shoulder from careless hoeing. Fix by improving drainage, avoiding
fresh manure ahead of this crop, and choosing canker-resistant varieties in districts where it's a
recurring problem.

## In the Bed

Parsnip belongs to the umbellifer family alongside carrot, celery, and dill for rotation purposes —
avoid following those crops with parsnip and leave several years before returning umbellifers to the
same ground. Its very long occupancy, 120-170 days and often longer if left for frost-sweetening,
means it ties up a bed for most of a season, so plan around it rather than trying to fit other crops
in afterwards; a hungry follow-on crop benefits from the deep cultivation the parsnip bed was given.
