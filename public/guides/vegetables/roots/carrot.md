## Overview

Carrot is a taproot, and everything about growing it well comes back to that one fact. The root only
grows straight and long if nothing gets in its way early on — no compacted soil, no stones, no clump
of clay, and above all no disturbance to the young root tip. Get the bed right before you sow and
the plant does most of the rest itself.

## When to Sow

Carrot seed will germinate anywhere from 8°C to 30°C, but it's slow and patchy at the cold end —
germination can take three weeks at 8°C versus a week at 20°C. In temperate southern Australia that
usually means late winter through to early autumn, roughly August to March in Central Victoria and
similar climates, avoiding only the coldest months. Coastal or warmer districts can push both ends
of that window further.

Sow too early into cold, wet soil and seed rots before it germinates, or germinates so slowly that
weeds get a head start and smother the row. Sow into the heat of a dry summer without attention to
watering and the row can fail to germinate at all — carrot seed needs continuous moisture for the
1-3 weeks it takes to come up, and a surface that dries out even once during that window can kill
the whole sowing.

## Sowing

Always direct sow. Carrot roots are extremely sensitive to disturbance — the taproot forms from a
single growing point, and if that point is bent, broken, or forced sideways while transplanting, the
root forks, twists, or stops growing straight down and instead grows squat and misshapen. There is
no transplanting technique that reliably avoids this, which is why carrot is essentially never sold
as seedlings.

Prepare the bed by digging deep and removing stones, clods, and old root debris to at least the
depth you expect the variety to grow — a 20 cm carrot needs 20 cm of loose, stone-free soil, not
just a loosened surface. Heavy clay or stony ground causes forking as the root tip meets resistance
and splits around it; on those soils, shorter or round-rooted varieties cope better than long
tapered ones. Sow seed thinly, only 5 mm deep — carrot seed is tiny and has little energy reserve,
so it can't push up through a thick covering.

## Growing On

Keep the row moist until germination is complete and even after — carrot seedlings are fine and slow
to establish, and a dry crust on the surface can stop them emerging at all even after they've
germinated below it. Weeding in the first month matters more for carrot than most crops, because the
seedlings are thin, grass-like, and slow, and can be smothered before you notice they're struggling.
Hand-weed carefully close to the row; a hoe run too close will cut roots you can't see yet.

Thinning is the single most commonly skipped step and the most common reason for a poor crop.
Carrots sown too thickly and left unthinned produce a mass of thin, tangled, stunted roots competing
for the same few centimetres of soil. Thin in stages — once seedlings have their first true leaf,
then again a few weeks later — down to the final spacing of around 5 cm. Thin in the evening and
water afterwards; the disturbance releases carrot scent that can attract carrot fly.

Carrot fly is a real risk in cooler, humid districts, less so in hot dry inland gardens. The fly is
attracted by the scent released when foliage is bruised or thinned, and its larvae tunnel into the
root. A physical barrier — fine insect mesh, or a solid barrier at least 60 cm high around the bed,
since the fly flies low — is more reliable than any spray. Avoid thinning on still, warm days when
the fly is most active.

## Harvest

You cannot see a carrot to judge it, so judge by the shoulders and by time. Once the
days-to-maturity window has passed, brush soil back from the top of a root or two to check shoulder
diameter — most varieties are ready between 1.5 and 3 cm across the shoulder, depending on type.
Pull one to check length and colour development before committing to the whole row. Carrots left in
the ground past maturity don't spoil quickly and can be stored there in mild climates, but in heavy
or wet soil they're prone to splitting and slug damage the longer they sit.

## Succession

Sow every 21 days through the sowing window for a continuous supply rather than one large harvest
all at once. Because carrots store reasonably well in the ground or in cool storage once mature,
successions don't need to be as tight as they do for genuinely perishable crops — but without them
you get a single glut followed by a gap.

## Common Problems

**Forked or twisted roots** — caused by stones, clods, compacted soil, or fresh manure in the row,
all of which deflect the growing tip. Fix by working the bed deeper and removing debris before the
next sowing; fresh manure should go on the bed for a previous crop, not this one.

**Thin, weak, tangled roots** — caused by failing to thin, so plants compete for space and none
develop properly. Fix by thinning earlier and harder next time; there's no way to correct an
unthinned row after the fact except to thin it late and accept a smaller harvest.

**Green shoulders** — caused by the top of the root being exposed to sunlight, which turns it green
and bitter. Fix by hilling a little soil over the shoulders as the roots size up, and sow slightly
deeper next time if it's a recurring issue.

## In the Bed

Carrot belongs to the umbellifer family (with parsnip, celery, and dill) for rotation purposes —
avoid following it with those crops, and leave 2-3 years before returning umbellifers to the same
ground to break any carrot fly or root disease cycle. It occupies the bed for 70-100 days, a
moderate-length crop. Good follow-on crops are leafy greens or legumes, which don't share pests with
roots and, in the case of legumes, will restore some of the nitrogen carrot doesn't use much of.
