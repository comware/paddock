## Overview

Radish is the fastest root in the garden and about the most forgiving — it's often the crop new
gardeners are told to start with because it's hard to get badly wrong in the time it takes to grow.
The catch is exactly that speed: because it moves from seed to harvest in under five weeks, timing
and thinning matter more than technique, and it punishes neglect quickly rather than slowly.

## When to Sow

Radish germinates across almost the full range from 8°C to 30°C, wider than most root crops, which
is why it can be sown for most of the year in temperate southern Australia — roughly August through
to April, avoiding only the depths of winter. It's genuinely one of the few crops that tolerates
both a cool start and a warm finish.

The real timing risk isn't germination, it's bolting. Radish sown in the lengthening days and rising
heat of late spring, or stressed by dryness at any time, tends to run to seed instead of forming a
root — you get a woody spike and flowers instead of the swollen root you wanted. Sow it as a
genuinely quick crop and keep it well watered rather than treating it as something you can leave for
a few weeks and come back to.

## Sowing

Direct sow only. Radish is one of the fastest-forming roots and has no tolerance at all for
transplant disturbance — the root swells within days of germination, and any check to growth at that
stage produces a forked, split, or misshapen root, or pushes the plant straight to bolting. There's
essentially no reason to transplant radish anyway, given how fast and reliably it germinates direct.

Sow shallowly, about 10 mm deep, and keep the row moist until germination, which typically takes
only 3-7 days. Soil doesn't need to be deep or stone-free the way carrot's does, since most
varieties form a root only a few centimetres across sitting near the surface — but a hard, crusted
surface will still stop fine seedlings emerging evenly.

## Growing On

Thin early and thin hard — radish is sown thickly because germination rates and speed make it
tempting to oversow, but a crowded row produces all leaf and no root, since the plants are competing
too hard for space to bother swelling. Thin to 5 cm as soon as seedlings show their first true leaf;
because the whole crop matures in under five weeks, there's no time to correct a badly thinned row
later the way you might with a slower crop.

Keep watering consistent. Radish's speed means a single dry day can be a meaningful fraction of its
whole growing period, and dry stress produces small, woody, overly hot-flavoured roots or pushes
bolting. There's very little else to manage in the time it's in the ground.

## Harvest

Judge readiness by the shoulders, which sit at or above the soil surface on most varieties — once
the root shoulder reaches 2-3 cm across (varies by variety), it's ready. Radish doesn't improve with
more time in the ground the way some roots do; left too long it turns pithy, hollow, or hot and
eventually bolts, so check by pulling one test root around day 25 rather than waiting for the full
window to close.

## Succession

Sow every 14 days for a continuous supply — radish's whole growing period is barely longer than the
succession interval, so without regular re-sowing you'll get one small harvest and then nothing for
weeks. It's one of the easiest crops to succession because each sowing is quick to assess and quick
to replace if it fails.

## Common Problems

**All leaf, no root** — caused by overcrowding from failing to thin, or too much nitrogen in the
soil pushing leafy growth over root formation. Fix by thinning harder and avoiding heavily manured
beds for this crop.

**Bolting before the root swells** — caused by heat stress, water stress, or sowing too late into
lengthening spring days. Fix by keeping the crop well watered and sowing within the cooler parts of
the window if bolting has been a recurring problem.

**Hot, woody, or hollow roots** — caused by leaving radish in the ground past maturity, or by
drought stress during growth. Fix by harvesting promptly once roots size up and watering
consistently through the short growing period.

## In the Bed

Radish belongs to the brassica family for rotation purposes, sharing pests and diseases with
cabbage, broccoli, and turnip — don't follow it directly with those, though its short time in the
ground makes it easy to slot around a rotation rather than disrupt one. Because it occupies a bed
for only 25-35 days, it's a useful gap-filler between slower crops, or as a marker sown along the
row of a slower-germinating crop like parsnip to show where the row is before the parsnip comes up.
