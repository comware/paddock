# Beetroot

## Overview

Beetroot is one of the more forgiving roots — it tolerates a wider range of soil conditions than
carrot or parsnip and doesn't demand perfectly stone-free ground, because its root swells rather
than tapering deep. The thing that trips people up isn't growing it, it's what actually comes up:
what looks like one seed is a dried fruit containing two to four true seeds, so every sowing point
produces a small cluster of seedlings that needs thinning.

## When to Sow

Beetroot germinates from about 10°C and grows on comfortably up to 30°C, which gives it one of the
wider sowing windows of the root crops. In temperate southern Australia that's roughly September
through to March, though it will also tolerate a mild autumn or even a protected winter sowing in
warmer coastal districts. Sow into cold soil below 10°C and germination is slow and uneven; sow into
the peak of summer heat and roots can become woody and bitter if watering lapses, since heat stress
pushes the plant toward bolting.

## Sowing

Direct sow. Like other true roots, beetroot's swollen root is the storage organ formed from the base
of the seedling, and disturbing that early growth by transplanting distorts its shape — you'll get
elongated, forked, or lopsided roots instead of the round or cylindrical shape the variety should
produce. Beetroot is more tolerant of a check to growth than carrot, so transplants are sometimes
used to fill gaps, but for the bulk of a crop, direct sowing gives the more even result.

Sow at 20 mm depth — deeper than most root seed, because the seed cluster is larger and has more
reserves to push up through. Soil doesn't need to be as stone-free as for carrot; beetroot swells
mostly above and at the soil line rather than boring straight down, so it's less prone to forking
around obstructions.

## Growing On

Because each seed cluster produces two to four seedlings, thinning isn't optional — it's built into
how the seed works. Left unthinned, you get a tight clump of thin, crowded roots that never bulk up
properly, which is the single most common reason for a disappointing beetroot harvest. Thin to one
seedling per 10 cm once plants have their first true leaves; don't pull the thinnings, snip them at
soil level so you don't disturb the roots you're keeping. The thinnings themselves are edible as
baby greens.

Keep the bed evenly moist. Beetroot copes with dry spells better than carrot, but inconsistent
watering — a dry stretch followed by heavy watering — causes roots to split, and stress from drought
or extreme heat can push plants to bolt (run to seed) before they've bulked up, especially if a cold
snap follows a hot one.

## Harvest

You can judge beetroot without pulling the whole crop because the top of the root sits at or just
above soil level — brush back a little soil and look. Most varieties are ready at golf-ball to
tennis-ball size, depending on preference; smaller roots are more tender, larger ones store better.
Beetroot doesn't have a hard cutoff like a bean pod going stringy, so you can start harvesting small
roots early and let others size up, effectively thinning your way through the row as you eat.

## Succession

Sow every 21 days through the season for a steady supply rather than one large harvest that all
matures together. Beetroot stores well once pulled — roots keep for weeks in a cool, dark place with
the tops removed — so successions don't need to be tight, but without them you'll get a glut of
tennis-ball-sized roots all at once and nothing after.

## Common Problems

**Crowded, undersized roots** — caused by not thinning the seed clusters. Fix by thinning harder and
earlier; this is by far the most common cause of a poor beetroot crop.

**Split roots** — caused by irregular watering, usually a dry spell followed by heavy rain or a deep
water. Fix by watering more consistently, and mulch to even out soil moisture between waterings.

**Bolting (running to seed early)** — caused by heat or cold stress, particularly a cold snap in
young plants or a hot dry stretch later. Fix by sowing within the recommended window and avoiding
transplant shock in seedlings raised early; not much can be done once a plant has committed to
bolting except pulling it for the greens.

## In the Bed

Beetroot belongs to the same family as chard and spinach (the amaranth/chenopod family) for rotation
purposes — avoid following it with those, and space rotations by a season or two to avoid shared
soil-borne diseases. It's a relatively short-occupancy crop at 55-70 days, so it fits well as a
gap-filler between longer crops or ahead of a hungrier follow-on like brassicas, which will benefit
from ground that beetroot leaves in reasonable structure.
