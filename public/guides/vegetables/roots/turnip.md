## Overview

Turnip sits between radish and swede in speed and size — quicker than swede, larger and slightly
slower than radish, and like both of them it's a brassica root rather than a true taproot family
member, which matters mostly for rotation planning. It's an easy crop provided you don't let it sit
too long, since turnip's flavour and texture deteriorate with age in the ground faster than most
roots.

## When to Sow

Turnip germinates across a wide range, 8°C to 30°C, and grows best in the cooler part of that span —
it's a classic cool-season crop that can also be pushed into a warm-season sowing if kept watered.
In temperate southern Australia that gives two natural windows: late winter into spring, and again
in early to mid autumn, roughly August-October and February-April, avoiding the hottest weeks of
summer when roots tend to turn woody and bitter fast.

Sowing into summer heat without reliable water produces small, pungent, fibrous roots as the plant
races to bolt rather than swell. Sowing too late into autumn risks the crop being caught by a hard
frost before it's sized up, though turnip tolerates a light frost better than most roots and can
even sweeten slightly after one.

## Sowing

Direct sow. Turnip forms its swollen root from the hypocotyl just below the seedling's first leaves,
and like other roots this development is disrupted by the disturbance of transplanting — you'll get
forked, elongated, or stunted roots instead of the round, even shape the variety should produce. It
germinates fast and reliably enough that there's no practical reason to raise it any other way.

Sow at 10 mm depth into a bed that's been worked to a fine surface — turnip seed is small and needs
good soil contact to germinate evenly, though the crop is not as fussy about deep, stone-free soil
as carrot since its root swells mostly at and above the surface rather than tapering down.

## Growing On

Thin to 10 cm once seedlings have their first true leaves. An unthinned row produces a mass of
small, crowded roots competing for space, and because turnip's growing period is short, there's
little time to correct a late or missed thinning before the crop is due. Keep the bed consistently
moist — turnip is quick to bolt or turn woody under drought stress, more so than beetroot and less
forgiving than radish's very short exposure to the same risk.

Turnip greens are edible and some varieties are grown specifically for the tops rather than the root
— if growing for greens, sow more thickly and harvest young leaves without waiting for the root to
size up.

## Harvest

Judge readiness at the shoulder, which sits at soil level — most turnip varieties are best pulled at
5-8 cm across, smaller for salad types eaten raw, larger for storage types meant for cooking. Turnip
does not reward patience the way carrot or parsnip can; left too long the root turns woody, pithy,
and stronger-flavoured, so it pays to pull a test root as the maturity window opens rather than
waiting for the whole bed to look ready at once.

## Succession

Sow every 21 days through each of the two seasonal windows to keep a supply of young, tender roots
rather than a single batch that all turns woody together. Turnip's relatively short time in the
ground and quick decline in quality once mature make regular small sowings more useful here than for
a crop that stores well in situ.

## Common Problems

**Woody, pungent roots** — caused by leaving the crop in the ground too long, or by heat and drought
stress during growth. Fix by harvesting promptly once roots reach usable size and keeping the bed
consistently watered.

**Crowded, undersized roots** — caused by skipping or delaying thinning. Fix by thinning to final
spacing as soon as true leaves appear; turnip's short season doesn't give you much room to fix this
later.

**Flea beetle damage** — small shot-holes across the leaves, caused by flea beetles which favour
brassica seedlings, especially in warm, dry weather. Fix with fine insect mesh over young plants
until they're established enough to outgrow the damage; mature turnip tolerates some leaf damage
without it affecting the root.

## In the Bed

Turnip belongs to the brassica family for rotation purposes, alongside cabbage, broccoli, and radish
— avoid following it with those crops and space brassica plantings by at least a season to limit
shared pests and clubroot risk. It occupies the bed for only 40-55 days, making it a useful
shoulder-season crop ahead of or behind something longer, and a reasonable choice to follow a legume
crop that has left the bed with a nitrogen boost turnip's leafy top growth can use.
