## Overview

Swede is a larger, slower, hardier relative of turnip, bred for storage and winter eating rather
than speed. It wants more room and more time than turnip, and it tolerates cold considerably better
— a light frost improves its flavour rather than damaging it, which makes it one of the better crops
for a genuinely cold Central Victoria winter.

## When to Sow

Swede germinates from about 8°C but grows best in the cooler half of its range, up to around 25°C —
it's a cool-season crop that doesn't cope well with sustained heat, unlike some of the faster
brassica roots. In temperate southern Australia the main window is late summer to early autumn,
roughly February to April, timed so the crop's 80-100 day maturity lands it in the cooler months and
lets it sit through winter frosts before harvest.

Sow too early into late-summer heat and the crop can bolt or struggle before cooler weather arrives;
sow too late into autumn and there may not be enough growing season left before winter slows growth
to a crawl. Swede handles a slow finish better than most roots — a frost-touched swede left in the
ground is often preferred to one rushed to harvest.

## Sowing

Direct sow. As with turnip, its close relative, swede's storage root develops from the seedling's
base, and transplanting disturbs that development enough to produce forked or misshapen roots rather
than the smooth, round shape wanted for storage. Direct sowing also suits swede's slower pace
better, since there's no advantage to be gained from a head start under cover the way there might be
for a heat-loving crop.

Sow at 15 mm depth. Swede appreciates deeply worked soil more than turnip does, since the mature
root is considerably larger — aim for loose ground to at least 20 cm to avoid the stunting and
forking that comes from a root meeting resistance partway through bulking up.

## Growing On

Thin to 20 cm, more generous spacing than turnip because the mature root is much bigger. An
unthinned row of swede wastes even more of the growing season than an unthinned turnip row, since
the crop is in the ground for months rather than weeks — crowded plants simply never reach a useful
size. Keep the bed moist through establishment; once the crop is well grown its extensive root
system copes with dry spells better than most roots, but early stress can still trigger bolting or
check growth permanently.

Swede is a magnet for the same brassica pests as cabbage and turnip — cabbage white caterpillars and
flea beetle in particular — so watch for leaf damage through the growing season, though established
plants tolerate more leaf loss than seedlings without it affecting the root.

## Harvest

Judge readiness by shoulder diameter at soil level, brushing soil back to check rather than pulling
the whole row — most swedes are ready from 10-12 cm across, though they can be left to grow larger
without losing quality the way turnip does. Swede's real advantage is that it holds well in the
ground through winter, improving with frost, so there's no rush to harvest the moment it reaches
minimum size; many gardeners leave the crop in situ and pull roots as needed through the cold
months.

## Succession

Sow every 30 days if a spread of harvest sizes is wanted, though because swede stores so well both
in the ground and after lifting, most gardeners get more value from one well-timed sowing than from
staggering several — the crop is naturally suited to a single autumn sowing that then supplies the
household through winter.

## Common Problems

**Small, poorly formed roots** — caused by overcrowding from inadequate thinning, or by heat stress
if sown too early. Fix by thinning promptly to full spacing and keeping to the recommended sowing
window.

**Woody texture** — caused by drought stress during bulking, or by leaving an overcrowded crop to
struggle for too long before thinning. Fix with consistent watering through the growing season;
unlike turnip, a well-grown swede does not turn woody just from age in the ground.

**Caterpillar and flea beetle damage** — chewed or shot-holed leaves, caused by the usual brassica
pests. Fix with insect mesh while plants are young; established swede tolerates moderate leaf damage
without a meaningful effect on the root.

## In the Bed

Swede belongs to the brassica family for rotation purposes, alongside cabbage, turnip, and radish —
avoid following those with swede and space brassica plantings across the rotation to limit clubroot
and shared pest buildup. Its long occupancy of 80-100 days, often extended by leaving roots in the
ground through winter, means it effectively holds a bed from late summer through to spring, after
which it's well suited to precede a hungry spring crop like a fruiting vegetable that benefits from
the deep cultivation swede's bed received.
