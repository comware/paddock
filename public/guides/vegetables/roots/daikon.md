# Daikon

|-----------|-------|
| **Difficulty** | Moderate |

| **Sowing Method** | Direct |
| **Plant Spacing** | 15 cm |

## Overview

Daikon is a large Asian radish, and it behaves like a radish that's been given room and time to grow
much bigger — the same speed and vigour, but a root that can reach 30-40 cm long and needs deep,
unobstructed soil to do it. It sits between radish and parsnip in difficulty: quicker and more
forgiving than parsnip, but demanding more soil preparation than a small salad radish.

## When to Sow

Daikon germinates from about 10°C and grows on well up to 25°C, narrower than small radish's range
but still giving a reasonable window. In temperate southern Australia the two natural sowing times
are early autumn and early spring, roughly February-March and September-October, avoiding both the
depths of winter and the peak of summer, both of which push the crop toward bolting or stress rather
than steady root growth.

Sown at the wrong time of year — into lengthening, warming spring days too late, or into shortening
autumn days too early relative to the variety's day-length response — daikon is prone to bolting
before the root has sized up, similar to smaller radish but with more time and soil invested by the
point you find out.

## Sowing

Direct sow. Like all radish types, daikon's root swells rapidly from germination and cannot tolerate
the disturbance of transplanting without forking, splitting, or bolting in response to the check in
growth. Because daikon roots grow so long, this matters even more than for a small salad radish — a
root deflected early in a 35 cm growing run ends up badly distorted by the time it's mature.

Prepare the bed deeply, to at least 30-40 cm of loose, stone-free soil, since daikon's long taproot
will fork around any obstruction the way carrot and parsnip do. Sow at 15 mm depth and keep the row
moist until germination, which is usually quick at 4-7 days given adequate warmth.

## Growing On

Thin to 15 cm as soon as seedlings show true leaves — daikon needs considerably more room than a
small radish to develop its full length and girth, and an unthinned row produces the same crowded,
undersized result as any other unthinned root crop, just on a larger and more wasteful scale given
how much soil each plant needs. Keep watering consistent through the growing period; daikon's size
means it draws more water than small radish, and drought stress produces pithy, hot-flavoured, or
split roots.

## Harvest

Because daikon grows mostly below the surface with just the crown showing, judge readiness by the
diameter of the crown at soil level and by time from sowing — most varieties are ready between 60
and 70 days, reaching several centimetres across the shoulder. Unlike small radish, daikon holds
reasonably well in the ground for a couple of weeks past minimum maturity without turning pithy,
giving a little more flexibility about exactly when to pull it, though it will eventually go the
same way as any radish left too long.

## Succession

Sow every 21 days within each of the two seasonal windows for a rolling supply, since a single
sowing of a fast-maturing crop like this produces a glut rather than a steady one. Given how much
bed space and soil preparation each planting needs, succession sowings of daikon are worth planning
deliberately rather than squeezing in as an afterthought.

## Common Problems

**Forked or split roots** — caused by stony or compacted soil deflecting the long taproot, or by
irregular watering causing splitting as the root swells unevenly. Fix by digging the bed deeper
before sowing and watering on a consistent schedule.

**Bolting without forming a usable root** — caused by sowing outside the recommended window,
particularly into lengthening spring days or unseasonal heat. Fix by keeping to the two seasonal
sowing windows and watering through any warm spells.

**Pithy or hollow centres** — caused by leaving the root in the ground too long past maturity, or by
drought stress during growth. Fix by harvesting within a couple of weeks of maturity and maintaining
even soil moisture.

## In the Bed

Daikon belongs to the brassica family for rotation purposes, sharing pests and diseases with turnip,
radish, cabbage, and other brassicas — avoid following those crops with daikon and space brassica
plantings through the rotation. Its relatively short occupancy of 60-70 days and the deep
cultivation its bed requires make it a reasonable crop to grow ahead of a deep-rooted follow-on,
since the loosened soil daikon needs benefits whatever comes next.
